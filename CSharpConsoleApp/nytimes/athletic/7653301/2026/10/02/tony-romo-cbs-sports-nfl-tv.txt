ARTICLE

Tony Romo’s CBS Sports exit ends backward spiral in a job he began with so much promise
Romo is not gone from CBS only because of his July OWI in Wisconsin. It's a bad and sad end to a job that had gotten off to a good start.
By Andrew Marchand
Published 2026-10-02 18:55 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/7653301/2026/10/02/tony-romo-cbs-sports-nfl-tv/

The shame of how Tony Romo’s time as CBS’ No. 1 game analyst has ended is in how it began.

In 2017, Romo, fresh off the field, shot out of the gate like a gunslinger in the booth. He predicted plays, spoke with such joy and had an unteachable TV naturalness.

It was like watching the game with your best friend, if your best friend happened to be the former Dallas Cowboys star quarterback with a tinge of “Oz the Mentalist.”

By the winter of 2019, Romo gave the greatest sports TV analysis I’d ever heard, making the Tom Brady-Patrick Mahomes AFC Championship Game classic feel even bigger by telling the audience every play before it happened, as if he were reading the offensive coordinators’ call sheet.

He was the best in the business.

And then he went backward. He and his partner, Jim Nantz, were supposed to be the next Pat Summerall and John Madden.

Instead, by their third Super Bowl in 2024, they messed up the call at the end of another Mahomes classic in the Kansas City Chiefs’ overtime Super Bowl win over the San Francisco 49ers. Romo just prattled after Mahomes’ decisive play.

From that moment on — and probably a little before, as CBS Sports executives flew down to visit Romo in Dallas for discussions — it seemed clear CBS wanted out of the Romo business, if it could find a way.

Romo is not gone from CBS only because of his July OWI in Milwaukee. No, the settlement on the final four seasons and $72 million of his 10-year, $180 million contract came because CBS wanted to break free of Romo’s unpredictability, lack of preparation and unreliability.

The arrest was not the reason — it was the excuse.

It allowed CBS Sports president David Berson to audible to future Hall of Famer J.J. Watt as Nantz’s new partner.

If Romo were still the “next Madden,” it would have been a different conversation since July, and he probably would be on your TV this Sunday. Everyone would have said Romo made a mistake in Wisconsin and focused on the fact that it was only a misdemeanor there.

Instead, CBS preferred to eat a good portion of the remaining money that added up to $72 million. The exact figure Romo walked away with is unknown, but the fact that litigation was avoided suggests he probably got a large share. It also means Romo will have worked six years for what approaches $180 million. It might be the worst contract in sports TV history.

In many ways, Romo’s CBS run mirrors his Cowboys career, which never reached its full potential, with only two playoff wins to his name. Sources have repeatedly said that, like when he was a quarterback, Romo didn’t work hard enough as a broadcaster. The further he got away from playing, the less he knew. He didn’t study and failed to learn new players and schemes.

There is a difference, though, between playing and broadcasting.

At 46, Romo could still march into the booth or a studio again. He has said he developed an “overreliance” on alcohol due to his pain from two back surgeries.

If he can overcome this problem and rewrite his history, maybe this is not it for Romo as a broadcaster. America loves a comeback story.
