Small left-wing parties are rushing to choose their leaders ahead of the Nov. 29 election and to capitalize on protests across Spain. Podemos, a far-left party, has proposed holding joint primary elections with other smaller formations this month. Sumar, the junior party in the current governing coalition, has announced that it and other parties would relaunch as an alliance called Frente Amplio. The left’s inability to unite under a single platform could complicate Sánchez’ gambit of trying to secure a parliamentary majority through new elections.

Voters from both the left and the right have welcomed the news of a snap election.

Gonzalo Matías, a 28-year-old civil servant from Madrid, says he hopes the election will allow the left to win the majority in Parliament that it currently lacks. Matías calls Sánchez “the best asset the left has” and praises his focus on human rights and global social justice.

Chavela Quesada, an 82-year-old pensioner, says she believes the election is “the best news Spain could have received, because the country is sinking.” She calls the current government “incompetent,” adding: “Everything they have done has been wrong: immigration, the invasion of Ceuta, everything is horrible.”

Spain’s government has faced months of spiraling crises.

Hundreds of activists have been sleeping in tents in Madrid’s Puerta del Sol in recent days to protest the housing crisis. On Monday, a court in the capital denied a request by the regional government to clear the encampment. Organizers said they would remain in the square despite the call for early elections and stay until a national strike is held.

Analysts say Sánchez’ decision to call a snap election, after a housing bill defeat, reflects an opportunity to mobilize sluggish left-wing voters around social issues.

Pablo Simón, a political science professor at Universidad Carlos III, says Sánchez will likely campaign on the risks that a right-wing coalition could pose to social rights, including housing.

“That could mobilize some left-wing voters and narrow the gap between the right and left-wing blocs,” Simón says, but “it still makes a renewed left-wing government unlikely.”

Isabel Díaz Ayuso, the right-wing regional leader of Madrid and one of Sánchez’ most prominent political antagonists, raised doubts on Monday about the integrity of the coming election.

“I’m asking everyone not to even think about voting by mail,” Ayuso said on Monday, according to the Spanish news agency EFE. The government says the postal vote system is safe and secure.

Opinion polls in Spain have consistently shown broader support for right-wing parties in recent months. According to current projections, Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’ left-wing bloc is falling well short of the numbers needed for a parliamentary majority.

But analysts are wary of counting Sánchez out. After nearly a decade of leading the country, he has built a reputation for political comebacks. In 2023, with polls showing the conservative Popular Party ahead, many expected the right to remove Sánchez from office. He ultimately secured another term by winning the support of Junts, a right-wing Catalan separatist party.

Spain’s failure to pass housing legislation last week was a defeat for the government, but it also handed Sánchez an opportunity to rally his base.

In his speech announcing the early elections, he said voters would have to choose between keeping Spain as “a bastion of social justice” or a “far-right wave that brings regression wherever it governs.”

Sánchez said it was necessary that “to secure a much larger progressive majority in Parliament — one capable of taking on the elites, achieving new social advances and continuing to transform Spain.”

Despite the political turmoil, Spain is currently one of Europe’s economic bright spots. The country’s economy is expected to grow 2.1 percent this year, which is three times the rate forecast for the wider eurozone, according to Goldman Sachs. While Sánchez has taken a tough approach to migrants illegally entering Spain from North Africa, his government’s relative openness to immigration from places like Latin America has supported economic growth. Spain’s unemployment rate is just below 10 percent, the lowest it’s been since 2008.

The immediate backdrop to Sánchez’ decision was the recent eviction of María del Carmen Abascal, an 87-year-old pensioner. Though she was eventually allowed to return, the sight of police officers forcing open her front door last month touched a nerve in a country increasingly frustrated with high housing costs. The eviction set off protests and put intense pressure on the government, which had promised to make housing a priority, leading it to try to enact new tenant protections last week. The measures were voted down because the government lacked a majority in Parliament — underlining its inability to pass key legislation and setting the stage for early elections.

Sanchez’ decision to call an early election has added to the shakiness that has been troubling European assets in financial markets recently. On Monday, the euro dropped to about $1.12, its lowest level since May 2025 against the U.S. dollar.

The currency has been under pressure for weeks, falling more than 3 percent in the past month as investors have become increasingly wary of the political and fiscal challenges in Europe.

While much of the attention has recently been on France, where the government is struggling to control debt levels, Spain’s move to call an early election shows that the turmoil is broadening throughout the region.

Sánchez and other left-wing politicians are seeking to frame the early election around Spain’s housing crisis, after thousands of people protested in recent days against soaring rents and home prices. But the conservative Popular Party and the far-right Vox party are expected to focus instead on the crisis in Ceuta, the Spanish territory in North Africa where thousands of migrants remain after surging in last summer, and on Sánchez himself, a deeply unpopular figure among right-wing voters.

Santiago Abascal, the leader of the far-right Vox party, said that his party was “ready” for the election and criticized the government for its handling of the crisis in Ceuta, a Spanish territory in North Africa where tens of thousands of migrants entered in late July from neighboring Morocco.

“We’re going into an election with Ceuta invaded and with a candidate who is aligned with Morocco’s interests,” Abascal said in a TV interview on Monday, echoing a longstanding conspiracy theory on the Spanish right that Sánchez is beholden to Morocco.

Recent polls have shown a rise in support for Vox. The party has placed third with around 18 percent in recent polls, compared to its score of 12.4 percent in the previous election, in 2023.

The recent surge of tens of thousands of migrants into Ceuta, a Spanish territory in North Africa, compounded the recent pressure on Sánchez. At least 141 people died during the crossings. While most of the migrants returned quickly to Morocco, thousands stayed — creating a lingering logistical and social challenge for Sánchez’ government.

Critics said Sánchez’ migrant-friendly policies had encouraged migrants to try their luck. He allowed more than one million undocumented immigrants to apply for legal status earlier this year, to bring them into the formal labor force.

Mónica García, the health minister in Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’ government, said that the early elections would be an opportunity to “defend the right to housing against speculators,” seeking to frame the vote around the housing crisis.“The Spanish people will rise to the occasion,” she wrote on social media. García is one of five ministers from a group of left-wing parties governing alongside Sánchez’ Socialist Party. She is among the top candidates who could lead a new left-wing party, Frente Amplio, that would likely partner with the Socialists in a future coalition.

Sánchez’ inner circle has faced growing corruption charges and accusations. Those include:

• José Luis Ábalos, a former top official in Sánchez’ party, who was sentenced in June to 24 years in prison for corruption.

• Sánchez’ brother, David, who was banned from public office after being convicted of receiving a patronage job.

• Begoña Gómez, Sánchez’ wife, who is being investigated for corruption and influence peddling.

• José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero, a former Socialist prime minister, who is being investigated for receiving kickbacks.

• A police raid on the Socialist Party headquarters, as officers searched for evidence of a mudslinging campaign against judges investigating Sánchez’ family.

They have all denied the accusations.

Alberto Núñez Feijóo, the leader of Spain’s main conservative opposition party, welcomed the decision to call an early election. “Finally, Spain will speak its mind; finally, the Spanish people will decide,” Feijóo tells a news briefing.

Feijóo says the election would offer a choice between “four more years” of the same left-wing government and an “opportunity for change.”

“Spaniards’ difficulties have piled up over the years,” Feijóo says. “The state has had more resources at its disposal than ever before, and yet housing is now out of reach, there are too few doctors and too many strikes, educational standards are declining, and immigration is out of control.”
