Battle for the House

As the race in Ohio tightens, Republicans are resuming their tepid support of Max Miller.

After Representative Max Miller, the Republican from Ohio accused of physically abusing his ex-wife and young daughter, rebuffed calls from his own party to drop out of his re-election campaign, aides close to President Trump stepped in.

They advised Republicans who had demanded Mr. Miller’s resignation to quiet down and let him try to win, according to two people familiar with the conversations who were granted anonymity to speak candidly.

They also spoke and texted directly with Mr. Miller, urging him to stop talking about his family drama in public.

Their warnings have largely been heeded, forcing many Republicans to walk an awkward line between helping Mr. Miller win and staying away from his embattled campaign. The pivot from urging Mr. Miller to withdraw his candidacy reflects the importance of his seat to the Republican Party’s effort to retain control of Congress. At a time when both parties are contending with the possibility that Mr. Trump’s unpopularity, along with rising costs, will sweep Democrats to power, the G.O.P. can ill afford to lose what should have been an easy win in a conservative part of the country.

Mr. Miller is fighting to win a third term in a Cleveland-area district that Mr. Trump won in 2024 by double digits. It was considered safely Republican until this summer, when Mr. Miller’s ugly custody battle with his ex-wife — the daughter of Senator Bernie Moreno, another Republican from Ohio — spilled into the public square, with each portraying the other as violent, unstable and dishonest.

The dueling allegations detailed in court documents, along with wavering Republican support for Mr. Miller, have proved to be a windfall for Mr. Miller’s Democratic opponent, Brian Poindexter, a union ironworker. In the last quarter, Mr. Poindexter’s campaign raised more than $2.4 million, according to an official familiar with the matter, granted anonymity to discuss numbers not yet made public.

As the race has tightened, political committees aligned with Republican House leaders have spent more than $5 million on the race, according to the ad-tracking service AdImpact.

Mr. Trump has told his aides he feels loyalty toward Mr. Miller, who has been devoted to the president since his 2016 campaign, one of the people said. But over the weekend, when Mr. Trump traveled to Ohio for a rally designed to support down-ballot candidates in tight races, Mr. Miller was nowhere to be found and did not merit a mention from the president.

“You have to go out and vote Republican,” Mr. Trump told the crowd at the rally on Saturday. “That includes for these great people sitting right over here.”

Beside him were the state’s Republican candidates for Senate and governor — and two congressional hopefuls. Mr. Miller was invited but was unable to make it, a spokeswoman said, because “his district schedule had been set in stone for months and he didn’t want to cancel on any of his constituents.”

“It’s unfortunate that timing didn’t work out,” Abigail Angelos, the spokeswoman, said in a statement. “President Trump agreed that Rep. Miller’s time would be best spent campaigning in the district.”

Asked what Mr. Miller was doing during the rally, Ms. Angelos said he was spending time with his family.

In August, as the deadline for Mr. Miller to withdraw neared, Mr. Trump called him and urged him to consider his odds. He did not ask him to drop out or rescind his endorsement. Mr. Moreno and Senator Jon Husted, another Republican from Ohio facing his own tough re-election bid, both called Mr. Miller unfit for office and pressed him to resign.

Mr. Miller’s ex-wife, Emily Moreno, has accused him of throwing scalding water at her and fracturing their young daughter’s collarbone. Mr. Miller has denied the allegations and said his ex-wife and former father-in-law fabricated stories to sabotage his career.

With early voting underway in Ohio, Mr. Poindexter is seeking to remind voters in the conservative-leaning district what Republicans said about his opponent before Trump aides told them to fall in line.

Mr. Poindexter’s campaign is planning to publish a digital ad this week featuring audio of Mr. Moreno telling reporters that Mr. Miller once withheld his daughter’s favorite blue bunny toy during a custody exchange, the official said. A spokeswoman for the Ohio Democratic Party said it was planning to send out mail pieces this week featuring Republicans calling on Mr. Miller to resign.

Republicans have sought to defend the seat by depicting Mr. Poindexter as a criminal with a long rap sheet.

During his teenage years in Cleveland, he picked up a string of misdemeanor charges, including assault. A single felony charge came when he and some buddies “got in a scrap” at a local beach, he said.

Mr. Miller has acknowledged that the lack of support from the party’s leadership has hurt his chances.

“This was Republican-on-Republican violence that the Democrats are now using to bludgeon me in a general election,” he said on a conservative talk show in Ohio last week.

Battle for the Senate

Here are 5 takeaways from the first Maine Senate debate.

Senator Susan Collins, Republican of Maine, and Troy Jackson, the Democratic former president of the State Senate who is challenging her in the midterm elections, faced off on Tuesday night in their first of four debates, exchanging barbs over President Trump, Immigration and Customs Enforcement, abortion rights and health care.

It’s not often that an incumbent agrees to multiple debates against a challenger. But Ms. Collins appeared to be betting that Mr. Jackson, who is known as a bit of an uneven campaigner and has never run in a statewide general election, would stumble.

Instead, he held his own against a five-term senator in a duel that was, at times, quite testy.

Here are five takeaways from the debate in Maine, a crucial state in the battle for Senate control.

Collins was on the defensive over Trump.

At times, Ms. Collins seemed to have two opponents on the debate stage: Mr. Jackson and Mr. Trump.

Throughout the night, Ms. Collins tried to rebut Mr. Jackson’s argument that she had failed to stand up to the president even as she often expressed grave concern about his policies.

She criticized Mr. Trump’s tariffs, said his immigration enforcement action needed more oversight and, at one point, proudly boasted, “I am not exactly his favorite senator.”

Ms. Collins sidestepped a question about whether she would stand with Mr. Trump if he were to visit Maine, which the president suggested this week that he might do. “I don’t think that President Trump is going to come to Maine,” she said.

Mr. Trump lost Maine in the past three presidential elections.

And her vote for Kavanaugh was under the spotlight.

From the start, Mr. Jackson made clear that he planned to target Ms. Collins over her vote exactly eight years ago to confirm Justice Brett Kavanaugh, who voted to overturn Roe v. Wade in 2022.

“Would you trust her to pick the next Supreme Court justice?” he asked, in his opening statement.

Ms. Collins, one of just two Republican senators who support abortion rights, has repeatedly said she does not regret her vote to confirm the justice. But the senator stressed that she did not agree with Justice Kavanaugh on Roe and was instead more aligned with the liberal justices she also voted to confirm.

And she tried to flip the attack back on Mr. Jackson. “There’s only one of us on the stage who has always been pro-choice, and that’s me,” she said, a reference to Mr. Jackson’s opposition to abortion rights earlier in his political career.

Mr. Jackson responded, “I just think it’s incredible she won’t admit to being wrong.”

There were stark disagreements over ICE.

When immigration came up, Mr. Jackson went on the offensive, targeting an issue that has been a sensitive one for Ms. Collins since a Colombian man, Johan Sebastián Durán Guerrero, was fatally shot by an ICE agent in Maine in July.

“He’s not here now because Senator Collins would not push back against ICE,” Mr. Jackson, who supports abolishing the agency, argued in one of the debate’s more tense moments.

Ms. Collins called the shooting “horrific” and said it had demonstrated that ICE needed “serious reforms.” But the senator noted that she had helped secure funding for training for ICE agents and called for an impartial investigation.

“You cannot abolish ICE,” Ms. Collins said, asking rhetorically, “Do you really want to abolish an agency that is responsible for deporting violent criminals?”

Mr. Jackson replied, “You don’t put more money into an agency that is killing people across this country.”

They clashed over the value of Senate seniority.

Ms. Collins promoted her seniority in Washington as a major benefit for her home state that helped her bring federal funds and jobs to Maine.

Mr. Jackson, however, insisted that she had failed to leverage her power in a way that helped working people in Maine. Again and again, he sought to blame Ms. Collins for Trump administration policies that resulted in higher costs for housing, gas, groceries and other essential goods.

Ms. Collins tried to defend her record with statistics and explanations about the workings of the Senate and the limitations of her power.

She described herself as the “most outspoken opponent of the tariffs with Canada” and highlighted her success exempting road salt and cement from the duties.

Mr. Jackson framed those efforts as too little and too late.

“Mainers can’t afford to pay their bills with a press release or a strongly worded letter,” he said. “What about everything else? We can’t eat road salt.”

They are just getting started.

The night set off a dizzying nine days in which Ms. Collins and Mr. Jackson are scheduled to debate three more times.

The furious schedule may suggest that neither candidate believes that they have established a clear lead, that Mr. Jackson is hungry to introduce himself to Maine voters after entering the race in a rare summer substitution, and that the experienced Ms. Collins is equally eager to take him on.

The next debate is scheduled for Thursday.

Weeks before the midterms, Trump distances himself from his political advisers’ work overseas.

President Trump distanced himself on Wednesday from work that two of his top advisers, who are helping to guide the Trump political operation’s midterm strategy, did last week for a pro-Russian government in the Balkans.

Mr. Trump wrote on social media that the advisers, James Blair and Chris LaCivita, serve as “outside consultants” for his political operation. He added that he did “not have any idea who else they work for but will, on reflection, ask them and, if there’s a conflict, I will terminate them, immediately!”

His post, a remarkable effort at creating daylight between the president and two of his top political aides just weeks before the midterms, appeared to be in response to a recent report in The New York Times. The report revealed that Mr. Blair and Mr. LaCivita were each paid hundreds of thousands of dollars to provide counsel to the government of Republika Srpska on voting issues related to the elections held there on Sunday.

The Republika Srpska is a small self-governing territory that was enshrined by the U.S.-backed Dayton Accords peace agreement in 1995 as one of two political divisions in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

Mr. Blair and Mr. LaCivita were paid by a Washington-based law firm that was retained by Republika Srpska’s government to provide counsel on voting issues. The political consultants did not respond to questions about whether they had discussed their work in the Balkans with Mr. Trump or intended to cease outside consulting work.

The law firm and the government of Republika Srpska also did not respond to requests for comment.

The White House, when asked whether the president had discussed the matter with the consultants, referred a reporter back to his social media post. Shortly after the post, Mr. Trump responded noncommittally when asked by another reporter whether there was a conflict of interest.

“If I thought they had a conflict,” the president said, “I would terminate, but all they are is consultants.”

The engagement is the latest example of Trump advisers profiting from far-right politicians abroad who have sought favor from the U.S. president while bucking the international community.

But the work in Republika Srpska by Mr. Blair and Mr. LaCivita drew criticism from Republicans who suggested it showed a lack of commitment by members of Mr. Trump’s team to helping his party stave off landslide losses in the midterms.

A spokesman for MAGA Inc., the Trump-backed super PAC that Mr. Blair and Mr. LaCivita are steering as it spends heavily to support Republicans in the midterms, did not respond to a request for comment. The spokesman had previously told The Times that the consultants remained engaged in their work for the super PAC even as they traveled last week to Republika Srpska for the elections there on Sunday. They returned to the United States on Monday.

Mr. Blair, who was the political director of Mr. Trump’s 2024 campaign, left his role as a deputy chief of staff at the White House in the spring to run MAGA Inc. Mr. LaCivita, the co-manager of Mr. Trump’s 2024 campaign, is a senior adviser to MAGA Inc.

The nationalist pro-Russian party that controls the government of Republika Srpska had courted Mr. Trump and his allies long before Sunday’s elections.

The party won decisive victories on Sunday.

In a victory speech late Sunday, the leader of the party, Milorad Dodik, credited Mr. Trump for creating the political environment that allowed his party to triumph.

Mr. Dodik had been under U.S. sanctions for years for undermining the Dayton agreement. But the Trump administration lifted the sanctions last year after the Srpska government funded a costly influence campaign that enlisted a number of allies of Mr. Trump.

Mr. Blair and Mr. LaCivita were not involved in the campaign to lift the sanctions.
