Live Updates: Bridge Upgrade Scrambles Commute From New Jersey to Manhattan

NJ Transit riders will face detours and delays for the second time this year as workers complete a bridge replacement. Tuesday is the first regular workday of the five-week project.

Oct. 13, 2026, 8:01 a.m. ET

Stefanos Chen

Here’s the latest.

New Jersey commuters will have to navigate numerous bus, ferry and train disruptions to and from Manhattan for the next five weeks, beginning Tuesday, as workers replace an aging rail bridge that has caused problems for decades.

The 116-year-old Portal Bridge, which crosses the Hackensack River, is undergoing the final phase of construction before all train traffic can be transferred to its much taller, more modern replacement, the Portal North Bridge. Work began over Columbus Day weekend, but Tuesday morning’s commute is the first day that many riders will encounter service disruptions.

NJ Transit, the most frequent user of the bridge, is expected to cut the number of weekday train trips between Newark and Secaucus in New Jersey by roughly half, with trips to and from Pennsylvania Station in Manhattan being diverted through Hoboken. Service on Amtrak’s Acela, Northeast Regional and Keystone lines will also be reduced. Regular service is expected to return on Nov. 15.

NJ Transit has asked commuters to work from home if possible. For those who must travel, the Hoboken station offers alternate ways to reach Manhattan, including PATH trains, buses to the Port Authority Bus Terminal and NY Waterway ferries. Riders can use their NJ Transit tickets for a free transfer.

The Portal Bridge replacement project is intended to remove one of the worst bottlenecks along the Northeast Corridor, the busiest passenger rail route in the country. The Portal Bridge carries about 200,000 daily riders between Midtown Manhattan and New Jersey, but has repeatedly — and with increased frequency — been the site of aggravating track malfunctions and delays.

In crowded New Jersey train stations on Tuesday morning, many riders said they had left home earlier than usual to navigate the disruptions. But some added that they were willing to endure the upheaval if the long-overdue work got results.

“Five weeks is a chunk of time, but if the improvements are going to mean that we have a smoother commute in the long run, then I’m all for it,” said Jocelyn Cordova, 62, who lives in Maplewood and works in marketing in Manhattan. “We pay a lot of money for this service, and we are all tired of the delays.”

Here’s what else to know:

  • Alternatives: Here are some travel options for commuters affected by the disruptions.

  • Déjà vu: This is the second time this year that New Jersey commutes have been disrupted by bridge work. The first phase of this project started in February and also lasted several weeks.

  • Farewell and good riddance: The Portal Bridge, a swing bridge that opens to maritime traffic below, often gets stuck, requiring workers to slam tracks back into place with sledgehammers. This final phase of the “cutover” project will enable transit officials to retire the bridge for good.

Stefanos Chen

This is the second and final phase of the Portal Bridge replacement project.

New Jersey officials have long sought to replace the 116-year-old Portal Bridge, a swing bridge between Kearny and Secaucus that has been the cause of hourslong train delays and fires that have suspended service.

The bridge, which crosses the Hackensack River, is one of the worst choke points along the Northeast Corridor, the busiest passenger rail route in the country. It carries about 200,000 daily riders, most of them NJ Transit customers, between Midtown Manhattan and New Jersey.

The 961-foot swing bridge, which opens to maritime traffic, often gets stuck, requiring workers to slam it back into place with sledgehammers. It has been the site of at least two fires, and even when traffic is moving smoothly, its outdated design requires trains to slow down.

In 2021, the former governor of New Jersey Philip D. Murphy and NJ Transit approved a $1.5 billion contract to build the Portal North Bridge, a 50-foot-high, fixed-span, two-track replacement that is twice as tall as the original, allowing ships to pass underneath without disrupting train service. The construction is being done in collaboration with Amtrak, the national railroad, which owns the track and surrounding infrastructure.

The first phase of the “cutover” — moving wiring and equipment from the old bridge onto the new one — began in February. The work lasted a month and was completed on time, but exasperated passengers still grumbled about detours and train delays. New Jersey-bound riders have already begun to ride across the much taller bridge, and eastbound traffic into Manhattan will begin after this final stage of work.

This time, the cutover will take five weeks because the work is “significantly more complicated,” Kris Kolluri, the chief executive of NJ Transit, said. It involves connecting tracks to a new interlocking, a system of train signals and other equipment.

The project, NJ Transit’s most expensive construction contract ever, covers nearly 2.5 miles of the busy Northeast Corridor line and will involve moving Amtrak-owned wires, laying new track and building other infrastructure.

The new bridge could be fully operational by the end of the year.

The work is part of the broader Gateway Program, the portfolio of infrastructure projects connecting New York and New Jersey that has been the subject of a multibillion-dollar funding fight between President Trump and state officials. But the bridge work, which is already funded, is not expected to be affected by that dispute.

After the cutover is completed, the old bridge will be decommissioned and demolished early next year, Mr. Kolluri said at a news conference last month.

Amtrak said the last train to ever use the old bridge — one of its new NextGen Acela trains — crossed the tracks Friday night.

Here are some travel alternatives for New Jersey commuters.

NJ Transit riders are in for five weeks of scrambled commutes in and out of Manhattan, with roughly half the number of regular trains in service and several detours to consider.

The closing of the Portal Bridge between the Newark and Secaucus stations means that weekday trips to Manhattan’s Pennsylvania Station will instead be diverted to Hoboken. There, riders will need to switch to buses, the PATH train or ferries to complete their trips. Weekend trains will still travel to Manhattan.

Nearly all NJ Transit rail passengers, with the exception of Atlantic City Rail Line riders, will have to navigate some service disruptions. Some Amtrak lines will also have reduced service.

During the Portal Bridge work, the railroads will perform a number of other infrastructure repairs along the Northeast Corridor that will affect some routes.

Riders can sign up for travel updates on njtransit.com.

Transit officials are encouraging people to work from home if they can. Below is a list of service changes to expect, and how to avoid the worst of it.

  • Morristown, Gladstone Branch and Montclair-Boonton Lines: Weekday trips will terminate in Hoboken. To continue to Manhattan, NJ Transit ticket holders can transfer for free to the 126 bus to Port Authority; the PATH train to 33rd Street; or a NY Waterway ferry to West 39th Street. Ferry service frequency will increase during the morning and evening rush.

  • Raritan Valley Line: All weekday trips to Manhattan’s Penn Station will terminate instead at Newark Penn Station. And on Saturday, Nov. 7, buses will replace trains between Cranford and Newark stations, during track replacement work.

  • North Jersey Coast Line: Passengers riding between Manasquan and Bay Head will need to transfer to buses until Oct. 25 while maintenance work is performed on the Brielle Bridge.

  • Port Jervis Line: Buses will replace weekday train service between Suffern and Port Jervis stations until Nov. 14 because of track maintenance work.

  • Acela, Northeast Regional and Keystone: Expect reduced service on these Amtrak lines, especially Keystone, which will cut weekday trips between Philadelphia and New York City to 10, down from 24.

Stefanos Chen

Five weeks of pain begin for New Jersey commuters.

New Jersey commuters should take a deep breath this weekend, as they brace for five more weeks of service cuts and disruptions to replace the century-old Portal Bridge, a critical link to Manhattan.

The work involves the final phase of a “cutover,” the complex process of moving wiring and tons of new equipment to the bridge’s taller, more modern replacement. Construction began on Friday, but service changes are expected to start on Sunday.

“Grin and bear it,” is how Jim Lyons, a 58-year-old who works in construction, said he would handle the disruption. He commutes to New York City from Monmouth County Monday through Friday.

“I’ll probably take an earlier train, but other than that, going home, I don’t know,” Mr. Lyons said Friday night at Pennsylvania Station in Manhattan.

NJ Transit, the most frequent user of the bridge, is expected to cut the number of weekday train trips between Newark and Secaucus in New Jersey by roughly half, with trips to and from Penn Station being diverted through Hoboken. Service on Amtrak’s Acela, Northeast Regional and Keystone lines will also be reduced. Regular service is expected to return on Nov. 15.

Beginning Monday, riders of the Morris & Essex, Gladstone Branch and Montclair-Boonton lines will be diverted on weekdays to end at Hoboken instead of Penn Station. Riders can complete their trips to and from Manhattan with a free transfer to the 126 bus; a ferry ride with NY Waterway; or the PATH train at the 33rd Street stop. Weekend trains will still travel to Penn Station.

Some North Jersey Coast line riders will have to transfer to buses while rehabilitation is done on a movable bridge in Brielle, N.J. Bus service will also replace some sections of the Port Jervis and Raritan Valley lines for track maintenance and replacement work. NJ Transit has more details on its website.

Amtrak, the national railroad that owns the track infrastructure, will also conduct a number of unrelated repair projects along sections of the Northeast Corridor, which runs from Boston to Washington.

Most riders won’t notice the changes until Tuesday, because of the three-day Columbus Day weekend.

The disruptions are in service of finally freeing train riders from the vagaries of the 116-year-old bridge, an often dysfunctional span along the Northeast Corridor, the busiest passenger rail route in the country. It carries about 200,000 daily riders, most of them NJ Transit customers, between Midtown Manhattan and New Jersey.

The 961-foot swing bridge, which crosses the Hackensack River, opens to maritime traffic below and often gets stuck, causing a cascade of delays in the region. It has been the site of electrical fires, and even when traffic is moving smoothly, its outdated design requires trains to slow down.

Its replacement, the Portal North Bridge, a 50-foot-tall, three-arch span high enough to allow ships to pass freely, partially opened in March, after four weeks of construction and service disruption. Train passengers heading to New Jersey are already crossing the new structure, and this work will open the bridge for eastbound traffic.

This final phase of construction will take a week longer, NJ Transit officials said, because the work involves connecting tracks to a new interlocking, a system of train signals and other heavy equipment.

Kris Kolluri, the chief executive of NJ Transit, acknowledged the inconvenience to riders, but has said the disruptions are necessary to prevent delays along this busy corridor for decades to come.

To ease congestion, the agency has asked commuters to work from home when possible.

That’s not an option for Anjelica Soto, 21, an assistant director at an Off Broadway show in the city.

“It has to be in person,” she said of her theater job. “I try to have the mentality of, we’ll just make it work. So that’s how I’m getting through,” she said.

The increased demand for buses in Hoboken is affecting Arnold Sánchez Hernández, 40, who used to leave home in Hoboken at 9 a.m. to work as a kitchen assistant six days a week in Midtown Manhattan. Now he has to leave an hour earlier because buses are more crowded and make more stops.

“I have to get up a little earlier so my commute doesn’t affect my arrival time at work,” Sánchez said in Spanish while waiting for a bus on Sunday at NJ Transit’s Hoboken Terminal.

Sánchez’s co-workers who live in New Jersey also have to leave home earlier.

“Now they wake up at 5 a.m.; before, they would wake up at 7 or 7:30,” Sánchez said.
