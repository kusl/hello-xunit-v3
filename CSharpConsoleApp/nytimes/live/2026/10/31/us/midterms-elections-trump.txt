Here’s the latest.

As candidates on Saturday barnstormed to make their final case in a midterm election that will decide control of Congress, a feud among top Democrats in Michigan escalated and took center stage, throwing new doubt into a race that could determine which party controls the Senate.

Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic Senate nominee, said Saturday that his former primary rival, Representative Haley Stevens, should not come to a Detroit church service he was planning to attend Sunday. The event, which is also expected to include Representative James E. Clyburn of South Carolina, had been billed as a Democratic unity gathering.

“I don’t want any distractions,” Dr. El-Sayed told reporters after a campaign rally in Sterling Heights, a Macomb County suburb. “It seems as though other folks are more interested in making this race about things that it’s not about.”

The growing tensions between Dr. El-Sayed, a progressive, and Ms. Stevens, the more moderate representative he beat in the primary, have clouded whether the Democrats would appear at a party unity event planned for Sunday in Detroit. The fault line between them has been differing stances on U.S. support for Israel.

That disagreement has taken on an outsize importance at a time when strategists on both sides say that Democrats are within striking distance of winning a slim majority in the Senate. They must flip at least four seats and retain all of their own to regain control of the upper chamber, and Michigan is seen as the best opportunity for Republicans to pick up a seat.

Dr. El-Sayed and his Republican opponent, Mike Rogers, were campaigning four miles apart in Macomb County, Mich., on the final Saturday before Election Day, as both parties fanned across the country in a late attempt to drive turnout and persuade undecided voters.

On Friday, Ms. Stevens had demanded in an open letter that Dr. El-Sayed disavow a political ad from a left-wing political group suggesting U.S. support for Israel was a reason for high prices, which she said was antisemitic. That afternoon, Dr. El-Sayed fired back, accusing Ms. Stevens of “relitigating a primary that ended three months ago.”

Ms. Stevens has in recent weeks done a series of interviews criticizing Dr. El-Sayed for his policies toward Israel and his refusal to fully disavow the advertisement.

Asked on Saturday if he would condemn the ad, Dr. El-Sayed replied: “I’ve already been clear that that ad was not made by me, and I do not agree with the contents of that ad.”

Newsom issues a warning to California judges about election interference.

Gov. Gavin Newsom on Saturday warned the state’s top judges that the Trump administration may try to seize midterm election ballots as a legal battle continued over unproven federal claims that noncitizens are voting in huge numbers in California.

“I have grave concerns federal agents will seek search warrants to seize ballots and ballot materials before the election results are certified,” the governor wrote in an extraordinary “alert” to the four U.S. District Courts that cover California.

He added: “Indeed, the only reason to do so would be to interfere in a State’s lawful counting of ballots and certifying the results of the election.”

The warning came as the Trump administration is also claiming that there are more than 20,000 California voters who are “likely noncitizens" — an assertion that state election officials have firmly rejected.

On Saturday, data scientists analyzing that list of voters said in a court filing that it contains thousands of errors.

Mr. Newsom’s concerns about the seizure of voting machines or ballots are not unfounded.

In January, federal agents executed a search warrant at an election center in Fulton County, Ga., hauling away boxes of ballots in an effort to discredit Mr. Trump’s defeat in the 2020 election. The warrant in Fulton County was approved by a federal magistrate judge even though it was based on a series of debunked conspiracy theories about the contentious 2020 presidential race.

Ever since, election officials have worried that the Justice Department might try something similar in different jurisdictions, especially during the highly fraught days after the midterm elections.

Those concerns have not been assuaged, as Mr. Trump himself has warned that the country’s voting machines are vulnerable to foreign meddling. The president has also said that he regretted not seizing voting machines after the 2020 election.

Mr. Newsom is not the only public figure who has expressed concern that the Trump administration might resort to seizing ballots or voting machines.

On Tuesday, two former federal judges issued an unusual public statement to their working colleagues, warning them to use extreme caution before taking the “uniquely serious” step of granting prosecutors a warrant for seizures.

“Unlike the execution of a search warrant for contraband such as drugs or for the records of a criminal enterprise, executing a search warrant for ballots or voting machines before an election is certified would cause irreparable harm to a vital public process: our elections,” the former judges, J. Michael Luttig and Nancy Gertner, wrote.

Mr. Newsom’s letter to the judges was issued on the same day two data scientists from University of California, Los Angeles, released their findings of an analysis of the list of the 20,000 registered voters in California the Trump administration has claimed are not eligible to vote because they are not citizens.

The analysis — contained in court papers filed in district court in Washington by immigration advocates — found that more than 40 percent of the people on the list do not appear on the California state voter rolls. The analysis also found that the list contained thousands of people who appear to be citizens.

The scientists conducted their study by comparing the government’s list of suspected noncitizens with lists of people who had obtained citizenship. They did so with the help of the Coalition for Humane Immigrant Rights of Los Angeles, a major California nonprofit colloquially known as CHIRLA.

The administration sued California seeking to force state officials to set aside any ballots cast by the 20,000 voters. But a federal appeals court has twice shot down that effort, saying it would rob the state of its authority to administer the election and violate the “plain text” of the Constitution. In between that court’s rulings, a district court in Los Angeles also rejected the administration’s request.

In his letter, Mr. Newsom wrote that, while he was not aware of any such pending application, “the tactics and rhetoric federal officials are using strongly suggest that, having lost repeatedly in federal civil proceedings thus far, they will quickly pivot to the criminal forum.”

The letter included a nonpartisan guide for evaluating search warrant applications that the judges could distribute to lower level federal magistrates.

Women voters are the silent Democratic majority.

In the scramble to understand where America’s head is politically this election season, the focus has been on the working class, on Hispanics and on young men. But the country’s single biggest demographic group — women — is still the main driver of voting and in this election is looking more Democratic than ever.

Women have been the backbone of the Democratic base for decades and vote overwhelmingly for Democrats most years.

In 2022, support dipped slightly, when 48 percent of women chose Democrats and 41 percent supported Republicans. But this year, support looks set to return to more typical results, with 56 percent of women saying they prefer Democratic candidates, compared with 37 percent who say they prefer Republican ones, according to New York Times/Siena University surveys.

Women are a potent political force because they vote at higher rates than men.

And this year, they are bringing a lot of energy to volunteer and get-out-the-vote efforts.

Lynda Hoving, a retired appraiser and longtime volunteer for the Democratic Party in Kent County, Mich., has been sending postcards and working the phones for Democrats.

She watched as her West Michigan county, home to Gerald Ford and a tradition of Republicanism, went from solidly red in the 1970s, when she and her husband moved there, to reliably blue in more recent political cycles.

“I was always trying to turn something from red to purple,” said Ms. Hoving, who spent years in Grand Rapids stuffing envelopes, door-knocking and making spreadsheets. “It has been my goal for my whole political life.” She said it was disproportionately the women in the county who “kept the balls rolling” for the slow, tedious work of local politics.

Now 73, she said she still reminded everyone to vote during election season, including, most recently, her orthodontist, who told her he had voted for Trump but was not happy with the way things had turned out.

But she didn’t get to try to pitch him on Democrats.

“Then they put the plastic in my mouth, and I couldn’t talk anymore,” she said. “That’s one way to force a Democrat to listen.”

Pat Smithson, 69, of Lenexa, Kan., has volunteered so much for Adam Hamilton, the Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate in Kansas, that he called out her name when he walked into a campaign event on Saturday morning. She had brought cinnamon rolls freshly baked by her daughter.

Ms. Smithson, a retired fund-raiser, has been calling voters all over Kansas since June and said that even her daughter, a Republican who voted for Donald Trump in 2024, planned to vote for Mr. Hamilton, too. “She listened to him on TV,” Ms. Smithson said. “She watches me volunteer, setting the example.”

Some of the shift away from Republicans among women in recent years has been driven by Donald Trump.

Annette Hunt, 58, who works in finance and lives in Dallas, grew up in a staunchly Republican family in East Texas.

“Reagan was almost like a God in our house,” she said.

She said she voted straight ticket Republican for most of her life. Then came Mr. Trump.

She thought he would be “like Ross Perot — a businessman who shook up the Republican Party a bit,” but she couldn’t bring herself to vote for him, so she wrote in her father, who died that year. She has always regretted it, and ever since has voted Democratic to “stop the current agenda of the Republican Party.”

Last Saturday, she voted early for James Talarico, the Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate in Texas. She said she did not feel at home in the Democratic Party either, despite voting for their candidates for the past 10 years.

“I’m a person without a party,” she said.

Jeanne Russell, 77, who owns a farm on Verona Island, Maine, said she grew up in a Republican household but was shaped by the politics that defined her young adulthood and ended up a liberal.

“I was a child of the ’60s and ’70s,” she said. “I was aware of the injustices in the country. It bothered me. So I’ve always been a Democrat.”

She said she has never missed an election.

‘Poop water’ churns a local election in parched Arizona.

A ritzy Arizona enclave is facing water cuts, and the most divisive idea to offset them begins with millions of gallons of sewage. Call it affluent effluent.

The plan — which involves rigorously treating and purifying wastewater, then routing it straight to residential taps — has long been seen as critical for the future of Scottsdale, a city heavily reliant on the dwindling flow of the Colorado River.

But in recent months, Scottsdale’s wastewater recycling program has been sucked into a bitter political dispute, and its fate could hinge on the outcome of the city’s elections on Tuesday.

The Scottsdale City Council, where two seats are up for grabs, is nominally nonpartisan, but this year’s race may change the balance of power on water issues. Two candidates, both running on a right-wing platform, deride wastewater recycling as an unproven experiment. They call it “toilet to tap” or “poop water.”

A third challenger, who is backed by Democratic groups, argues that the science behind wastewater recycling is robust, citing expert support for a technology considered safe and reliable. His opponents, he says, are capitalizing on residents’ fears of contamination and downplaying the seriousness of Scottsdale’s looming water crisis.

“They’re using buzzwords and scare tactics,” Raoul Zubia, a retired banker, said of his conservative opponents, Michelle Ugenti-Rita, a former state senator, and Barry Graham, an incumbent council member.

The dispute offers a colorful preview of the ways water is becoming increasingly politicized as scarcity forces local officials across the West to make difficult decisions and embrace unconventional solutions.

Scottsdale, a city of 240,000 northeast of Phoenix, is famous for its green golf courses and red rock peaks. It is an upscale destination for bachelorette parties and bar crawls, spas and shopping malls. But in wonkier circles, the city is also known as a leader in water treatment and engineering.

Nearly all American drinking water comes from lakes, rivers, reservoirs or underground aquifers. But Scottsdale Water, a city-operated utility, has been purifying wastewater for decades through a process with many steps and even more syllables — ozonation, membrane ultrafiltration, reverse osmosis, ultraviolet photolysis. Scientists say the resulting product is among the cleanest water in the world.

The city currently sends much of the recycled water to about two dozen golf courses, which use it for irrigation. It pumps the remainder into an aquifer, where it is eventually drawn out as drinking water, a longstanding process known as indirect potable reuse.

City leaders wanted to go further, skipping the aquifer and mixing purified water with other sources before delivering it to residential taps. That’s known as direct potable reuse.

Only a few direct potable reuse facilities operate around the world, but more are in the works. Scottsdale’s would have been Arizona’s first.

But in June, with the state updating regulations and the issue turning partisan, the City Council froze funding for the project, even as neighboring Phoenix pushed forward.

Supporters were dismayed.

“You cannot choose ‘no water’ because you have unfounded fears about water quality,” said Kathryn Sorensen, a water policy expert at Arizona State University.

Ms. Ugenti-Rita would be the program’s most vocal critic if elected. The idea is “disgusting,” she said, and she is worried recycled water could make residents sick. She has also balked at the cost of infrastructure upgrades, which city staff recently estimated at more than $160 million.

“There’s a lot at stake,” Ms. Ugenti-Rita said. “We don’t want to be the next Flint, Mich. I don’t want short or long-term health effects. We have a reputation to consider.”

Mudslinging reached its peak ahead of the summer primary election, when Ms. Ugenti-Rita appeared in a social media video with the MAGA-aligned comedian Rob Schneider.

Mr. Schneider, who was in the “The Waterboy” and lives in Scottsdale, held a cup of liquid the color of a milky latte while criticizing “toilet to tap.” He referred to one of Ms. Ugenti-Rita’s primary opponents, Solange Whitehead, as “Sewage Solange.”

Ms. Whitehead, who grew up wanting to be a sewage engineer, was unfazed.

“I was pretty flattered,” Ms. Whitehead said as she sipped a pale ale made with Scottsdale’s recycled wastewater, part of a campaign to help the public overcome the “ick factor.”

Ms. Whitehead, an incumbent council member who ran on water issues, finished first in the July primary, securing another term on the board and avoiding a runoff on Tuesday. She said she was concerned that her opponents’ rhetoric will turn residents against a critical technology.

“When you rile up people against something, it spreads,” Ms. Whitehead said. “This is not a good time to politicize and to try to create distrust.”

At Scottsdale Water’s sprawling campus, Thyra Ryden-Diaz, the utility’s interim director, defended the wastewater recycling program and said sending treated water directly to taps would not differ much from what the city was already doing.

“All water is recycled,” Ms. Ryden-Diaz said. “I don’t think that people remember their third-grade water cycle graph.”

Still, she said, it would not be a panacea for Scottsdale because the city already reuses so much of its wastewater, and unless facilities are upgraded, there will not be much left to send to taps.

Scottsdale is not running out of water anytime soon, but surveys show Arizonans are concerned that state leaders aren’t doing enough to save their most precious resource. Max Vega, a 40-year-old who works in the mining industry, is one of them.

Drinking recycled wastewater “may disturb some people,” he said on a recent evening after walking his dog at a large Scottsdale desert preserve. “But if you do it the right way, under safe regulations, then so be it.”

For others, the idea is a harder sell. Corlan and Becky Fawcett, who were visiting Scottsdale for a conference, said knowing wastewater ran from their hotel faucet would not stop them from returning.

“But it might make me want to buy my water when I get here,” Mr. Fawcett said.

The couple, who live in Utah, another water-strapped state reliant on the Colorado River, acknowledged that such technology might someday come to their hometown.

“What’s the alternative?” Ms. Fawcett asked. “What’s the other option?”

Democrats file suit over closed polling places in rural Alaska.

As a Justice Department lawsuit challenging the presence of two U.S. Senate candidates named Dan Sullivan on the Alaska ballot plays out, Democrats have filed a lawsuit in Alaska accusing election officials of failing to properly operate at least 18 rural polling locations.

The complaint, filed Friday afternoon in Anchorage Superior Court, warns that several thousand voters across remote, predominantly Alaska Native communities face disenfranchisement in a state where elections are routinely decided by a few thousand or even a few hundred votes.

Given Alaska’s vast geography and the logistical hurdles voters in remote areas encounter, state law allows any qualified voter to request an absentee ballot and establishes an in-person absentee and early voting window beginning 15 days before Election Day.

In remote communities, the state relies on agreements with local municipal and tribal offices to host and staff sites where residents can cast absentee ballots in person.

The Democrats’ lawsuit alleges that state-advertised absentee-in-person voting sites across 18 rural communities either failed to open, opened late, maintained irregular hours or lacked ballots altogether. In some communities, local workers reportedly resigned without replacements, while others had not received ballots or were unaware they were supposed to host voting.

According to the complaint, some voters traveled to their designated voting location “by boat or by plane” only to be turned away.

State Senator Bill Wielechowski wrote the elections division earlier this month, noting the 18 precincts that hadn’t opened and asking for help.

In her response this week, the Alaska elections director, Carol Beecher, said 12 of the 18 sites had confirmed they were open, three had declined to open, and state officials were still trying to contact the remaining three. She emphasized the role local partners are supposed to play in rural voting, suggesting blame for the confusion about where and when to vote rested largely in the villages rather than the state elections office.

Some 82 percent of Alaska communities are not accessible by road, and the state elections office routinely struggles to ensure residents in farther-flung places can vote, with ballots sometimes arriving too late to be counted or polling sites never opening.

This time around, Democrats want the state elections office to make sure all remote polling sites are open and stay open until polls close at 8 p.m. on Election Day. They also want the state to pay their attorney fees.

Susan Collins has delivered millions of dollars to Maine, but people say it’s best to avoid making her angry.

Jordan Wood, a Democrat from Lewiston, Maine, was the first person to announce a 2026 campaign challenge to Senator Susan Collins, and he made a priority of winning the endorsement of the mayor of his hometown. He met with Mayor Carl Sheline, a fellow Democrat, in his office and over drinks at a local Italian restaurant. They had a series of phone calls. But ultimately Mr. Sheline declined to back him.

Ms. Collins, the longtime Republican incumbent, was not in the room, at the bar, or on the line.

But she had made her presence known.

Mr. Sheline had various reasons for withholding his support, he said in an interview. Among them, he acknowledged, were a housing project, a food distribution center, and a mill museum in Lewiston. Ms. Collins had helped with the finances for all of them.

“It’s just an environment of possible retribution,” Mr. Wood said. “It is that fear she instills.”

Over nearly 30 years, Ms. Collins, a self-styled moderate in a familiar Maine mold, has cultivated a public persona as a workhorse and a fastidious maestro of constituent services and pork-barrel disbursements, using her Washington know-how to get her constituents what they need.

But for just as long, her detractors say, she has managed to mostly mask another reality. “The gentlewoman from Maine,” as her colleagues say on the Senate floor, is a formidable power broker who can play a fierce brand of politics with a how-dare-you air. According to more than 40 interviews with Republicans and Democrats in Maine politics, former staff, colleagues and longtime observers, Ms. Collins has wielded power not so much as a Yankee Republican but as a shrewd machine politician.

“It’s almost like Tammany Hall,” said Amy Fried, a retired political scientist from the University of Maine. “You’re going to get this benefit here if you vote for me.”

Lance Dutson, a Republican strategist and former aide to Ms. Collins, did not disagree.

“That’s exactly what it is,” he said. “She’s openly discussing the fact that Maine is going to be set back if she’s not in that position,” he added. “It puts people in the position of very clearly understanding that voting against her is a vote against their own well-being.”

Ms. Collins’s staff stresses that she has worked to bring money back to all communities in Maine — regardless of whether they support her.

Annie Clark, a longtime aide to Ms. Collins, provided The New York Times an exhaustive list of projects, from Orono and Ogunquit to Belfast and Blue Hill, for which the senator had secured funding since 2021. The senator got $234 million for 96 projects in Cumberland and Knox counties — the only two counties she lost in 2020. In Portland, where she received just 21 percent of the vote: $51.2 million for 28 projects.

“Senator Collins has been extraordinarily effective,” said Blake Kernen, a spokeswoman for Ms. Collins. “She works tirelessly to deliver results for the state, meets with thousands of Mainers every year, and she secures billions of dollars for communities in all of Maine’s 16 counties whether they vote for her or not.”

Ms. Collins, 73, the chair of the Senate Appropriations Committee, is running for re-election this fall the way she always has — no clickbait TikTok posts or big, rah-rah rallies, but a relentless focus on stops in small towns where she has brought home federal dollars for boat landings, emergency operations centers and wastewater treatment facilities.

At her campaign events, supporters hold signs that underscore the point she is trying to make: “Thank You, Susan.”

In debates this month with her Democratic opponent, Troy Jackson, a former logger and Maine Senate president, Ms. Collins was unsubtle about the crux of the deal.

“In the past five years I’ve secured $1.5 billion for properties all over the state,” she said.

She was even more blunt in a radio interview this week, citing 672 projects that she had secured across the state, including 45 new fire stations.

“All that goes away,” she said, “if Troy were to be elected.”

‘I have a lot of power’

Her whole life has been a lesson in the accrual and use of power.

Ms. Collins was born and raised in Caribou in Aroostook County, the state’s most northern terrain. The Collinses didn’t just own and run the local lumber company and hardware store. They were politicos too.

“My parents,” Ms. Collins told The New York Times this past summer, “both served as mayor of Caribou. My father was in the State Senate, my grandfather, great-grandfather served in the state legislature.”

Her parents were supporters of William S. Cohen when he ran for Congress in 1972 and beyond, and Ms. Collins was a driver and an intern for Mr. Cohen when she was in college at St. Lawrence University in Canton, N.Y. After she graduated, she was hired as an aide and was on his staff for a dozen years, known by her colleagues and peers as a meticulous note-taker and over-preparer.

She ran for governor in 1994 when she was 41 and lost. She ran for Senate in 1996 and won. She said she would serve no more than two terms but then ran for re-election in 2002, 2008, 2014 and 2020.

Over the course of her three decades in the Senate, drafting off the renown of her cordial, consensus-building Maine forebears like Mr. Cohen and Senator George Mitchell, Ms. Collins has emphasized her image as the last of a vanishing breed. James Talarico, the Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate in Texas, invoked the mythological grandness of his native state when he told a crowd in El Paso this week, “Our musicians are so iconic, they only need one name: Willie. Beyoncé. Selena.”

A little more than a year after Mr. Talarico began his candidacy, he has become a marquee political persona, and not just in Texas. His race, which could become the most expensive in the history of nonpresidential campaigns, may well decide which party controls the Senate.

The Democratic candidate’s celebrity has thrown Republicans into a panic, prompting the world’s richest man, Elon Musk, to donate an unheard-of $158 million to groups that support Mr. Talarico’s G.O.P. opponent and the state attorney general, Ken Paxton.

A smooth orator and prolific fund-raiser, Mr. Talarico has cast his upstart campaign in biblical terms. He is David, the corrupt political system exemplified by Mr. Paxton is Goliath and in whatever city he happens to be in, he declares, “You are my slingshot.” He has run a largely mistake-free campaign, although the flu took him off the trail for nine days.

He has consistently drawn overflow crowds, many of whom wear T-shirts declaring themselves to be “Talafreakos,” first deployed by Mr. Paxton as a term of derision. Last Tuesday, an estimated 4,000 people turned out in Lubbock — an extraordinary number for a Democrat in the ruby-red city — while Mr. Paxton was campaigning with President Trump less than three miles down the road.

“I think James has at most a 50 percent chance of winning,” said Luke Warford, the chairman of the Travis County Democratic Party. “Which, look, in Texas, that’s phenomenal. If you told me a year ago a Democrat would have a coin-flip chance of winning, I’d be doing backflips.”

People close to Mr. Talarico’s campaign privately echoed Mr. Warford’s assessment of the race. A New York Times/Siena University poll in September and a CNN/SSRS poll conducted Oct. 20 to 27 showed Mr. Talarico ahead by 6 percentage points, but other recent public surveys show him either tied with Mr. Paxton or within the margin of error with a 2-point lead. During the homestretch of Mr. Talarico’s campaign, from deep-blue regions (San Antonio, Laredo, the Rio Grande Valley and El Paso) to steadfastly Republican terrain (Midland and Lubbock), Mr. Talarico’s bursting-to-capacity crowds made it possible to imagine that he could flip a statewide race for the first time in decades. Mr. Talarico’s barnstorming tour, “Clear Eyes, Full Hearts,” is named after part of the motto of the film and TV series “Friday Night Lights,” about Texas high school football.
