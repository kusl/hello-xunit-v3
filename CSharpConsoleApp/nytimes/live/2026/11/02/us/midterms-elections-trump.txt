About 50 million Americans have already cast ballots in this year’s midterm elections, a surge of early voting that is outpacing the last midterm cycle in every major Senate battleground as campaigning draws to a close on Monday. In several states, the share of the early vote from registered Democrats was higher than in 2024, an encouraging sign for the party as it seeks to flip control of Congress.

Still, millions more ballots are expected to be cast on Election Day, and candidates had one final chance on Monday to deliver their closing arguments in high-stakes races all over the country. Interviews with more than 28,000 voters conducted by The New York Times and Siena University revealed consistent themes: economic anxiety, President Trump’s unpopularity and the importance of individual candidates over party affiliation.

Mr. Trump, who has been buffeted by plummeting approval ratings and worrisome polls, said he hoped voters were “ready to do the right thing” during remarks at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool. But he also seemed prepared for the possibility of defeat: “For some reason the sitting president doesn’t win the midterms,” he said, “but we gave this a very strong shot.”

His appearance came just hours after his administration appealed to the Supreme Court in its latest effort to intervene in a state’s elections. In an emergency filing, the administration asked the justices to order California officials to set aside 20,000 mail-in ballots that the administration claims were provided to noncitizens.

Democrats, energized by positive polls in several close races, expressed hope that reclaiming control of the Senate — a goal that once seemed well out of reach — was possible. “I am hopeful that tomorrow we can celebrate,” the Democratic Senate nominee in Maine, Troy Jackson, said as he spent Monday rallying voters and canvassers in Portland, a Democratic stronghold.

For weeks, surveys have indicated that voters’ frustration with the high cost of living has turned even deeply conservative states competitive, as Mr. Trump’s popularity has deteriorated. The latest Times/Siena polls, released on Sunday night, showed Democratic Senate candidates leading their Republican rivals in seven major battleground states.

David Jolly, the Democratic nominee for Florida governor, leaped onto a church stage on Sunday and insisted over and over again, with a sense of conviction unusual for Democrats in this Republican state: “We’re going to win this race!”

He sounded more like a preacher than a politician. And, against all odds, he has started to turn some Florida Democrats into believers.

The state has not elected a Democratic governor since 1994. It has become only more Republican over the last eight years, giving the G.O.P. a wide advantage in voter registration. Mr. Jolly has been outraised and outspent by Representative Byron Donalds, his Republican rival, who has the backing of both President Trump and Gov. Ron DeSantis, the state’s most popular Republican.

Yet it is Democrats who feel the wind at their backs ahead of Tuesday, at least in the governor’s race. Last week, a New York Times/Siena University poll found Mr. Donalds with only a slim lead in the unexpectedly close race.

At Mr. Jolly’s campaign rally on Sunday, Democrats took the microphone one after the other and said what few of them have dared to imagine for many election cycles now.

“Anybody feeling like this is our moment?” Mayor Eileen Higgins of Miami asked to cheers.

“I am so damn ready,” said Mayor Daniella Levine Cava of Miami-Dade County. “I feel for the first time in about a decade that there is real hope for change.”

Mr. Jolly, a former Republican congressman, campaigns from what he calls the “post-ideological space,” telling voters that he is trying to grow the Democratic coalition by welcoming Republicans and independents looking beyond party labels. The son of a pastor, he speaks about moral values driving public policy for the greater good.

“The new coalition that’s emerging in American politics is actually believing in the fundamental goodness of a nation that should provide opportunity to the most number of people, to build a bigger table and create better opportunity,” he said on Sunday at the Coral Gables Congregational Church.

In the audience were two of the three Democrats running for statewide cabinet positions, and one running for Congress. All were looking for a lift from Mr. Jolly’s campaign.

Some Florida Republicans have started to sound alarms about sluggish turnout.

Mr. DeSantis, the protagonist of many of Florida’s recent Republican victories, warned on Sunday that if G.O.P. lag persisted through Election Day, his party might be in trouble.

“If people stay home, you could end up seeing results that you’re going to be wondering what happened,” he said at a campaign event in West Palm Beach for James Uthmeier, the state’s Republican attorney general. “That’s just the reality.”

When he won re-election by 19 percentage points in 2022, Mr. DeSantis noted, “We had real strong enthusiasm.”

“Now, the atmospherics are different,” he added. “I mean, the reality is, gas is higher. That’s not good. Some of these Republicans that try to act like it’s no big deal, I disagree with that. That is a big deal for people.”

Even in Mr. Trump’s adopted home state, which has looked solidly red, not all Florida Republicans may be able to outrun Mr. Trump’s unpopularity and widespread voter discontent over the high cost of living. Mr. Trump has rallied in the state twice over the past 10 days.

As a former Republican, Mr. Jolly might not have otherwise appealed to some loyal Democrats.

We talked to 28,000 voters, and some clear themes emerged.

In the run-up to Election Day, people have looked to polls for indications of how the contest for control of Congress may play out. But beyond the horse race, polls this fall have sent some clear messages about what voters care about.

This year, the Times/Siena Poll made nearly four million phone calls to 1.6 million voters, completing more than 28,000 interviews. We will find out on Tuesday how accurate the polls were by our team and by other pollsters across the country, but there are issues on which voters’ opinions are consistent enough that the lead extends well beyond the margin of error. Those issues help shed light on the current political environment and the motivations behind various midterm election decisions.

It’s the economy …

Polls have consistently shown that the top issue for voters this year is the economy. Every Times/Siena poll conducted in 2026 has found voters listing it as their single biggest driver.

The economy was also a top issue in the 2024 presidential election, but in a departure from recent years, voters are placing their trust in Democrats this time. Nationally, they favor Democrats to handle the economy over Republicans by eight percentage points. The shift is a reversal from just two years ago, when Donald J. Trump was favored by six points over Kamala Harris on this issue.

Across the seven states in our final battleground poll, six of which Mr. Trump won in 2024, voters were nine percentage points more likely to say they trusted Democrats to handle the cost of living. In Kansas and Texas, both states that the president won by double digits in 2024, Democrats also held a nine-point advantage on the issue.

Trump is deeply unpopular, but he has a reliable base.

Regardless of the outcome of the elections on Tuesday, one thing is clear: Voters are frustrated with Mr. Trump. The president’s disapproval rating stands at 60 percent nationally, according to the New York Times presidential approval tracker.

Mr. Trump’s approval rating for his handling of the economy has fallen to 35 percent, a drop from 43 percent in April last year. It is down among men and women; white, Black and Hispanic voters; Democrats, independents and, to a lesser extent, Republicans.

A vast majority of Republicans, however, remain loyal supporters. More than 80 percent of Republicans in the latest Times/Siena poll said they approved of the president’s job performance. Signs of a mass Republican exodus are not there.

Democrats are not especially popular, either.

Democrats are expected to have a strong year, but polls show that the party should not take that as a positive sign for its political brand.

Only 28 percent of voters said they were satisfied with the Democratic Party in a Times/Siena poll from last month, compared with 36 percent who said the same about the Republican Party.

The dynamic can be seen most acutely in Texas, where a CNN/SSRS poll found James Talarico, the Democratic nominee, leading the Senate race by six percentage points in a state where he was more popular than the party that nominated him. He had positive favorability (44 percent favorable, 43 percent unfavorable) among likely voters, while the Democratic Party in Texas was decidedly underwater (36 percent favorable, 56 percent unfavorable).

Immigration is declining as a priority.

For the last two years, the immigration crackdown has dominated the headlines at times. But only 10 percent of voters listed immigration as the top issue motivating their vote this year, down from 15 percent in 2024, and it is mostly Republicans who cite it as an issue.

Trust on the issue has also changed hands. Voters now give Democrats a four-point advantage on trust to handle immigration, after Mr. Trump had an 11-point edge on the issue in 2024.

The individual candidates matter.

Voters’ feelings about individual candidates, not just their political parties, have been shown to matter a great deal.

In South Carolina, a recent Times/Siena poll found the race effectively tied between Senator Darline Graham and Annie Andrews in a state that Mr. Trump won by about 18 percentage points in 2024. Candidate quality has seemed to play a major role in making the race so close.

The poll found that supporters of Ms. Andrews, the Democrat, said their top reasons for backing her were related to positive views of her policies or character (and negative views of Ms. Graham). For Ms. Graham, the Republican appointed to the seat after her brother, Lindsey Graham, died this year, voters’ primary reason for backing her was to help the Republican Party. Less than half as many cited her policies.

The Trump administration asks the Supreme Court to order California to set aside 20,000 ballots.

The Trump administration asked the Supreme Court on Monday to require that California set aside 20,000 mail-in ballots that the administration claims have been provided to noncitizens.

The emergency application to the justices came after a divided federal appeals court rejected the administration’s arguments that California officials should impound the ballots for further scrutiny.

Legal experts have been skeptical of the Trump administration’s arguments in the case. Filing just one day before the midterm elections may forestall a ruling in the case — and a potential loss for the administration — until after Election Day.

The request requires the Supreme Court to consider how much power the federal government has to order states to make last-minute changes to their vote-counting procedures. Under the Constitution, states have responsibility for administering elections. The Justice Department has claimed that it is empowered by a federal law that makes it illegal for noncitizens to cast ballots.

In their emergency application, lawyers for the administration urged the justices to grant what they called a “modest remedy,” requiring California to separate ballots of “citizens and suspected noncitizens.” Solicitor General D. John Sauer wrote that such an order by the court “would not cancel any vote but would allow time to verify the eligibility of suspected noncitizens.”

The legal challenge to 20,000 California ballots is part of a broader effort by the administration to argue that the elections could be marred by illegal votes by noncitizens. The effort comes as polls show Republicans could lose control of one or both chambers of Congress and as President Trump has sought to cast doubt on the validity of the vote, despite little evidence of widespread problems with illegal voting. California officials have said the voter list provided by the Trump administration is filled with errors.

The solicitor general appeared to distance the lawyers in his office, an elite division of the Justice Department that represents the federal government before the Supreme Court, from direct knowledge of whether the people on the list were actually noncitizens.

Mr. Sauer wrote that his office “has been informed that the United States has identified more than 20,000 individuals” who appeared to be registered to vote in California and who “in the United States’ view, are likely not U.S. citizens.”

California leaders denounced the election-eve appeal to the Supreme Court.

The state’s attorney general, Rob Bonta, a Democrat, said in a statement that the administration’s filing “reeks of desperation,” calling it “another attempt to undermine our elections and trample on the rule of law.”

Dean Logan, the Los Angeles County registrar-recorder and county clerk, said in a statement that he had visited voting locations throughout the county over the weekend and was pleased to see voters and elections “not distracted by the outside noise and attempts to disrupt or sow distrust.”

The administration’s emergency application was assigned to Justice Elena Kagan, who handles such petitions from that region of the country and can choose to refer the matter to the full court. It was not clear how quickly she intended to act or whether the justices would take up the application before Election Day.

One elections law expert, Richard L. Hasen, a law professor at the University of California, Los Angeles, called the administration’s move an “emergency Hail Mary petition” that was unlikely to succeed.

As the lawsuit has wound through the lower courts, the administration has also threatened criminal charges over claims of noncitizens voting, including charging 28 people with violating federal election laws and subpoenaing county election workers in California.

The Justice Department sent the list of 20,000 names to Los Angeles County elections officials in October, claiming that those ballots should be separated because the names had been flagged through a government process as potential noncitizens. Officials have been vague about exactly how the list was prepared, and county officials have said that the list appeared to include people with addresses throughout California. Only 7,035 names on the list sent to Los Angeles were listed with addresses in Los Angeles County. Officials confirmed others were born in the United States and were citizens.

Shortly after, the Justice Department sued to stop the counting of mail-in ballots sent to people on the list until their citizenship could be verified.

On Friday, a divided three-judge panel on a federal appeals court rejected arguments by the Justice Department to segregate the contested ballots, finding that the administration had overstepped its bounds by interfering with the state’s role in setting its election rules. The panel also cited a principle repeatedly upheld by the Supreme Court that federal courts should avoid ordering changes to how ballots are counted too close to an election.

That same panel of federal judges had already rejected an appeal in the same case earlier in the week, declining to leapfrog a trial judge.

That judge, David O. Carter, then sided with California officials, writing that the federal government’s request could “wreak havoc” on state election administration and “create a chilling effect on election workers and voters alike.”

Trump returns to a landmark that has come to symbolize his administration’s dysfunction.

President Trump on Monday will make his closing argument before the midterm elections at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool, a landmark whose failed repair job became a high-profile symbol of his second administration’s struggles with governing.

The Reflecting Pool has been closed and fenced off since July, after earlier repairs ended in a debacle: The pool’s new blue waterproof coating peeled up, and algae blooms turned the water bright green.

Since then, contractors have applied a new layer of waterproofing and flushed out the pool’s filtration system. Photos of the pool in recent days have shown it gradually refilling.

Mr. Trump is scheduled to speak at the pool at 2 p.m. Eastern on Monday.

On Sunday, while campaigning for Republican candidates in Iowa, the president told reporters that he wanted to return to the pool to show “how beautiful it is.”

“It’s been finished,” Mr. Trump said.

Then he again blamed vandals for the pool’s problems, even though federal prosecutors and the contractors who repaired the pool said the biggest problem was flaws in the repair work.

“It was vandalized badly,” Mr. Trump said in Iowa. “We have cameras all over the place now for the vandals.”

Fences that have surrounded the pool since July were down on Monday morning, and the pool appeared to be refilled. That entire section of the National Mall was surrounded by a much larger perimeter of fencing set up for Mr. Trump’s visit.

The Interior Department, which oversees the pool, did not respond to questions about the repair work on Monday.

The Reflecting Pool, which is more than 100 years old, is a vast shallow concrete pond that reflects the Lincoln Memorial at one end and the Washington Monument at the other. For years, it has been plagued by leaks and algae.

Mr. Trump has spent millions of dollars seeking to repair and remake Washington landmarks, as well as to build new projects including a White House ballroom and a 250-foot arch near Arlington National Cemetery. Those repairs have often come at the expense of other parks around the country; the administration used at least $67 million in revenue from park entrance fees to help fund projects in Washington.

The Reflecting Pool was one of the icons he sought to fix. Mr. Trump’s administration tried to perform a rush repair job this spring, to fix the pool’s problems in time for the country’s 250th birthday celebrations.

But the repairs quickly veered off course. Instead of a shining symbol of the president’s efforts to beautify the capital, the pool became a stark reflection of dysfunction, dishonesty and the administration’s willingness to use the justice system against its perceived enemies.

The repair project began in April, when the Interior Department awarded a no-bid contract for the pool to Atlantic Industrial Coatings, a small firm in Virginia that had never won a federal contract before. The company was assigned to carry out a plan — designed in part by the general manager of Mr. Trump’s golf course in Bedminster, N.J. — to spread coating in a shade called “American flag blue” across the pool’s concrete slabs.

In all, the government paid that company close to $15 million. It also gave a no-bid contract to a company connected to one of Mr. Trump’s political donors, hiring that firm to upgrade the pool’s filtration system.

After weeks of work, the pool was refilled the first time in June. But its water quickly turned green with algae, and sections of the new blue coating began to tear and separate.

Mr. Trump blamed both problems on vandals, and federal prosecutors charged several people with crimes, saying that they tore the blue coating. But a few weeks later, Jeanine Pirro, the U.S. attorney for the District of Columbia, asked the court to drop those charges, saying that a botched repair job had actually caused the damage.

Atlantic, the company that carried out the repairs, reached a similar conclusion. It informed the Interior Department that the problems were caused by “human oversight” in the application of the blue lining and a flawed design in which one layer of coating destabilized the one underneath it, according to government documents reviewed by The New York Times.

After the pool was drained a second time, Atlantic returned to fix its original work under warranty. The company said that it had shifted its approach to minimize human error and that it would avoid layering the incompatible chemicals atop one another, government documents show.

A federal judge said last month that federal prosecutors could never revive the one felony charge they had filed against the former Olympian David Hearn, who was accused of pulling up a piece of the pool’s blue coating. Lawyers for Mr. Hearn have asked internal government watchdogs to investigate the case, saying senior administration officials ignored evidence of the botched renovation in a rush to satisfy Mr. Trump’s demands for criminal charges.

Early voting totals reveal a surge of registered Democrats in several key states.

Election Day is Tuesday, but about 50 million ballots have already been cast, with early turnout outpacing the last midterm cycle in each of the major Senate battlegrounds.

In several key states, the share of registered Democrats has increased in the early vote, which includes mail-in ballots and early in-person voting. The overall picture offers few positives for Republicans, who saw strong early in-person turnout in 2024. And the surge provides indications that it could be a high-turnout election in the realm of 2018 and 2022, which set records for midterm turnout.

But millions of voters will cast their ballots on Election Day — in recent elections, around half of the votes nationally have come that day — and the impact of the early vote will be known only after polls close. That makes it difficult to pinpoint what those early votes say about each race.

While the early vote can’t reliably predict who is going to win, the numbers through Monday morning offer clues about which subgroups are turning out, and there are a few warning signs for Republicans.

Early turnout is up, with Democrats at an advantage.

In every Senate battleground that we are tracking, the number of early ballots cast has surpassed the total number of early ballots cast in 2022. That year trailed only 2018 for total turnout in a midterm election. (Midterms typically run well below presidential elections in total voters.) In Maine, South Carolina and Texas, early turnout this year is closer to what was seen for the 2024 presidential election.

Democrats have traditionally been more likely to vote before Election Day than Republicans, so their advantage in the early vote was to be expected. Still, in several states, the partisan makeup of the electorate is even more Democratic than it was in 2024. (In Georgia, where voters do not register with a political party, The Times estimates partisanship using party primary participation.)

The increased early turnout could be a sign that overall turnout will be much higher this year. But it is unreliable to project overall turnout by extrapolating from early vote totals. Such an inference would have vastly overestimated turnout in 2024, for instance, when the share of early in-person votes surged in many competitive states.

It is possible that the higher early-vote tallies are a matter of people who would have previously voted on Election Day deciding to vote early instead.

In the South, Black early turnout is up.

In Georgia, North Carolina and South Carolina — Southern states that went for Donald J. Trump in 2024 but are a focus of Democrats’ goal of flipping the Senate — Black voters make up a larger share of the advance vote than they did in recent cycles. (All three states record race on their voter files.)

Black voters in North Carolina made up 21 percent of early votes cast through Oct. 31, which was only a couple of percentage points higher than it was in 2022 and 2024. But with Black voters in these three states leaning so heavily Democratic — in 2024, Kamala Harris won between 87 and 90 percent of Black voters in each, according to Times Voter Trends — even small increases in their share of the electorate are significant.

The unanswerable question is whether a disproportionate number of Black voters chose to vote early this year rather than on Election Day, and whether Tuesday’s turnout will skew significantly toward white voters. In all three states, a majority of votes are typically cast during the early voting period, making the early vote in past years more representative than it was elsewhere.

Early voting gets younger as Election Day nears.

Older voters traditionally dominate the earliest ballots, as they tend to become politically engaged earlier in the cycle. That pattern is particularly pronounced during midterm elections, and this year is no exception.

For example, in Michigan, the median age of those who have voted so far is 67. That is roughly on par with 2022, when it was 65. (In 2024, it was 59.)

The final days and weeks of early voting, however, typically see a surge of young voters. Four weeks ago, about 11 percent of the ballots cast in Michigan came from those under the age of 45. In the past week, it was 30 percent.

Other states followed a similar trend. In general, mail ballots that are received later tend to lean Democratic. This could be relevant in states like Michigan, where in past elections, a batch of late-arriving mail ballots from Detroit grew the Democratic candidate’s vote share after Election Day.

Turnout in Texas surges in Hispanic counties.

The vast majority of Texans cast their ballots before Election Day, and early turnout in Texas this year has approached presidential levels, with ballots cast through Oct. 30 off by only 15 percent from the same point in 2024.

Given that the state is broadly seen as a tossup for the first time in decades, with a Senate race that has drawn hundreds of millions of dollars in spending, such high turnout is unsurprising. Still, the raw numbers are staggering. Nearly eight million Texans have cast their ballots thus far, rapidly approaching the total turnout of 2022 — including Election Day votes.

Turnout is highest in the most Hispanic counties. Of the eight counties running ahead of their 2024 totals, seven of them are more than 70 percent Hispanic. That could be good news for Democrats, with the most recent New York Times/Siena poll of Texas showing James Talarico, the Democratic candidate for Senate, leading among Hispanic voters by 38 percentage points.

But high turnout among this group is not always a good sign for Democrats. In 2020 and 2024, large South Texas counties with high Hispanic populations, like Hidalgo and Starr Counties, saw record-breaking turnout paired with massive Republican gains — and Mr. Trump flipped many of them two years ago.
