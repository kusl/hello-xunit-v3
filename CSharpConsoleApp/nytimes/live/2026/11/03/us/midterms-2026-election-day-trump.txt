Here’s the latest.

Voters across the United States began heading to the polls on Tuesday, capping a divisive midterm campaign that will determine control of Congress and offer the first nationwide verdict on President Trump’s second term.

Mr. Trump, who has repeatedly urged his supporters to act as if he is on the ballot, has insisted that Republicans would win if his base turned out. Democratic campaigns have blamed the president and his signature policies for the struggles of Americans trying to make ends meet.

Republicans attempted to transform the election from a referendum on Mr. Trump into a choice between their party and Democratic opponents they cast as radical, far-left activists.

Polls show that Democrats are widely favored to win control of the House, although it is not clear how deep into Republican terrain their victories might extend. Surveys also indicate that Democrats are on track to increase their numbers in the Senate, and party leaders are cautiously optimistic about winning a majority.

But that is a tall order. Democrats would have to flip four Republican-held seats and defend all of their own to take control of the chamber.

Early voting has broken recent midterm records, with more than tens of millions of ballots already cast. Early turnout exceeded that of the last midterm cycle in each of the major Senate battlegrounds. And in several key states, the share of registered Democrats has increased in the early vote.

So has spending. This is the most expensive midterm election, as measured in political advertising, since at least 2018, according to data from AdImpact. Elon Musk, the world’s richest person, contributed $474 million to Republican candidates and their allies, more than any donor has ever spent in a single election cycle.

The first polls close at 7 p.m. Eastern, but it could take days or weeks before the full results are known, particularly if many of the races are close. In 2024, it took three weeks to determine the winners in California’s closest House races.

Here’s what you can (and can’t) do at polling sites.

In America, it is largely up to each state to decide the laws that govern elections. That means there is a jumble of rules in place around the country on Election Day, including whether voters have to present identification to cast a ballot, and even how closely you can approach a polling place with a political sign or T-shirt.

As such, it’s important to check your state government’s website to make sure you’re within the bounds of specific regulations. But here’s a broader look at what is and isn’t allowed at polling sites across the country.

Electioneering

Every state has some kind of law about political activity near polling places. And at a federal level, actual or attempted voter intimidation or coercion are criminal offenses.

Electioneering refers to any kind of overtly political activity near a polling site while voting is taking place. Most states restrict the display of signs or the handing out of campaign literature within 100 feet of a polling site.

Some states go further, and bar voters from wearing clothing or pins that bear a candidate’s name or political messaging. A handful prohibit general loitering around polling stations, and eight states prohibit the taking of exit polls as voters leave polling sites.

Providing ID

Most states require some sort of ID to vote. In the 14 that don’t, identification is instead required at the time of voter registration, and poll workers are not allowed to ask voters for identification on voting day.

Selfies

Snapping a selfie with your completed ballot is illegal in 15 states, including New York, New Jersey, Georgia, Massachusetts and Minnesota.

Arizona, Texas, Tennessee and West Virginia allow ballot selfies, but only for absentee or mail-in ballots.

While some argue that ballot selfies are a form of protected speech, critics have claimed that it can facilitate vote buying by giving people a legal way to prove they supported a specific candidate or ballot question.

Closing times

Each state decides the hour its polls close, but they all agree on one point: If you are in line to vote when that hour arrives, you will be allowed to cast a ballot.

Stepping out of that line after the polls have closed, however, is different. If you leave a voting line after polls have closed, you most likely will not be allowed to rejoin it.

Election Day could be a rainy one in some areas.

With millions of voters having cast their ballots already, experts say the weather likely won’t determine the outcome of Tuesday’s elections, even with rain in the forecast for states with competitive races, such as Texas, Iowa, Kansas, Mississippi and Florida.

But then again, it could.

Weather has always been an uncontrollable and slightly unpredictable factor in elections.

In recent decades, scientists have turned years of anecdotes into rigorous analyses of voting and weather data. Their conclusion: Yes, weather can indeed (slightly) influence turnout.

Overall, Tuesday is shaping up to be a “pretty tranquil day,” with the East Coast and West Coast looking clear and comfortable, said Jennifer Tate, a meteorologist with the National Weather Service. The exceptions will be portions of the central United States and Florida.

There will be at least some risk of thunderstorms from Iowa to Texas on Tuesday, and pockets of excessive rain that could lead to minor flash flooding.

How much does the weather matter?

It depends on whom you ask, and ongoing research continues to examine the trends in weather and voter turnout.

Bad weather in one or two tight districts could affect an outcome by keeping some voters at home, Kasper M. Hansen, a political science professor at the University of Copenhagen and an author of a study on weather and turnout, told The New York Times in 2024.

In that study, he found that what he termed “marginal voters” — people who only occasionally turn out to vote — are more likely to opt out if Election Day arrives with consistent, miserable rain.

Thomas Fujiwara, an associate professor of economics and international affairs at Princeton University, has also found that rainfall lowers turnout, though by modest amounts. He is an author of a 2016 paper that examined the effect of rain on one election and whether it reverberated in future cycles.

On Monday, Mr. Fujiwara said he hadn’t conducted any new research on elections and weather since then. But he noted that the impact on turnout is likely smaller today than in the period he analyzed (1948 to 2012), because a larger share of the electorate now votes early or by mail.

On the other hand, Mr. Hansen, suggested that recent research suggests that bad weather continues to suppress turnout, “especially on the marginal voter (e.g., young voters).”

Mr. Hansen’s research has shown that die-hard voters aren’t likely to be deterred by the weather. Those people tend to vote early, or will stand in line in any conditions. Instead, he has found, the people most likely to avoid voting in inclement weather are those for whom voting doesn’t fit easily into a busy schedule, or those living alone — typically people ages 21 to 30.

The Iran war is unpopular. Why does Trump keep talking about it?

The war in Iran is deeply unpopular with Americans. Recent polls indicate as many as two-thirds of voters disapprove of President Trump’s handling of the eight-month conflict, which has driven up prices and depleted U.S. ammunition stockpiles.

Yet in the final days of the campaign, Mr. Trump kept talking about it.

“We still have to take care of, I would say, one or two sites, but we can do that in 10 minutes,” he told reporters on Saturday.

“The only reason we’re not finished with Iran is, I want to be nice, I want to try and save all those lives if I can,” he said at a campaign rally on Sunday.

“We had a war with Iran,” he added Monday. “We won.” Hours later, officials confirmed that the U.S. military had struck Iran’s Qeshm Island in what they described as a defensive measure that was not expected to escalate.

Here are three reasons why Mr. Trump kept bringing up Iran, according to analysts.

To push Iran toward a deal

Mr. Trump, who prides himself on his deal-making, could be trying to restart talks.

“By threatening strikes, he wants to send the Iranians a message: ‘Come to the table now or else,’” said Arta Moeini, managing director of the nonpartisan Institute for Peace and Diplomacy, which advocates for military restraint.

Iran’s foreign ministry on Monday said it was prepared to reopen the Strait of Hormuz in two days if the United States agreed to several demands, including lifting U.S. economic sanctions against Iran and releasing billions of dollars in frozen Iranian assets.

The Trump administration is unlikely to accept either condition. For now, Mr. Trump “is going to use the promise of diplomacy again, as he has done before,” Mr. Moeini said. “He can announce that, and maybe get better results for the election, but still later say, ‘They didn’t do what they promised to do, and so we can attack.’”

‘Tough guy’ rhetoric has worked before

Threatening rhetoric is part of Mr. Trump’s brand and has helped him build a loyal following.

“There is a very real strand of American public opinion, the Jacksonian tradition, that likes a leader who projects toughness and who is prepared to whack the bad guys,” said Andrew Payne, a research director at Chatham House, a policy institute in London.

But Mr. Payne called that approach to Iran a “misguided” political strategy. “You’ve seen the polling,” he said.

He sees Iran as a legacy issue

Yet polling will matter less for Mr. Trump going forward. Regardless of the outcome of Tuesday’s elections, he will be entering his final two years in office, a period when presidents often take policy risks to secure their legacies.

“He has staked his legacy in foreign policy, to some degree, on Iran,” Mr. Payne said. “It’s more important for him to try and get some favorable resolution to that in the long term than worrying about the short-term impact at the polls.”

The Trump administration is already moving three aircraft carriers, thousands of Marines, fighter jets and Special Operations forces to the Persian Gulf region, to be ready to strike within days.

“I do believe that he will escalate no matter what,” Mr. Moeini said. “I don’t think that’s cheap talk.”

TV viewers will have a plethora of options on Election Night.

Inflation may be a top concern for voters in Tuesday’s midterm elections — but broadcast TV is embracing it.

As recently as 2014, the Big Three networks — ABC, CBS and NBC — aired little more than an hour or two of live coverage of midterm results, barely a blip between reality shows, sitcoms and procedurals.

On Tuesday, NBC will offer its local stations 13 consecutive hours of nonstop coverage, beginning in the late afternoon and extending all the way through early Wednesday. CBS and ABC will hand over their entire prime-time coverage to election results, and will return for more coverage overnight.

Because the results span hundreds of local races, midterm nights do not always offer the same tidy on-air narrative that a presidential election promises. But with the balance of power in Congress at stake — and Democrats and Republicans alike calling the evening existential — executives are expecting significant audience interest in Tuesday’s returns.

Here is a look at how the broadcast and cable networks, as well as some of the newer digital outlets, are covering the results.

The Broadcast Networks

NBC will start coverage at 5 p.m. Eastern, and won’t stop until 6 a.m. Wednesday. (Local affiliates will have the opportunity to take the entire package, or to take breaks for their local newscasts.)

The network’s coverage will be helmed by the “Today” anchor Savannah Guthrie, the “Nightly News” anchor Tom Llamas, the “Meet the Press” moderator Kristen Welker and the correspondent Hallie Jackson. The popular data-cruncher Steve Kornacki will return to his usual perch, ready to frantically punch at his big board. The “Kornacki Cam,” a continuous livestream of the NBC data analyst, will go live at 5:30 p.m.

CBS formally kicks off coverage at 7 p.m., led by the “Evening News” anchor Tony Dokoupil, the morning show co-hosts Gayle King and Norah O’Donnell, and the “Face the Nation” moderator Margaret Brennan. A livestreamed “Nerd Show” featuring the network’s polling experts will simultaneously air online from 5 p.m. to 2 a.m.

And ABC will kick off four consecutive hours of coverage at 7 p.m., with the “World News Tonight” anchor David Muir leading the coverage. Mr. Muir, along with a team that includes “World News Tonight” Sunday anchor Linsey Davis and “This Week” co-anchor Martha Raddatz, will be live for seven consecutive hours on ABC News Live, Hulu and Disney+.

The Cable Networks

Fox News’s coverage will be hosted by the anchors Bret Baier and Martha MacCallum, with Bill Hemmer utilizing the interactive digital map known as “The Bill Board.” An A.I. chatbot has been created on the channel’s app so users can ask specific questions about ongoing results. And Arnon Mishkin, the cello-playing pollster, will again be in charge of the network’s decision desk.

Jake Tapper and Anderson Cooper will lead CNN’s coverage, which includes John King and his usual “magic wall,” the name of his touch screen interactive map. The network said Mr. King would be outfitted with multiple magic walls for the first time, featuring additional data. On MS NOW, Rachel Maddow will lead an ensemble team including network favorites like Nicolle Wallace and Ari Melber. The anchor Ali Velshi is in charge of the “Big Board,” its digital interactive map.

Digital Livestreams

Of course, there will be hundreds of online sources for voters to consult, too. Nate Silver, the veteran election forecaster, will host a livestream with, among others, the writer Matt Yglesias. The Dispatch will host another livestream that begins at 9 p.m. Eastern. Chuck Todd and Chris Cillizza are collaborating with Decision Desk HQ, a polling and results-counting service, for livestreamed coverage. There will be, however, few late-night shows offering an on-the-fly comedic take on the results. CBS’s “Late Show” with Stephen Colbert, which regularly aired live episodes on major election nights, was canceled this year. NBC’s extensive coverage means there will be no reactions from Seth Meyers or Jimmy Fallon. “The Daily Show” has a new episode (with Ronny Chieng hosting), but it is taping in the late afternoon.

ABC’s “Jimmy Kimmel Live!” will air a new episode in its usual slot at 11:35 p.m., but it will not be live. Still, there could be some news. “Jimmy Kimmel Live!” is taping later than usual — after 9 p.m. Eastern — so it can incorporate some early results, and Mr. Kimmel’s guests will be the progressive hosts from the “Pod Save America” podcast.

How will tonight go? Here are the races that will provide early clues.

On Election Day, states where polls close and ballot counting begins early could offer the first concrete clues for how the rest of the night will go, with control of Congress on the line.

Here are some poll closures to watch in the first hours of election night. All times are Eastern.

6 p.m.: Kentucky and Indiana

President Trump won Kentucky’s Sixth Congressional District by 15 points in 2024, but its House race this year has been rated a tossup by nonpartisan election forecasters. If the Democratic candidate, Zach Dembo, a former federal prosecutor and Navy veteran, beats the Republican, Dr. Ralph Alvarado, a physician and former state senator endorsed by Mr. Trump, it would signal Democrats’ strength overall.

In Indiana’s traditionally conservative Fifth District, Representative Victoria Spartz, a Republican, won by about 19 points in 2024 and is favored to retain the seat on Tuesday, according to forecasters. But her lead has narrowed the area, where Democrats are usually not competitive. If the winner is her Democratic challenger, State Senator J.D. Ford, it could signal broader Democratic victories.

Democrats do not usually win votes from rural white voters in either district, and if one or both Democratic candidates prevail, that “would indicate that we are winning in a way that is going to translate across the country in many similar districts,” said CJ Warnke, a spokesman for the House Majority PAC, the biggest super PAC for House Democrats.

If a Republican wins either race, it could mean that their party might mitigate its losses elsewhere.

7 p.m.: Florida

Republican state lawmakers redrew several congressional districts to favor their party. Despite that, analysts say many of these districts have close races. If Democrats win in the Seventh, Ninth, 14th, 22nd or 25th Districts, it could bode well for them elsewhere.

Republican victories in these districts could mean that the party’s redistricting efforts successfully blocked a Democratic surge during a difficult year for Republicans.

Republicans are favored to win the Senate and governor’s races in Florida, according to analysts. But Democrats have lately been hopeful about the governor’s race, where David Jolly, the Democratic nominee, is challenging Representative Byron Donalds, a Republican. If Democrats win the Senate and governor’s races, they are having a very good night.

Even if there is a victory for Mr. Donalds or the Republican incumbent in the Senate, Ashley Moody, Democrats may find that some demographic groups bear watching.

Each Hispanic and Black community in the United States is unique, but significant shifts in how the groups vote in South Florida could indicate how Democrats elsewhere, like James Talarico in the Texas Senate race, will fare. Hispanic voters, who make up about a third of the Texas electorate, are a key swing voting bloc in the state.

7 p.m.: South Carolina

South Carolina’s First District has not only been traditionally conservative-leaning, but it has also been gerrymandered by Republicans to further benefit their party. Still, the race has been rated a tossup by analysts.

If Nancy Lacore, a Democratic former Navy admiral who was fired by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, wins the open seat over Jenny Costa Honeycutt, a Republican local councilwoman and lawyer, it would signal that a strong Democratic candidate could win in red territory elsewhere in the country. But if Ms. Honeycutt wins, it could reflect the strength of the region’s conservative base and the redrawn district lines.

The chances of a Democratic victory in the state’s Senate race are slimmer, but the lead held by Darline Graham, who was appointed to fill the seat after the death of her brother, Senator Lindsey Graham, has narrowed throughout her campaign. If she loses to Dr. Annie Andrews, a pediatrician running as a Democrat, that could mean that Republicans may lose their majority in the upper chamber.

7 p.m.: Virginia

Virginia’s Second District traditionally swings, and analysts said that the Democratic candidate, former Representative Elaine Luria, was favored to flip it. The result could indicate whether a strong Republican candidate like Jen Kiggans, a two-term incumbent, can survive Democratic momentum.

Mr. Trump won the neighboring First District by five points in 2024, but analysts said the House race there this year was a tossup. If Shannon Taylor, a Democratic prosecutor, defeats Representative Rob Wittman, a Republican first elected in 2007, Democrats could be in for a great night.

Voters prepare to deliver a verdict on Trump’s second term.

For the last two months, President Trump has offered a consistent plea: Pretend he’s on the ballot.

The midterm elections on Tuesday will give voters an opportunity to do just that. They are set to render the first national referendum on a second-term Trump agenda characterized by far-reaching tariffs, an unpopular war with Iran and an aggressive immigration crackdown.

Republican officials are bracing for a harsh judgment and potentially significant defeats. They widely expect to lose control of the House. They have grown increasingly worried about losing the Senate, too.

Democrats have sought to define their campaigns through anger over high prices driven by tariffs and the war, focusing heavily on the idea of making life cost less. Led by Mr. Trump, Republicans have struggled to articulate an affirmative counterargument, with the president dismissing concerns about the economy and sending an often scattered message.

American politics is of course full of surprises. While Republicans have underperformed in both midterm elections of the Trump era, the president has demonstrated his ability to turn out his most loyal supporters. He has also stepped up his baseless attacks on the U.S. election system, raising fears of federal interference in the results.

Democrats need a net gain of just three House seats to win a majority. The Senate is a heavier lift: Democrats must hold all of their seats and flip at least four Republican-held ones. They are competing in states like Texas, which has not elected a Democratic senator since 1988, and Kansas, which has not done so since 1932.

And if Democrats flip at least two Republican-held governorships, they will hold a majority of them for the first time since 2010, when a backlash to President Barack Obama fueled a Republican wave that has shaped American politics ever since.

Polls will close starting at 6 p.m. Eastern. The results that follow will shape the country for at least the next two years, and perhaps much longer. Here are six themes to watch as voting ends:

How deep into Republican territory can Democrats reach?

Look at where the competitive races were in the final days of the campaign.

Solidly red Kansas and Ohio have tossup Senate races. Democrats are seriously contending in House districts like the one in northern New York being vacated by Representative Elise Stefanik — where Mr. Trump won by 21 percentage points two years ago.

Governor’s races are tight in conservative states like Florida, Nebraska and Oklahoma, where Gov. Andy Beshear of Kentucky, the chairman of the Democratic Governors Association, spent Monday night stumping for the party’s nominee. The last Democratic presidential candidate to win even a single county in Oklahoma was Al Gore in 2000.

Over the weekend, Republicans rushed money into Louisiana’s Senate race, which had not been considered a competitive contest before then. Both parties have also directed late money to the Senate races in Mississippi and South Carolina, states that have been out of reach for Democrats for a generation.

With Democrats widely expected to win races in at least some red House districts and threatening to flip Senate seats in deep-red states, the question is how far into Republican terrain they can prevail.

How angry are voters about Trump’s handling of the economy?

Voters care most about whether they can afford their everyday needs, and polls suggest that they are angered about the high prices brought on by tariffs and a war ordered by Mr. Trump.

The president spent the day before the election talking about his alterations to the Reflecting Pool in Washington. Over the last few weeks, some of his most memorable rally lines have been about the anatomies of Republican Senate candidates.

Mr. Trump’s approval ratings have sunk to record lows. Republican candidates who have recast their political identities in his image are struggling to convince voters that they can be independent.

In many cases, Republicans have not even tried to make a case that the economy is in good shape. Instead, they have tried to change the subject.

In Michigan’s Senate race, former Representative Mike Rogers has sought to push the Muslim faith and family background of his Democratic opponent, Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, to the center of the campaign. In Texas, voters have seen an onslaught of Republican ads about how James Talarico, the Democratic Senate nominee, talked about transgender rights in the past. And in Maine, Senator Susan Collins is selling voters on the idea that her seniority after four terms is best for her state.

If Democrats win the House, how big will their majority be?

We could know fairly early in the evening what kind of night it will be for the House.

In Indiana and the eastern half of Kentucky, polls close at 6 p.m. Eastern. There are tight races in Kentucky’s Sixth Congressional District, which Mr. Trump carried by 15 points in 2024, and Indiana’s Fifth District, which he won by 17 points.

Both are competitive races that will reveal some of the first clues about the rest of the electoral map. They are districts that combine rural and suburban voters and are usually safe for Republicans.

There are dozens of similar House districts across the country.

Nevada’s Second District covers Reno and the state’s rural northern half. Mr. Trump carried it by 14 points. It is not a place where Democrats typically compete. But this year, Democrats have a strong candidate, the Republican incumbent is retiring and the party’s candidate is considered weak while a potential third-party spoiler is poised to siphon conservative votes with help from Democrats.

Districts like these will determine how big of a cushion House Democrats might have when new members are seated come January.

What’s the fallout from the national redistricting fight?

Starting with Texas last summer, Republican-led states began redrawing their congressional district lines to try to secure more House seats for their party.

The new maps were based on the idea that the electorate in 2026 would look somewhat like the one that carried Mr. Trump to victory in 2024.

That does not seem to be the case.

Latino voters have turned against Mr. Trump, shifting the politics of South Texas and South Florida, where Republicans tried to lock in advantages. Rural voters are furious about the price of diesel fuel. Black voters, early voting data suggests, are turning out in huge numbers.

The result is that House districts drawn with the express intent of putting them in Republican hands instead have competitive races. In Texas, Florida, Tennessee and North Carolina, there are increasingly tight races in districts Mr. Trump would have carried by double digits had they existed in 2024.

Meantime in California, voters approved a plan aimed at delivering five House seats for Democrats, and redistricting does not seem to have placed any of the party’s seats at risk.

How all of these races go may have ramifications beyond immediate House control. Several states are weighing redrawing their congressional maps before the 2028 elections. If this year’s new maps backfire, it could temper the aggression of next year’s cartographers.

What about all the Republican money?

Elon Musk spent at least $474 million to back Republicans in the midterm elections. The next four biggest donors are also Republicans. And Mr. Trump had an unmatched war chest.

If Republicans defy expectations, it will be at least in part because of a financial advantage built long before races became tight in the campaign’s final weeks.

At the same time, Democratic officials involved in House and Senate races have lamented that they do not have the money to compete in as many districts and states as they would like.

The Texas governor’s race, for example, became a source of frustration for Latino Democrats. For weeks, they have been angry that the party did not invest more in Gina Hinojosa, a state representative who polls show is running even or close behind Gov. Greg Abbott, the three-term incumbent Republican.

House Democratic officials have privately ticked through a series of districts where they believed they had chances to win if only they had more resources available.

Will Democrats be hurt by their Senate-race dramas?

The red-state Senate races in Iowa, Kansas, Mississippi, Ohio, Texas and South Carolina are competitive in part because Democrats have nominated a generally strong slate of candidates who have mostly avoided major problems on the campaign trail.

That’s not the case for their blue-state brethren.

Maine Democrats switched out their candidate in July after the primary winner, Graham Platner, was forced out of the race after a rape allegation, which he denied.

Michigan was convulsed this weekend by a late endorsement flip, with Representative Haley Stevens, who lost the Senate primary to Dr. El-Sayed, defecting and backing the Republican in the race, Mr. Rogers.

And in light-blue Minnesota, Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan has led in polling in her Senate race despite being trailed by the state’s major fraud scandal and being viewed as running one of the party’s weakest campaigns.

There’s also Alaska, where a New York Times report last week about Democratic chicanery to help Mary Peltola, the party’s nominee for Senate, by recruiting a second Dan Sullivan into the race set off a wave of Republican anger.

Polls show the Democratic nominees leading or with an edge in all four races, but some of these contests — especially in Michigan and Maine, where Ms. Collins, the incumbent Republican, is a formidable opponent — have required more resources and attention than party leaders would have preferred.
