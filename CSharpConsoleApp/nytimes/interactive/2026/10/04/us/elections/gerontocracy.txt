ARTICLE

Voters Say Congress Is Too Old. But Older Candidates Keep Winning.
In primary races across the country, incumbency trumped calls for generational change, as older members of Congress held off younger challengers.
By Lisa Lerer, Matt Zdun and Ashley Wu
Published 2026-10-04 09:01 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/10/04/us/elections/gerontocracy.html

In the lead-up to the midterm elections, calls for generational change have echoed through districts across the country, as younger candidates have pushed to replace the old guard.

In Massachusetts, Senator Ed Markey, 80, was diagnosed with “chronic re-election disorder” by allies of his younger primary opponent. “Doctors agree: Democracy suffers when politicians won’t pass the torch,” said an actor dressed as a doctor in an attack ad.

In Northern California, a spot denouncing Representative Mike Thompson, 75, compared the Capitol to a refrigerator, full of “career politicians” who “sit there year after year behind the pickles.” Thompson, the ad said, was “past his expiration date.”

And in Mississippi, an ad featured a woman identified as a retired public-school teacher wondering about the effectiveness of Representative Bennie Thompson, 78, who has been in Congress for over three decades. “Has he made things better here?” she asked. “Absolutely not.”

And yet, the gerontocracy remains alive and well in American politics. In primary after primary, voters rejected those calls for younger leadership, voting for incumbent politicians who were notably older than their challengers, according to an analysis by The New York Times.

Their wins didn’t stem from a failure by younger candidates to press the case: Campaign ads pushing for new, younger leadership ran in at least 77 House and Senate primary races, according to a Times analysis of data from AdImpact, a media tracking firm.

Instead, the results point to an unshakable reality of American politics: Incumbency remains a near-invincible force in congressional elections. With stronger name recognition and a fund-raising advantage, members of Congress typically hold onto their seats as long as they like.

Over the past four decades, the re-election rate for incumbents has rarely dipped below 80 percent. The incumbent has lost in only 78 primary races out of several thousand over the past two decades, according to a New York Times analysis of data from Ballotpedia.

Heading into November, several older incumbents are facing general election challenges from much younger candidates. In Ohio, for instance, Representative Marcy Kaptur, an 80-year-old Democrat, is in a tough race against Derek Merrin, a Republican who is 40.

Should they win in the general election, many of those older politicians will find themselves in good company in Washington. The current Congress is the oldest in modern history, with more than 135 members who will be 70 or older by the end of their term.

Octogenarians hold some of the most powerful positions in the federal government long past the point when most Americans have retired.

President Trump, now 80, became the oldest president to ever take office when he started his second term in 2025 — a title he claimed from former President Joseph R. Biden Jr., who entered the White House at age 78.

“In actuality, life begins at 80!” Mr. Trump proudly declared on social media in 2022, back when he was a spry 76 years old.

Senator Charles E. Grassley, Republican of Iowa, is third in line to the presidency at age 93 and leads the powerful Judiciary Committee. Senator Bernie Sanders, the Vermont independent who continues to hold sway in Democratic politics, is 85, one of seven octogenarians in the chamber. Senator Mitch McConnell, 84, who recently returned to the Senate after suffering a fall at his Capitol Hill home, served as party leader until the age of 82.

Like the indignities of growing older, signs of the nation’s aging political class have become increasingly difficult to ignore.

In 2023, video of Mr. McConnell, then 81, freezing for 20 seconds in front of television cameras reverberated across the internet and newscasts. Soon after, a clip surfaced of Senator Dianne Feinstein, 90, appearing confused when asked to vote in committee.

A year later, Mr. Biden ignited a firestorm after he faltered in his final presidential debate, inflaming long-simmering concerns about his fitness to serve a second term that culminated in an extraordinary wave of calls from within the Democratic Party to replace him on the ticket.

The party’s defeat — and the many months of recriminations that followed — ignited a broader reckoning over its aging leadership and a deep desire among Democratic voters for new faces.

Some members of both parties seemed to heed the call: This Congress saw a notable number of retirements, with 28 members over age 65 announcing their exit, according to a Times analysis. Their ranks included longtime party leaders like former Speaker Nancy Pelosi of California, 86, and Mr. McConnell, who is not seeking re-election.

“Watching the Biden thing really said something about the necessity for generational change in the party, and I think I want to respect that,” Representative Jerrold Nadler, 79, a New York Democrat, said when he decided to step aside last year after more than three decades in the House.

Several other older members died while in office. Over the past two years, the deaths of six lawmakers scrambled races from South Carolina to Arizona. Over the summer, Senator Lindsey Graham, Republican of South Carolina, died of a heart issue two days after his 71st birthday — relatively young by congressional standards.

But this year, when faced with the choice between an older incumbent and a younger challenger, voters more frequently chose experience over a fresh voice.

In Massachusetts, Mr. Markey defeated Representative Seth Moulton, 47, a moderate Democrat who had campaigned on a message of generational change. The oldest senator in his state’s history, Mr. Markey arrived in Washington in 1976, entering Congress before a majority of his current constituents were born. He served nearly 20 terms in the House before being elected to the Senate in 2013.

In Republican primaries, the biggest age gap was in Texas, where Representative John Carter, 84, defeated Valentina Gomez, who was 26 on the day of the election. For Democrats, it was in Ohio, where Joyce Beatty, 76, faced off against Joe Gerard, 26. The Gen Z challengers in each of those races captured less than a quarter of the vote.

Only seven Gen Z candidates took on incumbents in primaries, and all but one came up short. The exception was in Colorado’s First Congressional District, where Melat Kiros, who was 29 on Election Day, ousted the incumbent, Diana DeGette, who was 68.

To be sure, not all older incumbents succeeded. And among the handful who lost, several were bested by far younger opponents — in races with some of the largest age gaps in recent history. In New York, where Representative Adriano Espaillat lost to Darializa Avila Chevalier in a Democratic primary, there was a 39-year gap. In Texas, where Representative Al Green lost to Christian Menefee, the age gap was 40 years.

In Connecticut, Representative John Larson, a 78-year-old Democrat who attracted attention after suffering a complex partial seizure on the House floor last year, failed in his efforts to win a 15th term.

Campaigning across his Hartford-area district, Mr. Larson, who was backed by his party’s leaders, argued that voters would lose political power if they ousted him from his post as the top Democrat on the House Social Security Subcommittee.

His opponents questioned the potency of his seniority, noting that Mr. Larson failed to advance his signature piece of Social Security legislation for more than a decade, including when Democrats controlled Congress.

“This notion that you show up and you’re going to pass something — that’s a fallacy,” Mr. Larson said in an interview, as he campaigned at a senior center over the summer. “It takes time.”

But in August, Luke Bronin, 47, the former mayor of Hartford, won the primary race by a double-digit margin.

“This is a political earthquake in Connecticut,” Mr. Bronin proclaimed at his victory night party in Hartford.

Part of the challenge facing proponents of generational change in Congress is that Americans do not agree on how old, exactly, is too old.

While polling consistently shows that broad majorities of Americans support age caps for Congress, voters disagree over when those limits should kick in.

Rahm Emanuel, a prominent Democrat who has signaled his desire to run for president in 2028, has called for a mandatory retirement age of 75 for the president, cabinet officials, members of Congress and federal judges. Other politicians, including Gov. Ron DeSantis of Florida and Senator Ted Cruz of Texas, have proposed term limits — a policy that sidesteps the issue of age but would likely force the retirement of some of Congress’s oldest members.

But while other industries have mandatory retirement ages, members of Congress have shown little desire for policies that would amount to voting themselves out of a job.

Nor, it seems, have their voters.
