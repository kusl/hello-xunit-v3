Opinion

What
America is
Living
Without

By Alissa Quart
Photographs by Tim Davis and Anh Nguyen Alissa Quart is the editorial director of the Economic Hardship Reporting Project.

There are stories many of us tell ourselves about what we were given from birth, what we can attain and what we deserve — master narratives of our place in society. My parents sacrificed so I could get a good education, and now I should be able to have the kids, the house, the life I’ve worked hard for. Or I just got a promotion; that success should erase some of my worry. We tell these stories to help explain our lives to ourselves, and in order to understand our own trajectories.

But that story is getting harder to tell. While by some measures, members of the middle class now earn more than those of prior generations, the Brookings Institution recently found that one-third of the American middle class still can’t afford essentials. And for the rest, the high costs of child care, housing, medical care and other necessities make their incomes feel paltry. So they are trimming, reining in the lives they once expected for themselves.

Times Opinion asked readers what they can no longer afford, or have given up on having. These are some of their responses.

Two kids become one; a starter home becomes a forever home. People start fretting over whether they can get new winter boots or repair their cars or buy that next pair of contact lenses. These were not the preoccupations most middle-class Americans expected in the scripts of their lives.

Rising costs are often lumped together into a single issue — “affordability” — when they are in fact the result of many converging forces: the increased cost of medical plans; tariffs that suddenly make clothes and computers more expensive; conflict in the Middle East driving up the cost of oil; inflation and high grocery prices.

Most of these factors are out of everyday Americans’ control, which is why more and more feel that they have been forced to make cuts where they can.

There’s an existential crisis that comes with forgoing what you could once afford. Any ease or casualness you may have felt moving through the world? Gone. In its place is a much broader uncertainty, what I call a terra infirma: unstable ground.

What’s lost — good bread, regular haircuts, pets, food delivery, babysitters, fancy face creams — isn’t just stuff. It’s ambition. It’s the luxury of living a life without the constant hum of worry. It’s losing the idea of the future you had in the past. It’s nostalgia for a life that you imagined for yourself that will never come to fruition.

Drop down to Abercrombie and you’re still paying $60 to $70. I’m 32. I can’t put the clothes I want on my own back.”

We didn’t get our expectations about middle-class life from nowhere. After World War II, millions of people — though notably fewer nonwhite households — benefited from generous housing policies, particularly the first G.I. Bill, with its low-interest loans and college grants. Some moved to the suburbs, arriving there by newly built highways. The television and literature of the time gave shape to an image of American middle-class families with homes and cars and full kitchens, from “The Brady Bunch” to the novel “Revolutionary Road.” Extending into the 1990s, mass entertainment — everything from “The Simpsons” to “Growing Pains” — centered on families in the middle.

The middle-class type of the past did not experience the same sticker shock in grocery stores that we do today. Rather, the John Cheever white-collar cliché was one of too much comfort: the ennui of a predictable life.

But it isn’t just pop cultural figures that many Americans today compare themselves to. It’s also their own parents, and the creeping realization that they may never reach the financial security that their mothers and fathers had.

And it’s the people they follow on social media. Being able to see the intimate details of the lives of your friends and neighbors only exaggerates and highlights a sense of competition. People give up food deliveries or their winter vacations, and then open their phones to see an influencer or a friend enjoying both. Oriel FeldmanHall, a Brown University psychology scholar, theorizes that “everything is quantified through likes and shares, so there is no uncertainty about standing.” That sense of omniscience makes it easier to perceive those around us as more secure, and ourselves as less so.

Putting the troubled genie of economic insecurity back in the bottle is difficult. Optimism, or at the very least stability, is lost over time, over the many decisions that people have to make. These stymied small ambitions add up. Americans no longer feel as if the stories of their lives makes sense anymore.

Alissa Quart is the editorial director of the Economic Hardship Reporting Project. She is the author, most recently, of “Bootstrapped: Liberating Ourselves From the American Dream.” Additional reporting by Julie Ho and Tenzin D. Tsagong.
