The Midterms Are Next Week. Here’s Who We Think Will Win.

By New York Times Opinion Contributors

Oct. 26, 2026

In July and again in September, Times Opinion asked six polling and politics experts to look into their crystal balls and predict who would win control of the House and the Senate. Between July and September, they identified a shift in momentum toward the Democrats — a blue wave forming, but not exactly a blue tsunami.

Now, with just about a week to go, the polling suggests an extremely favorable environment for Democrats. When Times Opinion asked the same panel who would win if the elections were held today, five of them said Democrats would win both chambers. (One didn’t want to get his hopes up too high.) These are not scientific computations but educated guesses from people who think a lot about politics. They offer a fairly optimistic picture of what the future holds for Democrats as they struggle to claw back control of Congress.

Which party will control the House and the Senate?

To win a majority, 218 is the magic number in the House. Democrats would need to pick up at least four Senate seats (for a total of 51, including the two independents who caucus with them) to overcome the vice president’s tiebreaking vote and control the chamber.

If the elections were held today …

Mr. Bacon is a staff writer at The New Republic.

My head sees Trump’s incredibly dismal approval ratings, the strong polling for Democrats in Senate races — even in traditionally conservative places like Iowa, Kansas and Texas — and the huge Democratic lead in the House generic ballot. All that points to Democrats taking 235 to 240 House seats and winning a Senate majority. But my heart says I would rather not be disappointed again. I’ll go with the heart and predict a solid Democratic win — but not an epic blowout.

Mr. Odio is a founder of Equis Research.

A 50-50 Senate seemed a stretch earlier this year; now it’s one of the least optimistic Democratic scenarios, and the debate in the House is how big a majority Democrats will get. What’s changed for the G.O.P. is that nothing has changed. Gas prices are still high, the war and scandals continue, the president is doing nothing to help his party, and Republican spending hasn’t moved the needle. That doesn’t mean there isn’t uncertainty. Most major forecasts center on a 51- or 52-seat Democratic majority in the Senate — I come down on 52! — but my model gives any outcome between 49 and 54 seats a roughly 10 percent chance.

Mr. Ruffini is a pollster at Echelon Insights.

Nothing has slowed Democrats’ momentum heading into Election Day, which means they should easily take the House. Redistricting has emptied out the truly competitive battlegrounds that Trump won in 2024 by 10 points or less, but the Democrats’ national advantage now lets them raid the far more numerous prizes beyond that threshold. The Senate is closer to a tossup than most people think. The prospect of another red-state polling error looms — particularly in the Midwest. But even with a normal polling error, states like Alaska, Ohio and Texas are no worse than a tossup. Win two of those three, and Democrats are on track to win the Senate.

Mr. Silver writes the newsletter Silver Bulletin.

I’m taking numbers from the Silver Bulletin model “as is” for the Senate and the House. Republicans seem to be all but giving up on the House, and if something like the Times/Siena generic ballot poll is anywhere close to right (with Democrats favored by 11 percentage points), even Republican gerrymanders that were intended to produce flips might start to fail. That’s a big “if.” But if that’s the national environment we’re seeing, you’ll probably also see some surprising pickups in House districts against Republican candidates who have never faced an environment like this one.

Ms. Swasey writes the newsletter Medium Data.

Despite improved Democratic polling, I’m not moving my Senate forecast up, because of the base-line partisanship of the states in many of the close Senate races. It is looking like a very good year for Democrats, so a state like Ohio, at Trump plus 11.2 in 2024, seems like a safe Democratic pickup. The trio of seats around plus 13 (Alaska, Texas, Iowa) are also all in play. However, the next seat after that is Kansas, at Trump plus 16, followed by South Carolina at plus 18. Democrats are likely to top out in the Alaska-Texas-Iowa zone, winning two of those three states.

Ms. Vavreck is a political scientist at U.C.L.A.

Two forces are shaping these midterms: discontent and calcification. People are frustrated with the status quo, and they blame the president and his party. But at the same time, voters know the parties want to build very different worlds — and they know which world they prefer. Under these conditions, it’s hard to entertain voting for the other side. It makes politics feel stuck, or calcified. To account for both forces, my rule was to examine the close races in each chamber and assume Democrats (1) win the competitive seats they already hold, (2) pick up close seats if they won them recently and (3) otherwise lose.

Pay attention to these numbers

Which 2024 voters will turn out in 2026?

Mr. Bacon is a staff writer at The New Republic.

Midterms have drastically lower turnout than presidential elections. About 159 million people voted in 2020, but only around 108 million did in 2022. So one big question every midterm is “Which party brings back more of its presidential voters?” Right now, it looks like Democrats will. Eighty-seven percent of 2024 Kamala Harris voters say they are backing Democratic candidates this fall, compared with 77 percent of Trump 2024 voters supporting Republican hopefuls, according to a recent Pew survey. If Ken Paxton in Texas or another struggling G.O.P. candidate ends up winning, I would assume that some of that 23 percent of wary Republicans ultimately showed up for their party.

How are friends and families voting?

Mr. Odio is a founder of Equis Research.

Can we gauge how the prevailing winds are pushing less partisan voters? A personal favorite: My firm, Equis, asks voters how their close contacts are planning to vote. It gives us a sense of what they consider normal or popular. I’ll use Hispanic voters: The share reporting that their social circles were mostly supporting Trump went from 19 percent in 2016 to 30 percent in 2020 to 42 percent in 2024, per our best estimates. In our latest poll, we see a reversal, almost to the level of 2016: Just 22 percent of Hispanic voters say most of the people they interact with regularly are supporting Republicans in the midterms.

Trump’s approval rating is even worse than it looks

Mr. Silver writes the newsletter Silver Bulletin.

There’s been a lot of talk about Trump’s approval ratings, but in some ways they’re even worse when you get under the hood. According to our tracking, only about 20 percent of Americans strongly approve of Trump’s performance, while nearly 50 percent strongly disapprove. That’s hardened, vociferous opposition that would typically correlate with turning out to vote. Meanwhile, the deep devotion of Trump’s base has eroded into lukewarm support. His most fervent fans might turn out if Trump were on the ballot. But what about for Roger Marshall or Ken Paxton?

The vanishing American dream

Ms. Vavreck is a political scientist at U.C.L.A.

In 1986, The Wall Street Journal asked people if they thought the American dream was still alive. That year, just 11 percent of Americans said the dream was “not really alive.” An Economist/YouGov poll repeated the question recently. In 2026, the number who think the American dream is not alive jumped to 43 percent — almost half the country thinks it’s dead. In a similar vein, in a 1984 Time magazine poll, 61 percent of the country said they had grown more optimistic about life over the past decade. Today, less than a quarter of the population feels this way. Many Americans no longer believe tomorrow will be better than today. Democrats may get the nod this November, but this is a challenge for both parties — and right now, a bigger one for Republicans.

Gas prices could climb even higher

Ms. Swasey writes the newsletter Medium Data.

Gas prices have driven Trump’s approval rating to historic lows and set the stage for a blue wave. The question now is if late movement in those highly visible prices will turn public sentiment even further. A sudden spike to $5 a gallon, for example, could convince a lot of Republican voters to just stay home. If you’re the incumbent party, you really don’t want the last thing a voter notices before driving to the polls to be the cost of that drive.

Swinging away from Trump

Mr. Ruffini is a pollster at Echelon Insights.

Of the Trump 2024 voters who now disapprove of the job he’s doing, 42 percent rate the cost of living their top issue. Worry about costs is stronger among Trump defectors than it is for any other swing group. The very constituencies that helped get Trump elected again — working-class Latinos and young men — are the ones feeling the affordability crisis the hardest and now swinging away from him the most. If Democrats do win control of Congress, the reason will be simple: affordability concerns exacerbated by high gas prices brought on by the war in Iran.

How confident are you in the polls?

Mr. Bacon is a staff writer at The New Republic.

I’m fairly confident in the polls, in part because we have another indication that Republicans are unusually vulnerable this cycle: The party’s various campaign committees are spending millions of dollars in districts and states that are normally reliably Republican. I am very confident that Democrats will win a House majority and gain Senate seats (though maybe not a majority). When it comes to specific gubernatorial and Senate races, I’m less confident. I’m thinking of female Democratic governors in Ohio, Georgia and Texas. Or in Senate races, Democrats winning seats in Iowa, Kansas and Texas, and in Georgia and North Carolina by close to double digits. I just worry all of those polls are just too blue to be true. Pollsters may not have reached enough conservative-leaning white people for the polls to be accurate.

Mr. Odio is a founder of Equis Research.

What polls offer is perspective, not prophecy. They have taken us as far as they can, in painting the national environment, identifying voters on the move and spotting competitive seats. You look at surveys in a place like Kansas and see Trump voters hanging out in the undecided column. How those voters break, or whether they stay home, can be informed by polling, but ultimately we’re giving educated guesses. That said, I generally assume support for Democrats is overstated among white voters in red areas (and underestimated among Black voters everywhere). The current estimates could also be misreading the makeup of the electorate — for example, if more younger voters turn out than we’d normally expect in a midterm, the polls will have underrated Democrats.

Mr. Ruffini is a pollster at Echelon Insights.

Based on the polls, a good, if not great, Democratic night feels inevitable. But it is possible to read the environment right and the map wrong. Consider 2016: The national polls were not that far off the final margin in the popular vote, but they failed catastrophically in the very states that decided the election. There’s little evidence that the difficulty pollsters have had in contacting rural, working-class voters has been resolved. On the contrary, the problem seems worse this year than ever before. If there’s a recurring pattern, it’s that of overestimating Democrats in red states. And geographic sorting has made these states exactly where Senate control is decided.

Mr. Silver writes the newsletter Silver Bulletin.

Most analysts seem to have little to no confidence in the polling, but maybe they should. Polls in the past two midterms were pretty accurate and unbiased. More important, they may not be projecting anything all that remarkable. “Waves” are very normal in the midterms and almost the default outcome. You might expect a bigger-than-average wave given Trump’s approval numbers, gas prices, Iran and Epstein. I could absolutely see the polls being wrong again, especially in the Midwest, where they have overestimated Democrats in the past. But polling error can run either way; I could imagine Republican turnout collapsing, giving Democrats an even bigger rout.

Ms. Swasey writes the newsletter Medium Data.

I believe the polls are broadly correct, and Republicans hoping for a huge miss are fooling themselves. At the same time, I’m expecting the polls to be slightly too optimistic for Democrats, perhaps by two points, with variation by state. I’m particularly concerned about certain groups — like 2024 nonvoters and vote switchers who in 2026 are voting for a Democrat — responding to polls at high rates. This is difficult to correct as a source of error that could inflate Democratic polling margins, although reputable pollsters are aware of this and working to counter it. There are also state-specific factors that make polling difficult — in Texas, for example, reaching an accurate cross section of the Hispanic population, or in Maine, the high instances of ticket splitting.

Ms. Vavreck is a political scientist at U.C.L.A.

You might look back at recent elections and believe polls have gotten worse and decide to discount them. But that’s a mistake. Polling hasn’t gotten worse — elections have just gotten closer, making it harder to identify winners even when polling is just as accurate. A high-quality poll with a three-point margin of error can typically predict a winner in a race that is determined by seven points. But that same high-quality poll will have a harder time predicting the winner if the race comes down to a point or two, as in many recent elections. The poll didn’t get worse; the race just got harder to predict. Some of these midterm races seem quite close. That’s what the polls are reporting to us, and we should believe them.
