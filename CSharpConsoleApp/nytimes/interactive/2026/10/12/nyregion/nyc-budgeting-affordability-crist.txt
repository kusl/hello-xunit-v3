ARTICLE

How a Book Editor Lives on $74,000 in Carroll Gardens, Brooklyn
Mo Crist has three family goals, but financing them is a tricky proposition in an expensive city like New York.
By John Leland
Published 2026-10-12 09:02 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/10/12/nyregion/nyc-budgeting-affordability-crist.html

How can people possibly afford to live in one of the most expensive cities on the planet? It’s a question New Yorkers hear a lot, often delivered with a mix of awe, pity and confusion.

We surveyed hundreds of New Yorkers about how they spend, splurge and save. We found that many people — rich, poor or somewhere in between — live life as a series of small calculations that add up to one big question: What makes living in New York worth it?

Mo Crist arrived at Barnard College from a small town in western Pennsylvania with plans to become a doctor, then made one of those decisions that come with long-term ramifications.

“I thought if I can’t really hack it in the sciences, maybe I should do something that I really love, which is reading and writing,” Mx. Crist said. “And so I did my undergrad in English.”

Looking back on the career path that followed, they added, “I knew that it wasn’t going to pay the big bucks or help my life in New York to become the ‘Sex and the City’ kind of situation.”

At 30, after eight years of working in book publishing, they have a coveted editing job at Bloomsbury Publishing, a list of writers they enjoy and an apartment in a desirable neighborhood in a city that continues to delight them.

They also have student debt of $15,000, for which they are about $1,000 behind in payments.

And a salary of $74,000.

For Mx. Crist, who did not choose their career for the money, money now plays a large role in the choices they make. They and their partner, Sophie Pugh-Sellers, an assistant editor at Grove Atlantic, share an apartment in Carroll Gardens, Brooklyn, where they pay $2,600 in rent. They were lucky to find it through a friend, Mx. Crist said, but it has its quirks, including slanted floors and occasional “electrical issues.”

“We want three things,” Mx. Crist said. “We want to get married, to have kids and to buy property. And it feels like in our position in the world now, it’s really only possible to do one of those things, and so we’re constantly thinking about how to change that.

“Having kids is the one that feels the most necessary or the most exciting and doable to us. But there’s so much standing in the way financially. I think that we definitely don’t want to stay in this apartment when we have kids.”

No Fast Fashion

Mx. Crist regularly scans the apartment listings online, but even the unpromising places rent for $3,000 or more. “So we both feel stuck in this apartment right now, knowing that we have such a good deal in such a good location, but also knowing that it can’t last forever,”

In the meantime, the couple have lives to live and bills to pay.

At work, Mx. Crist said, many of their peers rely on money from their parents to afford New York City on a book publishing salary. Mx. Crist uses those people as motivation, they said. “I would say that my career has been fueled in large part by resentment and competition, which is not always the healthiest.”

Mx. Crist and their partner get most of their food from the Park Slope Food Co-op, which keeps their combined grocery expenses to around $700 to $750 per month. The couple takes turns paying for restaurant meals. About once a week, the two use the app Too Good to Go, which offers end-of-the-day “surprise bags” of food from restaurants for under $10.

They still go out to dinner twice a month, which they treat as special — i.e., costly — occasions. A recent dinner at a new Indian restaurant in their neighborhood, Malvan — “absolutely amazing,” Mx. Crist said — set the couple back $200 for entrees, a shared appetizer and a drink each. “I’m actively trying to stop myself from going there more often because it was pretty expensive,” they said.

By taste and by necessity, Mx. Crist buys their clothing and furniture used. “I’m firmly against buying any fast fashion, fast furniture kind of stuff,” they said. “And so I didn’t want to go the IKEA route or Wayfair or things like that. It’s so much more exciting and interesting to have a vision for something that you want, and then go out and find it. I find as much joy in the hunt as I do in finding the object.”

The Furniture Hunt

During the Covid pandemic, Mx. Crist’s parents gave them the car they’d driven as a teenager, a hatchback now 15 years old, with 250,000 miles on it. The car made it easier to go to the Hudson Valley for vintage furniture stores or estate sales — and also to get out of the city for hikes.

After a long search Mx. Crist found a midcentury dining room table at Dobbin Street Vintage Co-op in Greenpoint, Brooklyn, for $700, then matched it with four Cesca chairs from the ’70s, designed by Marcel Breuer, which they found at an estate sale for $40 each. “That was a great deal,” they said.

A less successful deal was a $250 coffee table from an antique shop in Beacon, which seemed like a good idea at the time. “I’m pretty good at not regretting my purchases,” they said. “But that’s one that I definitely regret.”

Mx. Crist shares the car with a friend, which keeps expenses down, but repairs still run to $2,000 to $3,000 a year. They recently switched insurance carriers, which cut their shared bill to $100 a month, from $300.

Mx. Crist eschews expensive haircuts or spa treatments, and dresses in a “uniform” from thrift stores like MyUnique Thrift in the Bronx or L Train Vintage in Bushwick, Brooklyn: “Black jeans, T-shirt and some kind of overshirt, which are all thrifted,” they said.

Priced Out of Dogs and Weddings

The couple don’t take vacations, except for an annual week in Cape Cod with Ms. Pugh-Sellers’s family. They had to skip two friends’ out-of-town weddings recently because they could not afford the airfare. They would like to have a dog, but that, also, is too expensive, so they foster dogs on a short-term basis, for a rescue agency that pays for medical care and food.

Asked about splurges, Mx. Crist considered. They are not really the splurging type, they said.

“I think the biggest splurge is living in New York itself, and having access to the New York life,” they said, speaking a few weeks before turning 30. “That feels every month like a splurge.”

Turning 30 has made them think more about the future than they did in their 20s, which has meant thinking more about money, which can be an uncomfortable subject. If they lived in Pittsburgh, they acknowledged, they could do a lot more for less.

“But I don’t think I would be as happy going into 30 if I didn’t live in New York,” they said. “I’m kind of shocked almost daily at how much I love about my life and how much that is dependent on the city that I live in.”
