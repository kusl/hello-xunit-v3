ARTICLE

This Poem Will Hit You Where You Live
Emily Dickinson makes 50 words contain endless possibilities.
By A.O. Scott
Published 2026-10-21 22:41 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/10/21/books/emily-dickinson-poem-i-dwell-in-possibility.html

We are all too aware of the gap between what is and what might be. On one side are the plain facts we are supposed to face, on the other the wild speculations we would rather indulge.

Here’s a short poem by Emily Dickinson that doesn’t so much close or narrow the gap as somersault over it, landing us in a place where the rules of time, space and meaning have been rewritten by the sheer force of an imaginative will, reveling in language’s generative potential.

In 50 words, Dickinson works her way through a single, elaborate metaphor.

The speaker takes on the voice of an architect, a builder — maybe even a real estate broker — as she details the features of her miraculous dwelling.

We’re given a brisk, three-stanza tour of the property. (“Stanza,” it’s worth recalling, is an Italian word for “room.”)

So much light!

You know it’s special from the moment you walk in.

Durable construction.

Plenty of privacy.

And the views!

“Gambrels” is an architectural term, referring to a sturdy, expansive roof design — the kind you might see on a barn.

But in a footnote, Dickinson offers “gables” as an alternative, for those who prefer a narrower, pointier option.

Of course this fantastic house isn’t for sale. It’s the speaker’s home, where she entertains dazzling company and goes about her work.

That work might be writing poems, something Dickinson herself always did by hand.

She copied out her poems and sewed them together into booklets known as fascicles.

(Here are those “Gambrels” and “Gables.”)

This poem is a solid piece of work. Rhymes and near rhymes are scattered throughout.

The lines have a vigorous rhythm, four beats followed by three (sometimes known as “hymnal meter” because of its ubiquity in Protestant sacred music).

And because this is a Dickinson poem, it doesn’t proceed in tidy sing-song fashion. There is syncopation and surprise, a music that is as otherworldly, and as precise, as the metaphor.

Such marvellous craft. Such pleasing carpentry.

Not that Dickinson was a stranger to prose, or to the pleasures of the literal. “They say that ‘home is where the heart is,’” she once wrote in a letter to a friend. “I think it is where the house is, and the adjacent buildings.”

Her own home — where she was born in 1830, died in 1886 and wrote most of her nearly 1,800 poems — was a solid brick Federal-style house in Amherst, Mass. It belonged to her father, a prominent lawyer and politician. During the Civil War, when this poem was bound in its fascicle, the Dickinson household included Emily, her parents and her sister. An adjacent building on the property was home to Emily’s brother; his wife, Susan Huntington Gilbert Dickinson; and their children.

Dickinson is sometimes thought of as a recluse — a devout, solitary shut-in communing with God, her genius and a handful of acquaintances, including a few well-known New England literary figures. But while it’s true that after the mid-1850s she rarely ventured far from the Dickinson homestead, she was nonetheless part of a busy social network.

She was a prolific letter writer, with many warm and familiar relationships, none more intense than the bond with Susan, her neighbor, sister-in-law and intimate lifelong friend. She wrote to Susan in both prose and verse — more than to any other correspondent, including early drafts and “letter poems” — enlisting her as critic, confidante and muse.

Let’s leave the house of prose and slip back into the poem.

If we take the first line as literally as we can, we can say it describes a conditional state of being, a place that is not a place at all.

It’s a more beautiful (and perhaps, in another meaning, a more equitable) situation than the alternative.

In the first stanza of another poem written around the same time, Dickinson associates prose with confinement and punishment.

To be shut up in prose is to be silenced. To be released from it is to find a particular kind of voice, though one that is not without constraints of its own.

The poetic house has a structure, though it doesn’t conform to prosaic building codes. The poem may be made of lines, but it isn’t linear. Its meanings radiate.

The house is full of windows and open to the sky …

… but it’s also solid and secret.

It’s the speaker’s personal domain, but she isn’t always alone in it.

And what exactly happens in this final room?

Is she writing herself a mansion of heavenly bliss? Gabling — or gambreling — her hands into a gesture of prayer? Greeting a religious vision? Enfolding her lovely visitor in an embrace?

Maybe this guest has dropped by from the humdrum place next door, to be welcomed into a private Eden built for two. A fairer home for the fairest one of all.

Anything is possible.
