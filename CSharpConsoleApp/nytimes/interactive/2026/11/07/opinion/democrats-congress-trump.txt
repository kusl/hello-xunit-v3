And Democrats have a self-interested reason to push for more construction: The lack of affordable housing in blue states like California and New York is driving Americans to red states like Arizona and Florida. The influx may have helped turn purple districts blue this year, but it could cost blue states as many as a dozen electoral votes following the next census in 2030.

Congress can’t force local governments to lift limits on housing construction. But the federal government can be very persuasive. What Congress could — and should — do is condition federal transportation funding on progress in housing construction. If state and local governments want federal money for roads and trains, they should be required to demonstrate that they are meeting clear goals for housing construction.

Such a requirement would build on legislation passed this year, which directs the Department of Housing and Urban Development to shift some community development funds from communities with less housing construction to those with more housing construction.

Federal transportation funding is a much larger pot of money. Lawmakers could threaten to withhold a portion of that funding, creating an incentive for states to override local opposition to new housing.

It has worked before. In the 1980s, the federal government sought to push states to prohibit alcohol sales to people under the age of 21. Under the Constitution, that power is reserved to the states, and most states set their minimums between 18 and 21.

In 1984, Congress tied federal highway funding to the minimum drinking age: States that didn’t raise it to 21 faced the loss of up to 10 percent of that funding. By the fall of 1986, only eight states still were holding out. By 1988, every state in the nation prohibited 18-year-olds from buying a six-pack.

The Affordability Act should also end surveillance pricing, where businesses set individualized prices based on personal data including your location, income, browsing history or even cursor movements. Some economists defend the practice on the theory that the wealthy will subsidize the poor — the “Robin Hood” theory — but what we’ve seen so far are just efforts to raise prices whenever possible. A few states have already enacted laws restricting surveillance pricing. Congress could follow suit.

There is a limit to what the pricing laws can do. Ideally, Congress would pair them with more structural measures, like limits on mergers in essential industries, and laws that increase the supply of scarce essentials, like housing.

But pricing laws do have one great virtue: Everyone can see their value. They represent government doing its job: protecting us from economic extraction. That makes opposition so revealing: Whatever politicians may say about affordability, votes on pricing laws show whose side they are on.

Some Democrats have called for a pause or a slowdown in A.I. development, but such policies are vague and difficult to enforce. Worse, they could freeze the current landscape, cementing big A.I. companies’ dominant status. Finally holding Big Tech responsible for their models is a better way to bring about a real, enforceable slowdown. Congress should also create regulatory and research institutions to oversee A.I. companies — with mandates for transparency and data audits — and look at the companies’ potential effects on employment, market concentration and institutions.

Not only is this all doable, but if Democrats moved to finally rein in Big Tech, some Republicans would join them — they know that the first party to distance itself from Big Tech will reap significant electoral rewards. Even better? We might even make sure these remarkable, powerful technologies serve human flourishing and advancement.

Other Democratic proposals could lower the Medicare eligibility age, fund free primary care and introduce a public option on insurance exchanges. These are worthy ideas, but there’s good reason to start with MediKids. Kids, for the most part, are inexpensive to insure. They can’t be criticized for being dependent on others. The preventive care they receive early in life shows up later when they don’t get sick. And interventions delivered at routine pediatrician visits can yield secondary benefits for maternal health, literacy and child safety.

Mr. Kim designed his legislation to be passed during reconciliation, when Democrats won’t need to beat the filibuster to get it to the president’s desk. If Republicans fight it, that opposition will only sharpen the distinction between the parties in advance of 2028: One party wants to lower costs for families, and the other doesn’t.

Americans trust Democrats more than Republicans when it comes to their health care. Democrats can build on that trust if they actually do something with it.

Regularizing appointments would increase the court’s size, but not radically so. If the five justices who are currently 66 or older were to leave the court in the next 20 years, there would be 14 justices at the end of that period — in line with similar high courts around the world.

Future appointments would align with the politics of presidents and the Senate, making the court more representative.

As important, a bigger Supreme Court could enable the justices to hear most cases in smaller panels rather than as a whole body. Not knowing who will decide their case would make litigants less aggressive in bringing extreme legal theories before the justices. Justices may be more willing to recuse themselves from cases for ethics concerns, since another justice could fill in. And a larger court could also have dedicated, rotating panels to resolve ethics complaints and hear cases that would otherwise be relegated to the shadow docket, allowing the justices to write reasoned opinions without taking away from the rest of the court’s business.

The Supreme Court will never be perfect, but by making its appointments regular, Congress can help make it worthier of the constitutional democracy its justices are sworn to defend.

Could such a measure pass? Lawmakers from both parties want to be seen as wary of government waste. Vice President JD Vance heads a task force to root out fraud. Severing the link between campaign cash and vast sums of taxpayer spending should appeal widely. Even if Mr. Trump rejected the measure, it would show that Congress is serious about action.

A contractor contributions ban is not the only change needed, of course. Congress should enact an end to dark money, set up a new anticorruption enforcement agency and more.

There’s a rhythm of reform in American history. Periods of abuse and corruption are often followed by reform and renewal. We last saw such a reform response in the 1970s after Watergate. This time, Democrats must get serious about making changes, or they too may find themselves at risk of being thrown out of office.

Iranian missiles and one-way attack drones have pummeled American bases across the Middle East. Hundreds of buildings are damaged. Eighty-one military aircraft have been damaged or destroyed, along with other equipment, resulting in up to $3.3 billion in losses. At least 23 U.S. service members have died and around 865 others have been wounded.

All of this has come at the hands of a much smaller, less capable military. The military campaign in Iran not only has failed to achieve its stated objectives on a short timeline, as initially promised, but has delivered lasting damage to America’s economic, diplomatic and military standing. The United States is locked in a frozen conflict with no end in sight.

Thousands of U.S. airstrikes have wiped out Iran’s military and industrial base and killed much of its political leadership, including the supreme leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. But the regime still controls 970 pounds of highly enriched uranium, which is the main material needed for a nuclear weapon. And Iran still controls shipping traffic in the Strait of Hormuz, which has driven up the prices of energy and other goods.

Congress has moved to rein in the president before. The House voted to end the Iran war three times this year with war powers resolutions. The Senate, on its 10th attempt, approved one of those resolutions in June, but Republican senators reversed their votes the next day after Mr. Trump berated those who supported the measure. A Democratic majority in Congress can and must reapply the pressure. Mr. Trump unilaterally brought America into this war; now he needs to be forced to end it.

There are plenty of ways for Democrats to go about this. They could legalize some or all of the unauthorized immigrants currently in the United States — as in the “amnesties” of the past. They could give anyone who has had authorized status or held a work permit for a certain number of years a way to apply for a green card. They could update the “registry date”: a provision that allows anyone who has been in the United States since a certain date to simply register for permanent status.

The point is to make immigration law match the way so many Americans — the millions who’ve never had to experience it firsthand — think it already works.

Barring a sudden and uncharacteristic attack of bipartisanship, Mr. Trump won’t enact any of these plans. But that clarifies where, exactly, the opportunity lies for Congress now. Lawmakers need to work to educate voters on the connection between the excesses they’re seeing now and the systemic problems that only legislation can fix — even if it’s a future Congress that ultimately has to fix it.
