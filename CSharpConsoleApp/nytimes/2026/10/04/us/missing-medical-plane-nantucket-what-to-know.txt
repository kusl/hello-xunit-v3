ARTICLE

What to Know About the Medical Plane That Went Missing Near Nantucket
A Gulfstream G100 carrying six people was flying from Bermuda to Boston when it lost contact near the Massachusetts island. A search for survivors has ended.
By Bernard Mokam and Georgia Gee
Published 2026-10-04 17:47 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/04/us/missing-medical-plane-nantucket-what-to-know.html

A medical transport plane lost contact near Nantucket, off the coast Massachusetts, in the early hours of Oct. 3, a Saturday. It prompted a federal and state search to find the aircraft and the six people who were on board that lasted nearly two days.

A debris field was located hours after the plane went missing.

The plane, a Gulfstream G100, lost communication with the Federal Aviation Administration just after 1 a.m. Eastern time. It was traveling from Bermuda, the British territory off the coast of North Carolina, to Boston.

There were four Canadian citizens and two Bermudian nationals on board.

Here’s what we know:

What Happened

The plane took off from Bermuda’s lone airport, on St. David’s Island, at 11:41 p.m. Eastern time on Friday, according to data from the website FlightAware.

Flight data shows that the air ambulance was flying consistently at an altitude of 24,000 feet before it plummeted several thousand feet around 1:05 a.m.

The F.A.A. lost contact with the plane and issued an alert around 1:20 a.m., activating search operations from the Coast Guard, the Massachusetts authorities and the New York Air National Guard.

The missing plane was operated by an Ontario-based medical evacuation company, Latitude Air Ambulance, which flies people to hospitals and offers repatriation services, according to its website.

On board the plane were two Canadian pilots, two Canadian medical staff members and two Bermuda nationals. All are still unaccounted for.

The pilots were reportedly trying to declare an emergency before it lost contact, said Preston Harimon, a spokesman for Nantucket Memorial Airport.

The Search

A continuous search for the six people traveling on the plane ended at sunset on Sunday, the Coast Guard said in a post on X. The six have not been publicly identified.

An interagency effort canvassed more than 2,800 square miles.

“This is a large and demanding search area,” Rear Adm. Matt Lake of the Coast Guard said on Sunday morning.

Rescue teams used fixed-wing aircraft, helicopters, boats and cutters in the search. The Coast Guard also worked with Massachusetts authorities to use an aircraft equipped with sonar to help with the search.

Crews located a debris field about 20 miles south of Nantucket.The authorities did not specify what they recovered in the crash area.

The depth of the water in the area was approximately 80 to 90 feet, Admiral Lake said.

Cause for Concern

Experts agreed that the plane’s reported rate of descent was unusual and concerning.

According to data from FlightAware, the plane was flying consistently at an altitude of 24,000 feet before it rapidly descended 13,000 feet at around 1:05 a.m.

Brad Wheeler, a retired commercial pilot who flew for nearly 40 years and has flown between New York and Bermuda, said the maneuver indicated a “significant problem.”

Mary Schiavo, an aviation lawyer and former inspector general for the Transportation Department, said that the speed adjustment just before the plane’s descent showed the situation had gone awry.

“They were responding to something,” she said.
