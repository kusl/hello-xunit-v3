To the Editor:

Re “Writing Isn’t as Important as We Think,” by Joe Cruz, a professor at Williams College (Opinion guest essay, Oct. 1):

As a Williams alumna, as well as a novelist, I was disappointed with Professor Cruz’s argument that writing isn’t as important to students as most of us think.

Engaging fully in the writing process is, and should remain, a central tenet of liberal arts education. Why? Because it is hard. Perseverance is the point. Guiding a piece of writing from a bad first draft into a polished final product is a difficult but meaningful exercise for the mind.

My favorite professor at Williams, the great American short story writer Jim Shepard, said that drafting a written piece was like combing very tangled hair. It is slow going. You get stuck, trapped in snarls, but with patience and grit, you gradually make the hair smooth.

Now, here is a professor guiding his students to hand over the comb to the robot that will do the pesky detangling work for them.

This is poor advice. Yes, this knotty process helps us master the art of writing. But it also teaches us a much more important lesson: Frustration, pushing ourselves to the edge of failure, is the very thing that crafts us into strong, capable people.

By all means, cede a majority of the writing process to A.I. Choose the easy path. But don’t tell us that the difficult one wasn’t worth much after all.

Lauren Edmondson Herndon, Va.

To the Editor:

Joe Cruz argues in his essay against a claim that nobody is likely to make — that students learn to think only by writing. Surely, all would agree that thinking is also aided by reading, listening and discussing.

The philosophical passage he cites that criticizes writing is from Plato, arguably the greatest of all philosophical writers. You might as well find a line from Shakespeare dismissing the importance of playwriting.

Professor Cruz notes that he doesn’t teach from prepared scripts. That observation, however, hardly implies that writing is unimportant. After all, his professorial role involves extensive writing.

Eventually, Professor Cruz admits that writing is an essential life skill, but he says nothing about how he teaches his students to write effectively. Or does he? He equates writing and archiving. Note, though, that his essay seeking to persuade is written.

Even Abraham Lincoln, one of our greatest orators and literary stylists, read the Gettysburg Address from a written script.

Steven M. Cahn Old Greenwich, Conn. The writer is a professor emeritus of philosophy at the Graduate Center of the City University of New York and the author of “Towards Teaching Philosophy.”

To the Editor:

As a former journalist turned entrepreneur who has long considered writing a strength, I’ve spent a lot of time wondering what A.I. will do to the craft.

I also have a high-achieving 10-year-old with dysgraphia, which for her manifests primarily as difficulty with spelling. Too often, that gets misconstrued by grade-school teachers as an inability to write or construct an organized argument. The result is that she can become reluctant to use her robust vocabulary or fully engage in writing assignments.

I’ve told her that the art of writing is really about formulating an argument, supporting it with evidence and, hopefully, engaging an audience enough so that it wants to listen.

That’s why I was so heartened by this essay and its argument for other ways to demonstrate knowledge. Quite honestly, it also reflects how most of us communicate in the working world. How many of us are regularly writing formal papers in the field or the boardroom to make a point?

Lindsay Stewart New York

To the Editor:

Reading Joe Cruz’s essay made me suspect he lacks knowledge about being an introvert. Besides needing downtime alone to recharge, introverts often need extra time for reflection and to formulate thoughts, and we do this best away from the clamor of others.

In small seminars at Wellesley College, I learned a lot from students batting ideas back and forth, and occasionally I thought quickly enough to get a word in edgewise. But for the thorniest issues, I have always done my best thinking on my own and with paper and pencil, still my best tools.

I have worked as a journalist and written children’s books. And I have led workshops for writers — in mental hospitals, jails and writing programs — where the mantra is “We write to discover what we think.”

Mary Carpenter Washington

To the Editor:

Joe Cruz has a low opinion of writing. The philosophy professor believes that conversation and debate are what best reveal deep thought, whereas writing is really “archiving” — a storage function — or “scaffolding,” a trifling utility. Elsewhere he states that writing “summarizes and compresses our thoughts,” as if it’s a zip-file option. He’s wrong.

Many smart and creative people over the years have observed that writing is thinking. Not separate from or duplicative of, but actually integral to it. As Joan Didion noted in 1976, for example, “I don’t know what I think until I write it down.”

That’s because you can’t efficiently generate, develop, organize, expand or ruminate on your ideas unless you’re committing them to the page. Even people with extraordinary memories require composition to construct deeper thoughts — not because they couldn’t remember three things, or 30, but because the process of writing necessitates the process of thinking.

Articulation requires cogitation. Or to use another metaphor, writing isn’t the frosting atop dessert; it’s the whole cake.

Joe Hayden Memphis

To the Editor:

Joe Cruz’s article on A.I. and writing in the college classroom showed a disappointing level of reasoning on two counts.

First, from my vantage as a longtime dual-enrollment college writing instructor, Professor Cruz seems blithely unaware of the scale at which most college teaching is done. He suggests 45-minute oral exam periods for a final. At the moment, I teach 90 writing students; if I followed his method, conducting finals would take 67 hours!

Professor Cruz does not mention the extent of his teaching schedule, but I suggest he more seriously consider the demands placed on the adjuncts and other typical instructors who do the majority of teaching. His system isn’t tenable when delivering instruction at scale, and the majority of college teachers (maybe not department chairs like him) are delivering at scale.

Second, I’m surprised at the professor’s use of Plato’s “Phaedrus.” Interpreting Plato always requires the reader to see the ironic, dramatic distance between Socrates, the character in Plato’s dialogues, and Plato himself, the thinker and writer. Plato chose writing to capture his thoughts. The Socratic criticism of writing in “Phaedrus” should be considered, but only as a criticism that Socrates’ best student, Plato, chose not to adopt.

William Hughes Charlottesville, Va.

To the Editor:

Joe Cruz cites Plato’s hostility to writing in support of his argument. Of course, Plato didn’t have access to studies documenting important changes in brain functioning associated with writing.

On the other hand, we should have taken his warnings concerning the vulnerability of democracies to demagogues much more seriously.

Thomas W. Allen Chesterfield, Mo. The writer is an emeritus associate professor of education at Washington University in St. Louis.
