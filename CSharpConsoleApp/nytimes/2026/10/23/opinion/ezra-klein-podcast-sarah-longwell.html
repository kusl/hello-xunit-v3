What Hundreds of Focus Groups With Trump Voters Revealed

Oct. 23, 2026

By Ezra Klein

Produced by Annie Galvin and Jack McCordick

This is an edited transcript of “The Ezra Klein Show.” You can listen to the episode wherever you get your podcasts.

Since he took office in 2025, Donald Trump’s net approval rating has collapsed, falling by more than 30 points. As we head into the midterms, Democrats now have the opportunity to do something that seemed unthinkable just a year ago: to take back not just the House but the Senate, possibly even winning in places like Texas and Kansas.

So how did Donald Trump go from winning his first popular vote victory ever to, in less than two years, what now looks like it could be an electoral wipeout for Republicans?

Sarah Longwell is a political consultant and the founder and publisher of The Bulwark. Since the 2016 election, she has conducted hundreds of focus groups with thousands of voters across the political spectrum that have become this really extraordinary source of qualitative evidence of how voters have evolved in the Trump era — particularly voters who had leaned toward Trump but maybe now don’t.

Longwell is also the host of “The Focus Group Podcast” — you can hear some of these groups there — and author of the recent book “How to Eat an Elephant.” She joins me now.

Ezra Klein: Sarah Longwell, welcome to the show.

Sarah Longwell: Thanks for having me. I’m superexcited to be here.

Donald Trump takes office again in January 2025. His approval rating is above 50 percent. He has his biggest electoral victory ever.

He’s now at 36 percent.

I think you’ve probably personally done more focus groups with Trump voters of different flavors and variants than anybody else in America.

So what happened?

It’s easy, actually. This is the easiest question in politics right now. Donald Trump made some very specific choices, and they were his choices, and everyone knows they were his choices.

The first one was when he did all of the unilateral tariffs, didn’t consult Congress and just got into trade wars with a whole bunch of people. And the second was starting the war with Iran.

Voters completely identify those two things with Trump. You can’t say Joe Biden did that. You can’t say it was someone else or Congress. There was nothing else.

The deep state went to war on Iran.

Donald Trump made these choices, and voters know it.

The consequences of both have been to raise prices for the American people at a time when they elected Donald Trump to do one thing: lower prices.

And as a secondary matter, they said: No more stupid wars.

So the voters right now recognize that their life is not getting better and that Donald Trump has broken his promises. That is the easiest way to explain what’s happening right now.

Tariffs were a promise from Donald Trump. He ran for office saying again and again and again: Tariffs on everyone, tariffs in all directions.

I can imagine, if you’re him, you look at your tariff policy as promises kept. So when his supporters tell you what they don’t like about them, what do they say?

Well, the thing about voters is that they are often not interested in the policy minutiae of how one gets to a place. They’re just saying: What are we going to do to lower prices?

The thing that has shocked me the most listening to voters — not even shocked, it’s more the thing that I find has changed my relationship with voters — is that the first thing we do is ask people: How do you think things are going in the country?

And every focus group, every single time, doesn’t matter — whether left, right or center — right now, people say: bad. And then they immediately start talking about the prices of something.

They talk about the prices of gas. They talk about the prices of beef. But they talk about something they did: I walked into the store today, and the beef cost X dollars, and that’s $2 more than it did, and it wasn’t even the good stuff.”

Americans don’t necessarily connect policy rhetoric to the outcome. They live through something, see the outcome, and then they decide how they feel about it.

On that point, about the ground beef, I want to play a clip that your team shared with me from one of the focus groups. This is someone who voted for Trump in 2024.

Archival clip: I grocery shop every week, so I know when prices are going down and up, and I’m like: He’s lying. Nothing has changed — OK, maybe the eggs have gone down, but that’s it.

I’ve never paid $9 for ground beef before — ever — even the good kind. So that is really, really crazy and getting very scary. I really thought that it was going to be about us here at home.

When that woman says to you: “I really thought it was going to be about us here at home” — what did she think?

What did the people who had swung toward Trump in 2024 see in him that they maybe hadn’t seen in 2020?

Well, this is the promise of “America first.”

“America first” has a lot of racist origins. But for the voters, “America first” is an idea that the president is going to prioritize them — Mr. and Mrs. American — and they like that promise. They like the idea of not sending all this money to other places.

I hear versions of this in basically every group that I do, which is: We want to be taken care of. We want money spent on our schools.

It’s interesting watching Abdul El-Sayed, because he’s doing a version of this right now, his own version of “America first.”

Often when he gets asked the question about Israel, he says: I don’t want our money going overseas. I want it spent here in Michigan. I want it spent on our schools, on taking care of our people.

Archival clip of Abdul El-Sayed: I’m opposed to unconditional military aid for any country. That is Israel, yes, but that’s also Egypt, where my family immigrated from. That’s Pakistan, that’s Saudi Arabia, that’s U.A.E. And that’s not because of a focus on what’s happening anywhere except for a focus on what’s happening here. We’ve got communities in my state where the median income is $14,000. And I believe they have a right to their tax dollars.

And that is something that appeals to Americans deeply. They say: Yes, we want to be taken care of.

They feel like, in the big maelstrom of conversations — whether it’s sort of the weird social issues that people get wrapped around the axle about or whether it’s the big geopolitical questions that politicians are always talking about — what they really want to know is: What are you going to do for me?

That disconnect between people whom we talk about as elites — but let’s just say you and I — we should admit this. We do not walk into the grocery store and say: I can’t buy this now because it’s $2 more expensive.

Nobody in Congress has that experience. Nobody who is in a lot of decision-making authority has that experience. And so it is so easy to forget that is the thing everybody wants.

I think this is a problem that ends up afflicting both sides. It happened to the Biden administration during their term, and now it’s happening to Trump, which is: You get elected, and the voters who crucially put you over the top — not your big base of voters who are always going to vote for you but the people who made the difference in the election — they hire you to do often a job that is maybe not the job that you’re most excited about doing.

Voters want lower prices. They’re maybe not as bought-in on climate change questions as Democrats are.

And there are all these people around you who have your ear — who, in fact, do not have problems buying groceries in the grocery store. They have things they are much more excited about doing. So you have this constant pressure to get off the course of what the swing voters wanted from you.

That seems, to me, to be a lot of the Trump story here. He won because inflation was high, but he didn’t really care about inflation. That’s not what gets him out of bed in the morning.

No, what gets Donald Trump out of bed in the morning is Donald Trump’s interests in enriching himself. This goes back to the earlier question: Why do Americans think that Donald Trump is in it for them?

The way that I heard so many voters characterize it is that the process was so slow and sclerotic, and they felt like nothing was changing, and the way that politicians talked all sounded the same.

But mostly, they thought: This guy is a businessman — and that means that he is going to figure it out. Because this is what Donald Trump pitched all the time: I’m going to cut deals for you. I know how this system works. It’s rigged against you. I’m going to unrig it.

And voters really did think, whether it was because of “The Apprentice” — and I cannot tell you how important “The Apprentice” is and was to the lore that sits inside voters ——

Still, by 2024, it was so important?

He lived, in their living rooms, at the top of a boardroom where he made decisions about business, and that is what voters really internalized about Donald Trump.

So when inflation was high, they thought he was going to come in, and in some way that is magic from him — sort of like the Kool-Aid Man busting through the window — and say: Here, I’m going to lower grocery prices on Day 1.

They think he has some secret sauce that allows him to do that. When, of course, it is just a lie. He couldn’t do it.

There are a lot of ways that the people who interpret politics publicly — my job, for instance — end up having a lot of trouble understanding voters.

One of the ways is that we think of the primary divide in the electorate as left or right. But the primary divide in politics, I always think, is between interested and uninterested. The hard-core left and the hard-core right, MAGA, the liberals, resistance, whatever it is, they’re all interested. They’re all following some media source constantly.

But a lot of voters don’t like politics. And they’re not that interested.

So I’d like to hear you talk about the different ways that people who decide elections but do not follow politics that closely in between elections — that difference in the electorate and how they then understand what is happening.

We talk in political science terms. We’re always slicing and dicing the electorate, and we often talk about “low-information voters.” I always think that is an unfair term to people who are just not sick in the head like us, who are not following everything, who don’t let their nervous systems be hijacked on a daily basis by the vicissitudes of political wrangling.

Low neuroticism voters. [Laughs.]

Yeah, that’s right. I think it’s a perfectly acceptable thing to be like: Actually, I’m going to focus on my family and getting my kids out the door, and then I’ve got to go to a job that’s quite demanding. And both parents in the household are working.

One of the things we ask about in the focus groups all the time is: Where do people get their information?

And it is unsatisfying now, because it used to be that people would be pretty clear. They’d be like: I watch Fox News. Or: I watch CNN. I read this newspaper. I read The New York Times. Or: I read my local newspaper.

The majority of people would often say that they watch their local news. But now what people mostly say is: I don’t know where I saw that.

They’re scrolling. They’ve got their favorite cooking influencer, or they’re scrolling on Instagram or TikTok — an algorithm-driven information environment where they mostly want basketball or sports. But then there’s Stephen A. Smith talking about politics.

So this is why, when people said: The left needs its own Joe Rogan — and they tend to reach for people who are actually political pundits. But that’s not what Joe Rogan is.

Joe Rogan is a comedian. I mean, I don’t watch a ton of his content, but I do think there’s a fair amount of getting stoned and figuring out who would win between a shark or a gorilla if they fought each other. And then they have a guest, and the guest says whatever, and Joe Rogan really doesn’t challenge him because he’s not a subject matter expert in much.

That’s about culture. There are cultural people all over the place where politics is injected.

There used to be this phrase: Politics is downstream from culture. I think, increasingly, the overlap between politics and culture combines into something that is a deeply nonpolitical-science term, which we would call a vibe.

And that vibe is what a lot of the low-information voters are getting. Something that washes over them, that is algorithmically injected into the cultural things that they imbibe.

Say more about that. What changes in politics — or what have you watched change in politics — as it has become more, in your language here, vibe oriented?

I think it’s the big difference between news seekers and news receivers.

We used to live in a world where you spent time with the news, and it was a specific time. You sat down at the breakfast table, and you read a newspaper. You watched the evening news. You made time for the news.

I just think that the news receiver means: I didn’t sit down to find something out. Information just washed over me, and there was no mechanism to verify it. I didn’t really sit and spend time with it and ask: Is this true? Does it even matter to me? It just hit me for a second.

It just flashes over them. So they wake up with an opinion without any idea of how it was formed.

I want to talk about something that seems to have begun breaking through. And I don’t really know why it’s finally breaking through now, but it is: corruption.

I want to play a clip from a Trump voter talking about corruption and how it has changed in his view.

Archival clip: I’m a Republican. I’m just saying he’s front and center now. And he’s just blatant with corruption. But we tolerated corruption in a way, or looked the other way. Even their own politicians look the other way at each other. But because it was more minimal or more obscure … every now and then you heard of a big corruption thing here or there, whatever, a pay-for-play.

But now it’s just like: bang! He just does it blatantly. He just says what he wants to do. He’s investing in crypto. His sons are invested in drones, just before the war started with Iran. It’s ridiculous. It’s so obvious and blatant now.

This Fox News poll from October asked voters how concerned they were about key issues. More expressed concern over corruption in government than any other issue. It came out higher in this Fox News poll than prices and health care, the war in Iran and immigration.

Corruption has just been constant in the Trump administration. I was watching corruption in polls through the whole administration, waiting for it to attain salience.

It was just like nothing, nothing, nothing, nothing, nothing — and then suddenly, it’s the top issue.

What broke through?

It’s prices again. Everything comes back to people’s relationship to the economy. And this is a bummer, actually, of an observation, but the American people — especially Trump voters and more low-information voters — would probably let Trump get away with corruption. In fact, we know they would — he was doing a lot of corrupt things in his first term.

People didn’t really care. You couldn’t get them to care about how much he was spending on golf or having big meetings at all of his hotels with the rich.

All the insane crypto stuff during the 2024 election, that was the one I always thought was crazy — the Trump family getting involved in crypto scams and meme coins during the election and the post-electoral period.

They don’t care. But again, it comes down to people’s relationship with their own money. If they thought Trump could do something for them — and you can see this especially with people who are superengaged on the crypto stuff — if they think they can make some money off Trump, they don’t care about his corruption.

There are two reasons that corruption is breaking through right now. The first is, people are suffering economically.

And so all of Trump’s focus on his ballroom and the Reflecting Pool frustrates voters that he is not focused on them — he’s focused on himself.

But also, the enriching himself is becoming difficult to ignore because it’s so out in the open. And when people are not doing well, they’re angrier about it.

The second reason, though, that corruption is now a very high-salience issue, is the same reason that democracy became a high-salience issue.

To some of your work on polarization, there’s a backlash effect that happens when Democrats start accusing Republicans of being corrupt because Donald Trump is doing this brazen corruption.

The thing that happens for Republicans is that they do the “What about…?”

So they say: No, Democrats are corrupt. Or: They’re all corrupt.

Part of what happens is this national conversation where one side is accusing the other of something, and so the other side does: What about…? — and then quickly it becomes a dominant issue.

Democrats were also accusing Republicans of being a threat to democracy after Jan. 6. But Republicans were accusing Democrats of having stolen the election. And pretty soon, democracy is the No. 1 issue.

And Democrats think: Oh, look, everyone cares about democracy. And I’m going: No, no, no, no, no — they don’t care about it the way you want them to care about it.

The Democrat who seemed to really figure out how to make the corruption case against Trump and get people to pay attention to it this cycle is Jon Ossoff.

And Ossoff, in Georgia, is fascinating because that should be, in theory, a swing state. It’s a close state. We don’t even talk about it as a swing state this cycle. It’s like: Is he going to beat his opponent by eight points, by 10 points, by 12 points?

There’s more interest in whether or not he’s going to run for president than in whether he’s going to win in Georgia.

So what worked there?

Well, first of all, Jon Ossoff has the thing. He is a very talented person. He writes those speeches himself. He appears to believe it deeply.

But he understands something about storytelling. I think oftentimes Donald Trump’s scale of corruption, scale of venality, leaves Democrats sputtering to make the longest list that they can make of all of the horrible things. And it leaves people just with their arms up.

Jon Ossoff is taking it slow. He’s taking it one bite at a time, and he is writing to say: Hey, you’ve heard about this.

Archival clip of Jon Ossoff: Now maybe you saw Trump’s so desperate, he’s begging Putin for diesel. But listen to this.

Maybe they have, maybe they haven’t. But he’s inviting them into a story that he’s going to tell where he’s going to give them information about what Donald Trump is doing.

Archival clip of Ossoff: Y’all remember Jared Kushner, the president’s son-in-law, the so-called special envoy responsible for diplomacy with Russia and in the Middle East? Do you remember the other special envoy, Steve Witkoff, who co-founded the president’s crooked crypto business, World Liberty?

OK, so follow me through on this. What Jared and Steve are doing right now is negotiating with Putin to sell billions in Russian oil fields and refineries and gas stations to the president’s friends.

You’ve got Sheikh Tahnoon, the Emirati spy chief, who paid half a billion to the Trump-Witkoff crypto business. Then you’ve got the Al-Khayyat brothers, two Syrians who are financing Jared and Ivanka’s multibillion-dollar Albanian resort project.

And then the third partner, a billionaire megadonor to Trump’s campaign. Oh, and the U.S. government would take a 15 percent stake, by the way. Are you getting the picture?

He’s just very adept at saying: I’m going to walk you through what’s happening.

Archival clip of Ossoff: Amid a global energy crisis, the president’s son-in-law and the president’s business partner are brokering the sale of Russian oil fields to the first family’s friends.

The same friends who have poured hundreds of millions of dollars into Trump family businesses. […]

They are literally the elites they pretend to hate.

More Democrats need to figure out how to talk about this. Because I do think Americans — whether it is all the court cases, all the lawsuits against Donald Trump — when there is scale, they get lost, and it becomes white noise. It’s why Hunter Biden, the specifics of it, Republicans can make a meal out of it.

But the level of Donald Trump’s corruption is so big and so multifaceted and so difficult to follow, that it can become white noise if you don’t lay it out step by step. And that’s what he’s doing.

How much did Jeffrey Epstein matter here?

I think it mattered in terms of setting up Donald Trump to fail his base immediately.

One of Donald Trump’s promises to his voters was that he was going to be the most transparent president in history. And this is something that I think people miss about Trump voters’ relationship with Trump.

They think he’s their guy on the inside. There’s the deep state, there’s the elite cabals, and he is there to take it all and show it to the American people. He’s going to share it with you.

For him not to do that, not come through on that, broke trust with a specific strain of Trump voter, and many of them were swingier kind of voters.

This was one of the early fights I had, both in The Bulwark and other places — people say: Epstein is not going to matter.

And I’m like: Disagree.

I hear voters, especially young men, male voters of Donald Trump, talk about it all the time — how mad they are that he has not made the Epstein files available. It still comes up now.

In that world, Epstein has become this synonym for a corrupt, pedophilic, untouchable elite.

It’s all just him.

I think that’s right. I talk a lot about the schism that I see between the MAGA establishment and the America firsters.

Why do his voters think he did it? I want to play a 2024 Trump voter from one of your focus groups who is saying something that I’ve heard a lot of people say.

Archival clip: My main concern is the war with Iran and everything. I’ve heard that a lot. That’s from one of your groups. How present is that?

It’s incredibly present. I can tell that person is on the younger side, and that really tracks with Trump voters who are under 35 — who have a very different perspective on Israel than people who are older. They believe that Netanyahu walked Trump into this war.

I think there are two ways to have that belief. One is to say that Trump and Netanyahu are allies, and Netanyahu persuaded Trump that this war was a good idea, that the Iranian regime would fall easily, smoothly.

The second one, the way it was said here by this woman — Israel controls President Trump — is different than that.

Yeah.

And what I see happening on the right, though not only on the right, that maybe Trump is holding back a little bit: There’s a real pressure for a story that Israel is in control, and that’s why all these things are going wrong in American politics. In a way that strikes me as actually quite dangerous.

But it feels like it has stuck as a kind of interpretive frame that I suspect is going to outlast Trump.

It has stuck, and it’s a big part of the conversation right now, in both political parties. The left is dealing with this antisemitism problem. There’s this schism between old-school Dems who want to support Israel and a rising tide of folks on the Democratic side who want to re-evaluate America’s relationship with Israel.

There is the anti-Israel faction, but then there’s also just the people who’ve now become convinced, in part because of Iran, who say: I don’t want us sending money to Israel. Or: Why are we doing so much? Why are we so involved in their foreign policy?

So I hear that a lot on the left.

On the right, though, what I hear, especially from younger voters, is closer to a very blatant kind of antisemitism.

And it is related to the idea that Trump can never fail — he can only be failed.

So you have to have a — and I use this term advisedly — “scapegoat” for why Trump is failing. It’s funny because, in part, it’s a dual admission. In this frame, you have to say Trump is so weak and so not smart that he can just be dog walked into a war with Iran.

But that frame really has stuck among younger people on the right, who basically share their counterparts on the left’s suspicion of the role that Israel is playing in American politics right now.

The other main way that the war in Iran has become a bigger problem than just a failed war for Trump is that it’s another example of his raising your prices, doing something you didn’t want him to do, that has been bad for the price at the pump.

It seems like a distraction from what you elected him for. I often think that people connect to issues, sometimes not so much about the issue itself, even if it’s failing, but because it reveals something larger. And to me, the war in Iran seems like it has become the ultimate example of Trump doing his own thing — not your thing.

Yeah. What really activates voters is when an issue hits them in several different places. So the Iran war is both Trump getting into a war in the Middle East — and let me tell you, Americans do not want to be at war in the Middle East. They remember. This activates something deep inside of them: Do not get us into a forever war in the Middle East.

Indeed, Trump ran on that promise and that critique.

It was literally how he ran against Jeb Bush in 2016.

Archival clip of Donald Trump: The war in Iraq: We spent $2 trillion, thousands of lives. We don’t even have it. Iran is taking over Iraq, with the second-largest oil reserves in the world. Obviously, it was a mistake. So George Bush made a mistake. We can make mistakes, but that one was a beauty. We should have never been in Iraq. We have destabilized the Middle East.

So that is one part of it.

Then the other part of it is when voters are experiencing direct negative personal consequences, and that is obviously at the gas pump, and they can connect, right? I listen to voters all the time. They can’t quite pronounce or even conjure the Strait of Hormuz, but they know about it. They know there’s this reason that gas isn’t getting here, and that’s why everything is more expensive.

They also understand that gas prices aren’t just about gas, especially in a place like Iowa.

None of these things are in a vacuum. You get these higher gas prices that are also raising food costs, that are also raising farmers’ costs, that are having an effect on the supply chain and the trucks that drive it. Americans actually understand more of that type of thing than they do a lot of the complexities around health care or other issues.

They understand that prices are rising across the board in a lot of different ways that are squeezing them. They experience it like a cavalcade of prices going up across the board that they feel. And then they look, and they see the war, and it gives them a place to say: That’s the thing doing it.

I think we are in an era of price politics that we were not in for a very long time, because inflation was so low for so many years in America.

Yeah.

If you look at inflation statistics during the Trump era, during Trump’s second term, it’s not actually that high. It’s a little bit higher than would be the ideal trend. It’s more 3 percent than it is 2 percent.

But I do think one of the things that is just going to be very difficult for politicians of all parties for an extended period is that prices went up so much over the past couple of years that now every incremental increase is just agonizing. Because what they are not doing ever is going down.

This feels to me like something that he has not internalized. It’s going to be very interesting to me to see whether Democrats have actually internalized it. Because right now, you need to treat prices as an emergency, and anything you do that seems like it is raising prices is going to infuriate people.

We’re not talking about country club Republicans who want to talk about different insurance markets. No, and I actually really try to speak up for the American people on the issue of immigration sometimes. Because I do think that people think everyone who is a Trump voter is just profoundly racist, and that’s where their desire to see immigration curbed is coming from.

There is a spectrum of Trump voters on this. There are racist people, and there are people who are just like: Deport harder — get every single person out of here. That is not the majority of Trump voters.

The majority of Trump voters make a couple of distinctions. One is the one we’ve talked about, where they say dangerous criminals should go. But the person who’s paying taxes and has been here for 25 years, who lives down the street — leave that guy alone. That’s part of it.

The other distinction they make, though — and I hear this all the time from voters — is a sense of fairness. They say: I want immigrants to come here, but I want them to come — and this is the key phrase you hear — “the right way.”
