What Hundreds of Focus Groups With Trump Voters Revealed
Sarah Longwell has conducted hundreds of focus groups with Trump voters, and many of them are feeling betrayed.
By Ezra Klein, Annie Galvin and Jack McCordick
Published 2026-10-23 09:02 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/23/opinion/ezra-klein-podcast-sarah-longwell.html

Since he took office in 2025, Donald Trump’s net approval rating has collapsed, falling by more than 30 points. As we head into the midterms, Democrats now have the opportunity to do something that seemed unthinkable just a year ago: to take back not just the House but the Senate, possibly even winning in places like Texas and Kansas.

So how did Donald Trump go from winning his first popular vote victory ever to, in less than two years, what now looks like it could be an electoral wipeout for Republicans?

Sarah Longwell is a political consultant and the founder and publisher of The Bulwark. Since the 2016 election, she has conducted hundreds of focus groups with thousands of voters across the political spectrum that have become this really extraordinary source of qualitative evidence of how voters have evolved in the Trump era — particularly voters who had leaned toward Trump but maybe now don’t.

Longwell is also the host of “The Focus Group Podcast” — you can hear some of these groups there — and author of the recent book “How to Eat an Elephant.” She joins me now.

Ezra Klein: Sarah Longwell, welcome to the show.

Sarah Longwell: Thanks for having me. I’m superexcited to be here.

Donald Trump takes office again in January 2025. His approval rating is above 50 percent. He has his biggest electoral victory ever.

He’s now at 36 percent.

I think you’ve probably personally done more focus groups with Trump voters of different flavors and variants than anybody else in America.

So what happened?

It’s easy, actually. This is the easiest question in politics right now. Donald Trump made some very specific choices, and they were his choices, and everyone knows they were his choices.

The first one was when he did all of the unilateral tariffs, didn’t consult Congress and just got into trade wars with a whole bunch of people. And the second was starting the war with Iran.

Voters completely identify those two things with Trump. You can’t say Joe Biden did that. You can’t say it was someone else or Congress. There was nothing else.

The deep state went to war on Iran.

Donald Trump made these choices, and voters know it.

The consequences of both have been to raise prices for the American people at a time when they elected Donald Trump to do one thing: lower prices.

And as a secondary matter, they said: No more stupid wars.

So the voters right now recognize that their life is not getting better and that Donald Trump has broken his promises. That is the easiest way to explain what’s happening right now.

Tariffs were a promise from Donald Trump. He ran for office saying again and again and again: Tariffs on everyone, tariffs in all directions.

Well, the thing about voters is that they are often not interested in the policy minutiae of how one gets to a place. They’re just saying: What are we going to do to lower prices?

The thing that has shocked me the most listening to voters — not even shocked, it’s more the thing that I find has changed my relationship with voters — is that the first thing we do is ask people: How do you think things are going in the country?

And every focus group, every single time, doesn’t matter — whether left, right or center — right now, people say: bad. And then they immediately start talking about the prices of something.

They talk about the prices of gas. They talk about the prices of beef. But they talk about something they did: I walked into the store today, and the beef cost X dollars, and that’s $2 more than it did, and it wasn’t even the good stuff.”

Americans don’t necessarily connect policy rhetoric to the outcome. They live through something, see the outcome, and then they decide how they feel about it.

One of the things we ask about in the focus groups all the time is: Where do people get their information?

And it is unsatisfying now, because it used to be that people would be pretty clear. They’d be like: I watch Fox News. Or: I watch CNN. I read this newspaper. I read The New York Times. Or: I read my local newspaper.

The majority of people would often say that they watch their local news. But now what people mostly say is: I don’t know where I saw that.

They’re scrolling. They’ve got their favorite cooking influencer, or they’re scrolling on Instagram or TikTok — an algorithm-driven information environment where they mostly want basketball or sports. But then there’s Stephen A. Smith talking about politics.

And that vibe is what a lot of the low-information voters are getting. Something that washes over them, that is algorithmically injected into the cultural things that they imbibe.

Say more about that. What changes in politics — or what have you watched change in politics — as it has become more, in your language here, vibe oriented?

I think it’s the big difference between news seekers and news receivers.

We used to live in a world where you spent time with the news, and it was a specific time. You sat down at the breakfast table, and you read a newspaper. You watched the evening news. You made time for the news.

I just think that the news receiver means: I didn’t sit down to find something out. Information just washed over me, and there was no mechanism to verify it. I didn’t really sit and spend time with it and ask: Is this true? Does it even matter to me? It just hit me for a second.

It just flashes over them. So they wake up with an opinion without any idea of how it was formed.
