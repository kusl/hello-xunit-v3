North Korea’s Crimes

To the Editor:

Re “North Korea’s New Clout Has a Human Cost” (front page, Sept. 20):

It’s not just overseas workers who suffer from the abuses of the Pyongyang government.

A United Nations commission of inquiry concluded over a decade ago that North Korea’s government perpetrates crimes against humanity against its own people, including “extermination, murder, enslavement, torture, imprisonment, rape, forced abortions and other sexual violence.” Amnesty International notes that the threat of execution is persistent, including for the “crime” of watching South Korean television.

North Korea’s government provides none of the freedoms promised by the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. The country is a vast constellation of police stations, detention facilities, prisons and labor camps into which countless people disappear to be beaten, tortured, starved and killed. Neighbors are expected to monitor and report on neighbors under threat of further deprivation.

Chronic food insecurity and shortages of hospital supplies, medicine and fuel for heat severely affect the most vulnerable: children, the elderly and people with disabilities. Meanwhile, the authorities have devoted huge resources to maintain a standing army of 1.3 million, fourth largest in the world, and to develop nuclear weapons and ballistic missile systems.

North Korea cannot thrive if its authorities sacrifice the rights, well-being and lives of its people. The authorities there must realize that impunity does not last forever.

Jack Rendler Calistoga, Calif. The writer is the North Korea country specialist for Amnesty International U.S.A.

Venezuela’s Gold and Indigenous Peoples

To the Editor:

“Venezuelan Gold Chased by Trump Sits Untouched” (front page, Sept. 29) details growing U.S. interest in Venezuela’s gold reserves. Yet largely missing from the conversation are the Indigenous peoples whose territories are increasingly threatened by mining and who could bear the greatest costs of a new gold rush.

For communities in southern Venezuela, gold mining is not simply an economic or geopolitical issue. Illegal mining has already contributed to deforestation, mercury contamination, violence, disease and incursions into Indigenous territories.

The risks are particularly severe for uncontacted Indigenous peoples, for whom increased mining and the roads and infrastructure that accompany it can have devastating consequences.

Expanding or formalizing Venezuela’s gold industry will not automatically eliminate these threats. Any new investment must include meaningful protections for Indigenous territories and respect Indigenous peoples’ right to determine what happens on their lands.

As the United States and investors pursue Venezuela’s gold, Indigenous communities should not be forced to bear the cost.

Keith Slack Washington The writer is the director of Survival International USA.

Feeding the Birds

To the Editor:

Re “Maybe Britons Should Leave the Birds Alone,” by Lia Leendertz (Opinion guest essay, Sept. 30):

As an ornithologist, I’m often asked, “Is it OK to feed birds?” My answer is yes, so long as you do so responsibly. A bird feeder is not just a decorative feature of our gardens. It is an invitation to our feathered friends. Come eat here.

Just as we would never serve our friends dinner on dirty plates, we should not offer birds seed in dirty feeders. It is important to clean feeders regularly. It is also important to provide a safe space for dining. Keep your cats indoors, and take measures to make glass windows more visible to birds.

Of course, bird feeders are not the only way — nor are they the best way — to attract birds to your garden. The best thing you can do to support local birds is to build back the habitat birds have lost.

Plant native plants. Leave your leaves, if you can. Construct a brush pile. Downsize your lawn. Stop using pesticides. Let it be a little messy; let it be a little wild.

If you build it, I promise the birds will still come.

Olivia V. Sanderfoot Ithaca, N.Y. The writer leads Project FeederWatch at the Cornell Lab of Ornithology.
