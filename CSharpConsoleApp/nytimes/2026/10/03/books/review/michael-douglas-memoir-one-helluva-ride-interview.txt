ARTICLE

Ignore the Profanity. Could Michael Douglas Actually Be Mellowing?
At 82, the famously roguish leading man has written a memoir, “One Helluva Ride,” that’s as candid and intimate as it is feisty.
By Elisabeth Egan
Published 2026-10-03 09:00 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/03/books/review/michael-douglas-memoir-one-helluva-ride-interview.html

His hair is more salt than pepper. His forehead has deep furrows. His brows have a mind of their own.

But Michael Douglas’s eyes are as they always were, close set and blue, with the piercing gaze he brought to his most famous line, “Greed is good,” as Gordon Gekko in “Wall Street.”

Once a poster boy for ’80s excess, Douglas, 82, has returned to his hippie roots, at least ideologically.

“Obsessions aren’t healthy. We have to rekindle a philosophy about trying to help each other,” he said, leaning forward as if to emphasize the point. “Train yourself to help others.”

We were at Estiatorio Milos, a cavernous Greek restaurant in Midtown Manhattan, to discuss Douglas’s sprawling memoir, “One Helluva Ride,” coming out from Grand Central on Oct. 6. The place wasn’t yet open to the lunch rush, but members of the wait staff bustled around, refilling our water glasses at attentive intervals, and Douglas was polite, almost courtly, with each one. This might seem like a low bar, but it says something about a person.

“I’ve been working my ass off for 55 years,” Douglas said, glancing proudly at his 496-page book, which had a seat of its own at our table. “This is sort of a culmination.”

Some celebrity memoirs skate over the good stuff, offering a highlight reel more than an in-depth feature. “One Helluva Ride” is a blockbuster — intimate and uncensored, worthy of a bucket of popcorn with extra butter.

Aside from Clint Eastwood, Robert Redford and maybe George Clooney in a decade or two, few actor-producers have had Douglas’s staying power.

His memoir covers it all: six decades of filmmaking (including Oscars for “Wall Street” and “One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest,” which he produced with Saul Zaentz); two generations of Hollywood royalty (Douglas’s father, Kirk, paved the way); two marriages (one turbulent, the second, to Catherine Zeta-Jones, “a joy and a comfort,” he said); many, many romantic dalliances (with Brenda Vaccaro, Jeanne Tripplehorn, Kathleen Turner, the Times opinion writer Maureen Dowd, an Israeli soldier he met on the side of the road, the list goes on); lots of drugs (including suitcases of “grass” that Douglas sold to pay for his last two years at the University of California, Santa Barbara, and a memorable bender after which he woke up with a cocaine straw still up his nose); and parenthood, recounted with refreshing candor.

Douglas’s older son, Cameron, spent seven years in prison on drug charges, including two in solitary confinement. Douglas’s descriptions of that time — and of Cameron’s battles with heroin addiction — are among the most powerful in the book, which is as much a family story as it is a time capsule of a certain era.

“I wasn’t around a lot, so I have my own guilt with Cameron,” Douglas said, referring to the difficult years when he and his first wife, Diandra Luker, lived on opposite coasts.

A few minutes later, he answered a FaceTime call from his 23-year-old daughter, Carys Zeta Douglas, who was filming a movie in the Canary Islands. Their exchange was quick and endearing — “Angel? Hi, sweetheart,” Douglas said, his face alight with a blindingly white smile. But after he hung up with a promise to call back later, he briskly returned to the subject at hand: a 34-year-old tabloid rumor that, in 1992, he went to rehab for sex addiction. In fact, he was at Sierra Tucson in Arizona to kick a drug habit and he still resents being sold out by fellow patients, whom he spoke of with contempt. Ditto the paparazzi who followed his children to school when he had cancer.

“I was really pissed off,” Douglas said.

There’s a lot of that in “One Helluva Ride” — Douglas gets irritated with Oliver Stone on the set of “Wall Street”; with Steven Soderbergh, when he almost loses his part in “Traffic” to Harrison Ford; with Kathleen Turner, when she hires Roy Cohn to represent her in a lawsuit over “The Jewel of the Nile”; and so forth.

The book contains 22 F-bombs and an impressive tally of lesser expletives. It’s not bashful about sex, either — no surprise, when you consider that Douglas starred opposite Sharon Stone in the erotic thriller “Basic Instinct.” (And let’s not forget “Fatal Attraction,” with its elevator scene and its kitchen-table-to-countertop-to-bedroom scene.) There’s plenty of nudity in the memoir, too, both onscreen and off; apparently Douglas did without clothing while living in a commune during college. His father, visiting at the peak of his fame, was amused to learn that the place had no toilet.

In person, Douglas appeared more polished than he has in recent Instagram videos, where his hair often has a mad scientist vibe à la Doc Brown in “Back to the Future.” He wore a meticulously pressed blue shirt and his hair was combed back in a way that can only be described as power broker chic. Thanks to a walnut-size tumor he had removed from his tongue 16 years ago, his voice is slightly altered — it’s as gravelly as ever, but now sounds as though he’s talking around a wad of Bubble Yum.

Douglas’s Stage 4 throat cancer diagnosis led to a reckoning with mortality — “I wondered if my luck had run out,” he writes — and a new appreciation for the friends who stuck around.

“There’s something about cancer, it makes people not want to be near you,” he said. “I was surprised by how few people checked in. They don’t know what to say.”

One person he heard from regularly was Shane Salerno, a screenwriter he met in the early 2000s, who fired off a series of missives ribbing Douglas about being an unserious television actor and living in his father’s shadow, both charges that had dogged him early in his career.

“I tried to remind Michael that every time someone had told him he couldn’t do something he had proven them wrong,” Salerno said in an interview.

After a few months of these irreverent notes, Douglas finally fired back: “Keep ’em coming, motherfucker!!!!”

He said: “I was really touched. It meant a lot.”

When Salerno first broached the idea of collaborating on a memoir a decade ago, Douglas declined. He was busy; the time wasn’t right. But his father had written a memoir, as had his mother and his son Cameron — so maybe it was a family tradition? Then, after playing Benjamin Franklin in the Apple TV+ series “Franklin” in 2024, Douglas took a break from acting and started to think about his legacy.

When asked how he sees that legacy taking shape, he gave a self-aware shrug.

“Pretty good batting average,” Douglas said. “A reflection of my times. A reflection of what was going on.”

He and Salerno teamed up with Michael Fleming Jr., a writer for Deadline and Variety who had interviewed Douglas on the eve of a Hollywood Reporter story that included allegations from Susan Braudy, a former employee who said that Douglas had mistreated her and subjected her to sexual harassment decades earlier. Douglas acknowledged using raunchy language in front of Braudy but denied her claims that he had masturbated in front of her and blackballed her in the industry. He does not address the issue in his memoir.

In our interview, Douglas said that Fleming’s article helped him “get out in front of it.”

The three worked on the memoir in hotel rooms, on the phone and on hundreds of Zoom calls. Salerno and Fleming conducted more than 40 interviews, with Zeta-Jones, Douglas’s children, Danny DeVito, Glenn Close and a bevy of other sources. They selected 10 movies they wanted to focus on alongside Douglas’s evolution from son of a star to a leading man in his own right, with plenty of struggle along the way.

He had “four or five turkeys” before he really got started as an actor. After a college production of “Much Ado About Nothing,” he recalls in the book, his father said: “Son, you were terrible. Absolutely terrible.”

In those days, Douglas suffered from debilitating stage fright. He said he’d been told early on: “The camera can tell when you’re lying. So there was a whole period of method acting, of just trying to rip the skin off my face and get down to the truth. Then one day it dawned on me: Acting is all about lying. You become a better liar to convince an audience. Once I realized that, it changed the whole picture.”

“One Helluva Ride” is packed with vintage grudges and escapades — the time Douglas passed over his father for the lead in “One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest,” a decision that took years to smooth over; the time Luker walked in on Douglas with an unnamed paramour in a room at the Beverly Wilshire; the time he snorted cocaine through a rolled-up dollar bill with O.J. Simpson at a “Saturday Night Live” after-after-party; the time he insulted Julia Roberts in lewd terms at a Golden Globes party; the time Debra Winger bit his arm, costing herself the lead in “Romancing the Stone.”

The role went to Kathleen Turner, with whom Douglas had an on-set romance.

“There will be some intimate relationships revealed here for the first time,” Douglas writes in the prologue to his memoir. “I called each woman involved and asked for their permission to share our story.”

DeVito, who also appeared in “Romancing the Stone” and its sequel, “The Jewel of the Nile,” said in an interview that he hadn’t noticed the fireworks between Turner and Douglas at the time, but said there was no denying Douglas’s star power.

“He had a mystique,” DeVito said. “He was a babe magnet. But he was also a big softy and a sweet guy.”

DeVito fondly recalled their days of “chasing windmills” and behaving “like a couple of wackos.”

During our interview, Douglas was more a romantic than a rogue, repeatedly steering the conversation back to his family, to whom the memoir is dedicated.

“I started realizing how important family really is,” he said. “This is what holds us together.”

He spoke lovingly of his mother, Diana Dill, a Bermudian actor who completed the Sunday Times crossword puzzle in ink, and of his father.

“My love for him grew as we both aged,” Douglas said. “Between Dad and I, we had 80 years in the business, 160 movies. He’d say, ‘Michael, if I knew how successful you were going to be I would have been nicer.’”

But Douglas got misty-eyed when he spoke of Bill Darrid, who became his stepfather when Douglas was 12.

“He was the first person who listened,” Douglas said. “I remember the shock that he was actually listening to what I had to say.”

Douglas talked about the rage he inherited from his father: “I used to count on anger as a false sense of energy. Anger gives you energy,” he said. But age and success and a lasting marriage have tempered all that: “I have really found much more joy and peace with Catherine all these years we’ve been together and less involved in the business.”

He’s dismayed about the state of the world — our fractured politics, our sense of disconnection from the public good that was an organizing principle for him in the ’60s, even as a child of privilege.

“This is the most difficult time I can remember in my lifetime,” Douglas said, sounding more like a worried grandfather than a guy who used to party with Jack Nicholson and Jann Wenner. “I’m an optimist and I really try to be hopeful, but I’m deeply scared.”

He mentioned the Jewish concept of tikkun olam, or “repairing the world.”

He said: “Something’s got to give. There’s too much focus on money.”

Many years ago, Douglas was asked to consider a run for California governor — he’s played several politicians onscreen, including the president — but he declined.

“In terms of skeletons in the closet,” he said, “particularly the drug areas going back 20 years, it probably was not a good idea.”

Nevertheless, Douglas has strong opinions: “We should be taking a fraction of these military budgets and using them for positive good. Trying to bring back the Peace Corps. Trying to improve inequality in terms of affordability and education.”

Then he seemed to catch himself.

“I love this country,” Douglas said. “I feel very patriotic. But we’ve got to wake up. We just have to wake up. This is not good. And I guess I’m speaking like a cancer survivor. But beautiful weather anyway, huh?”
