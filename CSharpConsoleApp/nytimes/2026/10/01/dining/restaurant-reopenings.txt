ARTICLE

Three Restaurants That Came Back From the Dead
What to eat at the reanimated locations of Carnegie Deli, Sam’s Restaurant and Ugly Baby.
By Luke Fortney
Published 2026-10-01 20:08 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/01/dining/restaurant-reopenings.html

Growing up, I was a sucker for zombies. So, when one of my favorite video games, World of Warcraft, announced a highly anticipated reboot this fall, I quickly loaded in and created a character as my favorite fantasy race: the rotting, slack-jawed undead paladin. (How did I spend last weekend’s Nor’easter? Now you know.)

But this is bigger than role playing games. Some of my favorite restaurants right now are coming back from the dead. After their leases expire or original owners retire, they return years later under the same name and different circumstances. They aren’t exactly as you remember them — check out the spine on my paladin, Snurp — but there are enough old bones to know exactly where you are. This week, we’re headed to three new old restaurants, recently brought back to life.

Home of the big pickle

Sarri Harper always thought Carnegie Deli would mount a comeback. (Why else would she keep its giant pickle and hundreds of signed celebrity photographs in a storage unit all this time?) Still, Ms. Harper, whose grandfather took over the deli in 1976, never imagined that it would return at the address of its longtime rival, Stage Deli, which closed in 2012, concluding a 75-year pastrami war.

The sandwiches are as tall as ever. I ordered the Broadway Danny Rose (formerly known as the Woody Allen) and counted 25 folds of corned beef and 15 slices of rich, peppery pastrami. A 42-layer sandwich, if you include the rye bread. (A fork and knife is required. So, perhaps, is a Lipitor.) Everything else is similarly oversized. The full sour pickle above the register measures just over 3 feet long, end to end; the carrot cake is a gravity-defying tower of cream cheese frosting; and of course, there’s the vast dining room itself. That last part made it easy to slip in for lunch on a recent weekday without a wait, despite the terrifying lines I had seen online.

834 Seventh Avenue (West 54th Street), Midtown, Manhattan

Magma flows at Ugly Baby

You could close your eyes at the original Ugly Baby and know exactly where you are. The clanging pots in the open kitchen. The carbonated snap of beer cans cracking open. The fog of bird’s-eye chile in the air … and eventually, on your lips, tongue and throat. Anywhere but the eyes, hopefully, since the peppers burned like magma.

It went on like this, night after night, until the winter of 2024, when the chef Sirichai Sreparplarn closed the restaurant. He was burned out, having worked, he said, from 6 a.m. to 4 a.m., seven days a week, for seven years.

In August, Mr. Sreparplarn reopened Ugly Baby in Williamsburg, spitting distance from the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway. Everything seemed to be in order when I stopped by during the opening week. Red chiles glowed on every plate. Diners dabbed their sweaty foreheads. Even Bond, a server from the original restaurant, had returned. (He was hurrying around the dining room, fighting fires with a water pitcher, just like I remembered.)

What to eat? A completely new menu that pairs strawberries with sticky fish sauce, hanger steak with mild beef tripe, and bony sea bass with fermented bamboo shoots in sour curry. The food is still thrillingly spicy, and I was glad to see that the charmingly long beer list had survived the move. As any true Ugly Baby fan knows, a sour coconut-lime beer is the only way to fight the flames.

364 Grand Street (Marcy Avenue), Williamsburg, Brooklyn

Play it again, Sam’s

Shame on me for never visiting the original Sam’s in Carroll Gardens before it closed in the spring of 2025. Not that I didn’t have plenty of chances. The Italian stalwart — clams, chops and unexplainable art choices — had been open since 1930, surviving nearly a century of neighborhood change and the periodic rumor about the retirement of its owner Louis Migliaccio, who was born above the restaurant.

Before Mr. Migliaccio finally retired last year, Georgia Fulton, a server at the nearby Long Island Bar, took up an apprenticeship at the restaurant, converting shorthand measurements into usable recipes. With his blessing, she reopened Sam’s in September. The veal Parm is still glorious, crusted in a thick layer of cheese with your choice of baked ziti or spaghetti on the side. And the pizza — some argue Sam’s was never a restaurant, but a pizzeria — has been gently reinvigorated by Andrew Halitski, a talented chef who I met at La Rose Pizza in Cobble Hill (now closed, sadly) while scouting for our New York pizza list. The flour is better and the topping options have changed, but the goal, Ms. Fulton said, was for everything to feel “in essence, the same.” Even the appetizer of celery and olives survived. In the past, it called for a whole head of undressed celery. Under Ms. Fulton, you get a few trim stalks and a snacky portion of pitted drupes. “It looked like a painting to me,” she said. “I had to recreate it.”

238 Court Street (Baltic Street), Carroll Gardens, Brooklyn
