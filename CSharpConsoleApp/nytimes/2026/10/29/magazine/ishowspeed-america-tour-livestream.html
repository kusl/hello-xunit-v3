How IShowSpeed Became the Most Influential Streamer in the World

Livestreaming is unstoppably ascendant — and often boring. IShowSpeed became the king of the form by bringing his act into the real world.

By Jon Caramanica

Jon Caramanica traveled with Speed to seven states over the past 18 months, including four days and three nights on the Speed Does America tour.

Oct. 29, 2026

With seven minutes left in the second quarter of their fall 2025 homecoming game, the Custer High Wildcats of Custer, S.D. — population 2,341 — were down 36-0 to the St. Thomas More Cavaliers. If they gave up another 14 points, the game would be over by league mercy rules.

In the back of an S.U.V. speeding down Route 16A, Ames Ward, a senior producer for the livestream superstar IShowSpeed, was counting the minutes, too. He was exchanging direct messages with the Custer High media class’s Instagram account, trying to gauge whether Speed’s caravan might make it to the game before it ended.

It was Day 23 of the Speed Does America bus tour: a month straight on the road, finding adventures and creating chaos while livestreaming on YouTube 24 hours a day. At the beginning of the tour, Speed had 36 million subscribers; at any given time, tens if not hundreds of thousands of fans were tuned in. That meant a lot of planning and a lot of improvisation — any way to turn a small moment into a potentially viral event.

Streaming has become one of the dominant modes of entertainment on the internet, with hundreds of thousands of people worldwide broadcasting their lives in real time via platforms like Twitch, Kick and YouTube. These performances are largely lo-fi affairs, a monitor-mounted camera trained on a person gaming or watching soccer or playing poker. A set of open portals into long-form thumb twiddling. Even many of the biggest livestream personalities — the antic hangout host Kai Cenat, the professional gamer Ninja, the artist and producer PlaqueBoyMax — broadcast primarily from their desks.

Speed has taken one of the most limp modes of entertainment and put it in Technicolor, bringing his cameras and his curiosity out into the world on a series of dynamically disorderly quests all over the globe. In the last two years, he has visited over 100 countries across five continents — Chile, Zambia, New Zealand, Ethiopia, Turkey, Uruguay and more. Trailed by a rotation of camerapeople and flanked by security guards, he moves around the world and invents his entertainment from the raw cloth of human interaction.

His streams function on multiple levels: travelogue, durational art piece, technological stunt, family sitcom, brandable marketing opportunity. Each stream — some last a few hours, some last days — is a one-man play, with improvisational work contributed by umpteen secondary characters who flock from their homes to see him, sometimes by the thousands, or watch from home, sometimes up to a million at a time. In Speed’s hands, livestreaming multiplies the spontaneity of social media by the intimacy of podcasting: The viewer is thereby transformed from consumer to sidekick, a remora hitching along for an unpredictable ride.

This dynamic has made Speed, whose real name is Darren Watkins Jr., the most effective translator of online passions to the real world — a star who literally jumps out of the screen and lands in your hometown, ready for action. It has also made him the top streamer on YouTube and arguably the most popular livestreamer in the world. In January, on his 21st birthday, he notched his 50 millionth subscriber on YouTube, while streaming in Lagos, Nigeria. That number now tops 62 million. This summer, he was the only streamer granted permission to broadcast live from the World Cup, a testament to his global reach; his roisterous, hyperenthused streams from the matches collectively generated over a billion views.

By the time Speed’s caravan arrived at Wildcat Field, two things had already happened: The game ended in a 50-0 mercy-rule blowout, and everyone under 18 heard that Speed was on his way. Speed jumped out of his vehicle and was immediately swarmed by hundreds of people with phones, who followed him as he marched down a hill from the parking lot to the field, along with a dozen of his camerapeople and handlers and his younger half brother, Jamal.

He made his way to the Custer locker room and was confronted with around a dozen dejected players. The odor was almost physical. “Be careful of teen nudity,” Ward cautioned one of the camerapeople. (There is often no broadcast delay in livestreaming.)

The Wildcats hadn’t anticipated becoming supporting actors on the IShowSpeed stream, but they proved game collaborators. For five minutes, Speed worked his way around the room, alternately encouraging — “Y’all big, y’all strong! Get in the weight room!” — and needling the losing team.

“I drove 30 minutes to see y’all play,” he shouted, “and I didn’t even get to see y’all play!”

He zeroed in on the smallest player in the room, who was giggling at the speech. “I was a freshman just like you — I was laughing in the locker room,” Speed said. “But see these people right here. They’re seniors, bro. This they last year.” Then he learned the pipsqueak was, of all things, a linebacker. Speed winced and walked him over to the team’s coach, issuing stern orders: “He should be at D.B. or safety. Coach, this is not a linebacker body.” Before leaving, he promised the team that if it won all of the season’s remaining games, he would return.

And then he left, on to the next set piece.

A Speed “IRL stream” — as he refers to his roving broadcasts — consists of dozens, if not hundreds, of such largely impromptu interactions. His distinctive gift is to shrink the gaps among him, the people he encounters in his travels and the people watching at home. This easy benevolence has made him a global superstar at 21 and a template for what livestreaming, still a relatively young medium, can become.

“I don’t know if ‘relatable’ is the correct word for him,” said MrBeast, who with over 500 million subscribers has become the defining figure of the YouTube era, thanks to his highly choreographed challenge videos, almost the opposite of Speed’s improvisational dazzle. “I mean, he’s relatable in some regards, but he’s also superhuman.”

He continued, “For some people, it’s like being able to watch a cartoon live, you know what I mean?”

A viewer’s attention is precious and fleeting, and a great livestreamer will do anything to keep it. For Speed, that means living in a perpetual state that mingles childlike wonder with teenage mischief. As a livestream performer, he uses all parts of the screen. He back-flips off any available surface. He barks. There is almost never a risk of a dull moment.

Speed’s longtime head of production and technical director, Samuel Iles, who goes by Slipz, says that Speed is mindful of many different vectors when he is streaming: “screen space as well as physical space and space in time.” Anything that might make a viewer tune out is ruthlessly excised. “Time is money with these things,” Slipz says. “He hates it when viewers are left waiting.”

And he can tell if they’re getting antsy. At the side of every stream is a rapid-fire comment section, which Speed and his team refer to as, simply, Chat. If Chat gets bored, it’s time to move on. Chat is a stand-alone entity, something like the computer in “WarGames” that’s issuing instructions — a real-time Q Score fire hose, only more amorphous and rambunctious. There are tiers within Chat — regular posters, or those who pay to have their text read as speech, or pay for an explosion sound to try to wake Speed up in the middle of the night. (The 24-hour streams continue while he’s sleeping, his one concession to calm.)

Every stream begins with an exhortation: “W’s in the chat!” When Chat appreciates someone, they put a W in front of the person’s name. Should Chat disapprove, a flood of L’s isn’t far behind. This shorthand extends to Speed’s banter. Throughout the day, whether on or off stream, he vocally congratulates his friends and collaborators this way — “W Slipz,” “W Henry,” “W Jamal.”

Everyone in Speedland is subject to Chat’s whims. For a while on the America tour, Anthony Alvarez, one of his newer camerapeople, was a punching bag, subject to teasing and hazing. But he persevered, taking on the cleaning of the tour bus when it got particularly unruly. Eventually Chat noticed and was won over. Sydney Long, Speed’s personal assistant and the only young woman on the day-to-day team, is at times the object of Chat’s prurient interest. Chat’s attention extends out in the real world; in almost every city, as Speed navigates crowds, someone shouts out the names of his security guards, who have their own dedicated fans, too.

Chat is perhaps the one thing with more autonomy than Speed himself. He is its parent and also its child. He generally travels with an extra phone strapped to his wrist — on the America tour, it was the Huawei Mate XT, a foldable model triple the width of an iPhone — purely so he can monitor Chat’s opinions and edicts. It’s a fool’s task — it moves far too quickly to absorb in full — but he insists he can do sentiment analysis in real time.

He picked up three of the phones while in Shenzhen, China, last year, on a trip in which his initial local production partners mysteriously dropped out after the first day. If this alarmed Speed at all, in the image-conscious and authoritarian state, he didn’t admit it. “China was like heaven for me,” he said. “5G everywhere.”

Speed has a relentless gift for staying in the present, constantly taking in new information while never being burdened or distracted by any of it — ceaselessly curious but somehow impervious to deep learning. He moves from shiny object to shiny object easily. In Salt Lake City, over the course of an hour, he beat a local YouTuber in a balloon hop race, then led a flash mob of fans through the State Capitol, then briefly joined a Mormon service at the Salt Lake Tabernacle, treating each event with equal reverence and nonchalance.

On these tours, few details are communicated to Speed in advance, to ensure the relative purity of his response. “When it’s too structured, the streams aren’t as good,” says Mason Klein, Speed’s manager. “If he sees a carrot dangling, he’s going to follow it. His sense for entertainment is so good that it’s like, Hey, let him go do the thing. And if we’ve set up too many things, then it gets confusing sometimes.” Protecting Speed, his direct line to the camera and, by extension, Chat is the No. 1 goal of the traveling pod.

“You don’t want to teach,” says Ward, the producer. “You want to create the opportunity for learning.”

A typical day for Speed — the 22nd day of the Speed Does America tour — looked like this: He woke up in Denver, met with a local fixer who owns a vegan rolling-paper company and ate breakfast, and then his fans figured out where he was and started calling the restaurant’s landline, asking to talk to him. He headed to the zoo, trailed by a black Cybertruck, white and blue BMW sports cars, bicycles, scooters, a wheelie-popping BMX bike, lowrider pickup trucks — every conveyance a young person who had determined his location could legally pilot.

As usual, it was easy to find Speed. “You’re always compromised,” said Henry Roper-Spring, the redwood-size head of the security detail, which on this day consisted of his core team and up to 11 local guards.

Then Speed hopped on a decommissioned school bus painted Fortnite blue and set up for gaming, but Wi-Fi wasn’t available, so he headed downtown and bought organic dog popcorn, then gave it to an older woman eating al fresco next door, then bought an expensive vintage Denver Broncos jacket and, upon learning it wouldn’t fit over the phone permanently tethered to his wrist, took it off and threw it into the assembled crowd.

He went to the experimental art space Meow Wolf, then rock climbing in borrowed boots, then to Casa Bonita, a cavernous tropical theme park restaurant with a kitschy live band and cliff divers, where he got a cheery balloon hat but still wound up glum: The restaurant staff told him he couldn’t jump off the cliff.

He finished his night at the compound of Brian Shaw, four-time World’s Strongest Man, who wore Size 18 Air Jordans and had hands the size of bowling balls. Shaw good-naturedly beat Speed at arm wrestling. Outside, a man who appeared to be homeless got onto the property and asked for a job when he saw Speed, before beginning to bark at him. The stream kept going as Speed returned to the bus for its overnight ride to South Dakota.

Speed did not grow up traveling. “The traveling was Ohio to Michigan,” he deadpanned last May in an S.U.V. back home in South Florida. Cincinnati, where he lived before relocating to Detroit at age 15 to move in with his father, was rough. “I traveled the world. I know what poverty is. It’s not that poverty, but I’d say in American terms, it’s poverty.”

When he felt isolated, he turned online, where he absorbed himself in a then-nascent livestreaming ecosystem, mostly young men who filmed themselves playing Fortnite, NBA2K and FIFA. By 13, he was doing the same, feeling more himself in front of the screen than out in the real world. But the form was largely inert: people watching other people on their computer screens who are watching their own computer screens, an infinitely recursive loop of passive absorption.

In its earliest incarnations, streamers had loftier, more artful ambitions than that. In 1990s New York, during the first Silicon Alley boom and not long after “The Real World” mainstreamed the idea of civilians living life on camera, the tech entrepreneur Josh Harris founded Pseudo, one of the first companies to broadcast live video online. He created an art installation called Quiet, in which dozens of people shacked up in the basement of a building in TriBeCa and had their every interaction recorded. (The experiment-slash-rager was broken up by the New York Police Department in the wee hours of New Year’s Day 2000, after a month.) He even outfitted his own apartment with cameras to document his domestic life and ultimately the collapse of his romantic relationship, a radical novelty at the time.

Toward the end of the 2000s, Justin Kan, then a fledgling entrepreneur, made a website called justin.tv, which began with him broadcasting his own life and grew into a platform where others could do the same. Kan went on to be a co-founder of Twitch, which was bought by Amazon in 2014 and remains one of the dominant forces in the medium. The widespread availability of high-speed internet and smartphones democratized the medium, turning it from a pursuit of idiosyncratic online performance artists into the most seamless way to express the self online, even if that means just playing video games and howling along.

This is what Speed was up to in 10th grade, when his mother told him he had to quit streaming until he finished high school. But after moving to Detroit, he didn’t have these limits. He would race home after school to begin streaming, sometimes still with his school uniform on. On Speed’s 16th birthday, his father recalled, Speed blew out the candles on his cake and went right back to his stream.

He started making money, lots of it — he would send his father thousands of dollars for groceries — and began to see the contours of a future career. He became close to the streamer Adin Ross, who would later become known for his friendship with Drake and his support of Donald Trump’s 2024 re-election campaign; at one point in 2021, Ross, who also began as a video game streamer, unsuccessfully tried to persuade Speed’s mother to let him stay in Los Angeles to pursue his career — a failure itself immortalized on stream.

By junior year, Speed had two million YouTube subscribers following him for his hyperactive gameplay, full of shrieking and bug-eyed reactions and impromptu back-flips off his furniture. Scrawny and often shirtless, he alternated between reverie and delirium. Going to school became uncomfortable. “Everybody was staring at me. People were just, like, asking me for pictures,” he recalled. “It was just weird.” Eventually, he dropped out and completed his high school requirements online.

“I think about that every day,” Speed said about the parts of growing up that eluded him. “I didn’t really get to have that childhood that I wanted — 15, 16, 17. ’Cause I was just so hard-core on it. Like, I didn’t have a social life. I didn’t go outside.”

The ultimate unit of transaction of a stream is one performer, one screen, one viewer — if that thread is broken, the whole project collapses. Turning that intimacy into a mass event is the goal. The mass manifests in the crowds Speed generates, seemingly instantaneously, and also in the fullness of Chat at any given time. To say nothing of the untold millions who consume Speed’s content in highly edited short-form bursts published elsewhere on the social internet.

As the streams are happening, a legion of clippers — people who make money by excising catchy moments and posting them to a network of short-form accounts across TikTok, Instagram and X — exponentially grow Speed’s reach. On the America tour, after almost every significant interaction Speed had, everyone in the production car immediately looked at X to see the reactions to the instant clips being shared. This is how the ephemeral medium achieves some amount of permanence, though only as permanent as short-form videos in algorithmic feeds can be. But in order for these clips to work — for them to feel spontaneous and antic in the right way — the stream has to feel that way too.

“The stream is always the main priority,” Speed says. He moves with security to protect himself but, maybe more important, to protect the space between him and the camera.

Speed’s livestreams are “an incredibly egalitarian art form,” says Ward, who was an independent film producer before being hired to coordinate the America tour. “The random grandma here has as much screen time, and has the opportunity to create just as much of a moment, as the most famous person from that country, or the best athlete here, or the president.”

Speed seems most at ease around children and animals. Celebrity holds little sway with him — at times, when he’s streaming with famous people, who insist on navigating the world on their own terms, rather than through the lens of Speed and his camera, it reads as an irritant. One evening in Brooklyn, early in the America tour, Speed met up with the rapper Lil Uzi Vert and the skater Tyshawn Jones at a skate park, but he spent most of his energy engaging Uzi in playful dance battles while Jones drifted off, bored and a little bewildered.

“I don’t know,” Speed said. “I just don’t like being cool.”

If American cultural exports of the past forced other countries to adapt to our way of doing things, Speed’s success is predicated on a curiosity about other cultures and a lack of interpersonal arrogance (though he’s occasionally bratty). He is a sponge, displaying no awkwardness in new situations, as comfortable in Thailand or Trinidad and Tobago as in Tennessee. The medium he works in necessitates that he hand off the megaphone to anyone nearby to do with it whatever he or she wishes — destabilizing, or outright inverting, the relationship between creator and consumer, star and fan. His openness and eagerness have made him one of the most visible ambassadors of American youth culture around the world.

As long as the technology works, that is. Each location brings its own challenges, Slipz says: “When we’re streaming in the Okavango Delta, there is no cellular connection, so that was full satellite. When we’re streaming inside the pyramids in Egypt, we have a wired connection to a satellite outside.” In Bolivia, they experienced so many tech glitches that Speed couldn’t stream at all.

Speed deploys a strategic buffer between himself and political or social agitation, sidestepping it with an antic mien. On the same afternoon in South Dakota, he had glossy, frictionless encounters that should have felt at odds but didn’t: doing back-flips at Mount Rushmore and then being smudged with a sacred eagle feather at the still-under-construction Crazy Horse Memorial. (“How much would it cost to finish?” he asked, as if he might be able to scrounge up the cash or crowdsource it from Chat.)

This philosophical cushioning extends, also, to how Speed navigates the sort of racially charged incidents that have become almost de rigueur when he is abroad — fans shouting slurs or handing him watermelons, acts that read somewhere between pure disrespect and cultural mistranslation. While traveling in Chengdu, China, Speed was approached by a young woman who shouted at him, using a racial slur: “I am racist! I hate [expletive]. I’m a [expletive] killer. I’m so sorry about that.” Speed appeared genuinely (or generously) baffled, telling his companions, “I don’t think she knows what she was saying.”

The cesspool of racist invective extends to Chat, too. But Speed is almost studiously deaf to it, owing to his upbeat disposition but also the years he spent as a gamer. There may be no purer distillation of the bile of online discourse than the lawless conversation in a multiplayer video game. Foul language abounds, as young men with underdeveloped frontal lobes test out extreme insults and sometimes outright bigotry. (Before he smoothed out his rougher edges, Speed was suspended from both YouTube and Twitch several times for coarse language and behavior.)

It’s little wonder the racism of the real world feels theatrical and abstract — being raised in an environment in which everything is thrown at you but nothing can truly stick creates an unusually thick layer of Teflon for dealing with real-world antagonism. Someone sent bananas to Speed’s father’s house once. “We laughed about it,” his father said.

“I done heard it all,” said Speed, who was often an eager participant in the edgy smack talk when he was younger. “When you’re just on a game, you’re just in a different mode. It’s reckless.” For Speed, it’s all content — relentless positivity is his currency, and he is rarely willing to display vulnerability onscreen.

Typically, his response to racist taunts is to taunt back, or feign shock, or simply roll his eyes and keep moving. “I don’t like to get too emotional during streams,” he said when describing a recent trip to Senegal, where he visited the Maison des Esclaves on Gorée Island, a slave trade way station that’s now a museum. “I didn’t want to show people me crying.” His audience is there to watch him be superhuman, not regular human.

Speed enjoys reminding viewers that he is not, in fact, a regular human. He has been blessed with a freakish natural athleticism, which he deploys to comic, sometimes preposterous effect. During a 2024 livestream, he jumped over a Lamborghini speeding toward him (it was driven by his father) and then a McLaren just behind it. He was characteristically equanimous about this. “I woke up one day,” he said with a shrug. “I was like: I can jump over my car.”

A few months later, he arranged an impromptu 50-meter race with Noah Lyles, the reigning Olympic 100-meter champion. Lyles won, but with less breathing space than you might assume. Most people would accept being outpaced by Lyles, but not Speed. “I really lost,” he said, as if it were surprising. “I’m like, I never want to lose to you again.”

Since last fall, he has been sprint training with a coach, Ato Boldon, the four-time Olympic medalist. “You get a Ford, you think, eh. You get a Ferrari, it keeps you up at night,” Boldon said. “A Ferrari dropped in my lap.” By next year, Speed said he expected to run 100 meters in under 10 seconds, something fewer than 250 men have ever done. In July, Speed announced that he was preparing himself for a shot at the 2028 Olympics. “I can’t be letting nobody beat me in a [expletive] race,” he said.

Athleticism is universal; it allows Speed to be understood even when language is a barrier. It’s also a natural bridge to soccer, which is the common tongue he deploys to the greatest effect abroad. It was soccer, Speed said, that first opened his eyes to the world — players, languages, jerseys, cultures. It was watching Cristiano Ronaldo, his favorite player, that gave him his signature celebration: big jump, arms spread, shouting “SIUUUUUUUU.”

The first time Speed barnstormed a continent for his stream, it was because of soccer. He had planned on livestreaming from the UEFA Euro 2024 matches, but the league said he couldn’t. “And so we’re like, Well, what do we do now?” Slipz recalls. “And Speed says, Well, let’s just travel to every country in Europe.”

On that tour alone, Speed went to 18 countries with no plans and only Slipz and Henry by his side. But his fans showed up. And the crowds, thrilled to see Speed in their country, grew in rapid and unpredictable ways, birthing a phenomenon.

“The fans started turning it into a game,” Henry says. “We are going to make a way bigger crowd than, you know, Lithuania. You know, we are going to be way more inviting here than that country.” In Oslo, thousands of fans showed up to greet him within the first 15 minutes of his stream, the rest of which was largely devoted to figuring out ways to flee. In Albania, he was taken to meet the prime minister. It turned out Speed didn’t need soccer at all — he was the main attraction.

The soccer authorities have since recalculated. This year, for the World Cup, FIFA, Fox and YouTube helped clear the path for Speed to be able to stream from any game he attended, not as a commentator but as an enthusiast with wild access. He was the only creator granted such an arrangement.

“A gamer sort of overlay to live sports makes a lot of sense to an audience,” said Angela Courtin, YouTube’s vice president of sports and entertainment marketing. Teaming up with Speed allows the company to “bring more surface area to the sport.” Which is to say, younger viewers can watch in younger ways.

Those who market sports, or films, or songs, or books, or any cultural product that used to have a stranglehold on its audience now must seek out, and do business with, intermediaries who narrate their consumption experience, sometimes unpredictably. A generation of young people has grown up watching people watch things, or watching people play things. In fact, they’ve come to expect it in a way no previous generation has. For some people, watching Speed react to the game is more enthralling than watching the game itself. (Or watching the game at all; the cameras for Speed’s stream stay on him the whole time, only incidentally showing the game for brief moments.)

In July, Speed flew to Atlanta for the World Cup semifinal match between Argentina and England. For many earlier games, he wore split jerseys to demonstrate affection for both teams. But here he wore England’s kit, perhaps because of an incident during an earlier Argentina match against Cape Verde in the round of 32, in which a fan told him to “go cry at the zoo.”

At Mercedes-Benz Stadium, in a quirk of fate, the field-level luxury box Speed had access to was just below the Argentina ultras, their bloodthirstiest fans. Seeing Speed, a handful of them began to throw things in his direction, including plastic cups, leaving him drenched. Some Argentina fans in the next box began yelling at Speed to go home. “I’m not going home,” he boomed back. “You go home — I’ll be right here.”

He was unflustered about what happened at the game. “The football fans is what’s part of football. And you can’t get that on a broadcast — you can’t get that one-on-one connection of the fans,” he said a few days later. “I heard the worst of the worst. I’ve been through the worst of the worst. So it doesn’t faze me as much.”

And besides, Speed said, it wasn’t the whole of the truth. “I kid you not,” he said, “if I pop a stream right now in Argentina, it will be love.”

A decade or two ago, a diligent teenage talent with a bright smile and an optimistic countenance would have been quickly routed into the Disney or Nickelodeon universes, had a franchise hung on his shoulders and emerged a few years later with a growing career, a built-in audience and, very likely, a crippling inability to navigate adulthood. But Speed has already reached a level of visibility greater than what any specific media conglomerate could offer him.

In a decade or so, the hordes of young people who track Speed’s every move will have money of their own to spend. It’s not hard to imagine a company like Marvel deciding to build a new superhero universe with a Speed character at the center — Speed films, Speed books, Speed action figures, Speed athleisure, Speed supplements. In the meantime, he is at work on an anime series in which he is the protagonist, made in collaboration with Brian Robbins, a longtime children’s television executive, and the avant-sleaze filmmaker Harmony Korine, who once likened Speed’s streams to the naturalistic work of the Russian director Andrei Tarkovsky.

Speed’s vision for the future of livestreaming includes both artistic and structural innovation — as long as the camera is on, he can evolve what he feeds it. “Streaming is endless — it’s like a galaxy,” Speed says. “Some people view streaming in a whole different way. They feel like you can complete it. You can’t never complete streaming.”

At a certain point, the ubiquity of streaming and the spread of surveillance technology begin to dovetail. Cameras, private and public, are everywhere, and there is perilously little a person can do to opt out of being filmed. Spend enough time in proximity to the Speed Cinematic Universe, and you inevitably will become a bit player. There is simply too much time and screen to fill, and anyone can be a foil, even a well-intentioned reporter.

I spent almost four straight days on the Speed Does America tour — sleeping on a top bunk on the staff tour bus; showering at random hotels; giving chase alongside his camerapeople, producers and security guards. Mostly, I tried to avoid the camera. Journalistic impartiality demands remove — at least, that’s how the old rules function.

But those frameworks feel especially sclerotic and fragile in Speed’s world. Scrub through the stream archives from those days, and you can spot me in the background here and there. Living inside the Speed bubble, it was hard not to think that, over time, everyone on earth would become a background player in someone else’s stream for 15 seconds — the broadcasters and the broadcasted in perpetual tense embrace. (Some people understand that and then lean in. At almost every city on the tour, a streamer nicknamed White Speed showed up unannounced, inserting himself into Speed’s stream, building his own career in Speed’s slipstream.)

By the second half of the third day, Speed seemed to decide I could be turned into a character, if a nameless one. At the Obsidian Dining Room in Yellowstone National Park, after we had spent some time at Old Faithful, I was sitting off-camera with Ward, who was discussing how his acting background was helpful, in a way, for playing the role of Ames Ward, camp counselor of Speed’s ultimate summer adventure, when Speed plopped himself between us and asked, “What are y’all talking about?” I told him we were discussing what it means to play a character, the difference between performing a version of yourself and just behaving normally, without worrying about being perceived.

Speed listened with a kind of mock intensity and then started vibrating, shaking madly as if having a seizure. I reached out to soothe him, and he slowly began to return to normal. He concluded that everything around us was, in essence, performing. “This cup, it has a point,” he said. “It’s playing the role of putting the liquid inside of a cup.” I nodded along — my first short guest role.

After dinner, on the two-hour ride to Jackson, Wyo., I rode on the main bus and watched as Speed settled in to a burst of “the zoomies,” which is how he refers to the far looser late-night desktop streams that are closer in spirit to his teenage years. He turned on Pharrell’s “Happy” and started jumping on his bed, cheerful and petulant, arrested in adolescence. Ward and Jamal joined in, squeezing through the narrow hall that led to the back of the bus.

Speed then came out to the front, where I was sitting, and looked me up and down, deciding whether I could be entertaining.

Moments later, a Twitter account with 80 followers posted a screen grab of the ensuing interaction. There I was: Speed clutching my throat with his right hand and wagging his tongue in my face as I clutched his left shoulder and wagged my tongue back at him. The poster had added a tender caption: “The whole team just like him” — meaning adorable, I presume. I had notched one tiny victory in the Speed wilds. W me?
