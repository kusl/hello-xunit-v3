The main argument of that is that there was a group of people who called themselves neoliberals — coined it in 1938. And when they called themselves neoliberals, they meant that they needed to figure out how to protect the principles of market freedom at a time when that was under challenge from, as they saw it, the left and the right.

So some of these people became quite famous: Milton Friedman, Friedrich Hayek, Gary Becker, George Stigler. And through the Cold War, of course, they exercised their political influence as well as they could and became favorites of Thatcher and Reagan. Come the end of the Cold War, one would think that they had reached their ultimate victory, right?

And yet, and this is what was so fascinating, when I went back to look at transcripts of the meetings that they were having, the papers they were publishing, as the Berlin Wall fell, as the Soviet Union fell apart, they were actually panicked about a new set of threats. The red enemy, as they often put it, had turned into a green one.

Remember, this was an early high point of activism against greenhouse gases and the dangers of what was then more often called the greenhouse effect and global warming than climate change.

They were worried about the resurgent demands for affirmative action and equality of representation in the workplace, equal pay. They were worried about minority sexual demands from the gay rights movement to the feminist movement.

So this becomes important because it creates the conditions for an alliance that would only really come to full fruition years later between people who wanted to defend free market freedoms as best they could with people who seemed to have opposition to those same progressive politics but for different reasons.

This is the moment of the paleoconservative alliance, for example, between a radical libertarian, like Murray Rothbard, with someone who is mostly interested in cultural nationalism, like Pat Buchanan.

Alternative for Germany is a perfect example of this, as well — founded by neoliberal economists who thought the euro was being mismanaged, creating alliances with ethnonationalists and opponents of Muslim immigration. How these two things ended up getting linked together seems to be an important question to figure out about our politics.

Well, one of the ways that I understand you as making the argument is that they’re linked together in the view that very fundamental categories and hierarchies that persist in society are natural. That they reflect I.Q. differentials, cultural differentials, people who are more or less fit either at the individual level, at the group level, etc.

And what I thought was interesting about this book in conversation with your Musk book is that, to me, there’s a lot of this sense, in the way Musk talks about the woke mind virus, that there are important hierarchies, categories in society, and that the danger comes from a constant progressive push or a woke push to dissolve them. That you can’t have Western civilization without the hierarchies upon which Western civilization was built.

I just interpret a lot of Musk’s political activity as not about free markets or any of these other things but about this.

Yeah. The subtitle to that book is “Race, Gold, I.Q. and the Capitalism of the Far Right.” And in all of those cases, there was an expressed belief that nature was an unequal place, and it distributed gains unequally, either through birth or territorial endowments, and that to deny that basic natural inequality was to pave the road to serfdom, as Hayek described it, in a new way.

Now it would be paved with green bricks and pink bricks instead of red bricks. And someone like Musk, I think, absolutely falls into that category.

Where I think the ’90s conversation gets updated and transformed in our present moment is especially around a category like I.Q. I.Q. is fascinating because in the 1990s, it came surging back. The arguments that put it back on the table were especially those of Charles Murray, the think tanker, and Richard Herrnstein, the Harvard psychologist, in their enormous best seller, “The Bell Curve,” from 1994.

That book said two things. It said at the one end of the bell curve, we’re going to get people whose I.Q. is going to be ever more determinative of economic success because of the knowledge economy we’re in. And at the low end of that bell curve, we’re going to have people who are going to be less and less able to perform in this knowledge economy, and they’re going to be a social problem.

And the incendiary part, of course, was that there are group differences in racial intelligence such that we can assume that there will be a disproportionately large number of Black people at that bottom end of the bell curve.

So the era of the knowledge economy is also the era of mass incarceration and the real turn toward more punitive police law-and-order policies. The way that this was mapped onto an idea of who is equipped and who isn’t for the big transition in how money will be made and jobs will look ends up being a pretty good kind of way to read that.

Why that’s different now is that, by their own statements, the tech leadership now assumes that almost all of those knowledge jobs are going to be automated away. So if I.Q. was predicting even at the far 10 percent of the curve for the people who would thrive in the coming internet world, then what happens if that has now been reduced to like 0.1 percent? Because all those other jobs — the coders, the project managers, the copywriters, the technical writers — are all going to be done by L.L.M.s and agents, which is what they are banking on.

Then you have a question where A.I. has actually eaten I.Q. And now you’ve gone from just a cognitive elite, which is what Herrnstein and Murray called it, to a hypersmall cognitive super-aristocracy.

I want to stay on this point because, to me, this coheres a lot of things in Musk that are puzzling from the outside. You’ve got this guy for whom the most important thing in the long-term vision is expanding the light cone of human consciousness through the stars.

There’s this bending of the psychic knee to the A.I. superiority. Musk is at the same time very worried about falling birthrates as he is destroying foreign aid, which can certainly help people thrive and be alive and live long lives and reproduce.

At the same time, he is virulently against the immigration policies of many of these countries. It’s like, where does this guy who, on the one hand, appears to believe in maximizing the quantity of consciousness fit with this guy who seems not to really like a lot of humanity? Where does this guy who believes in humanity fit with this guy who seems to be accelerating the A.I. turnover?

But if your sort of fundamental view of the world is about a kind of cognitive hierarchy, and you have certain views about where A.I. will sit in that, where different cultures sit in that, it’s not like one-to-one with any individual thing, not one-to-one with race, not even one-to-one with silicon versus flesh.

It’s just one-to-one with a certain vision of who or what qualifies as having the capacity to be a main character in this performance. Then a lot of it, I think, begins to fall into an unsettling, but a kind of coherent space.

I think there are a couple of ways to look at it.

One is to draw back on his grandfather’s ideology, which I hesitate to do too much because there’s no evidence that Musk himself was influenced by his maternal grandfather.

But his maternal grandfather, Joshua Haldeman, was a very active member of something called Technocracy Incorporated in Canada in the 1930s. And Technocracy Incorporated was proposing something very similar to what we’re talking about here.

It was about the dissolution of the existing price system, the dissolution of democracy altogether, the reduction of the unit of exchange and, I guess, store of value to something called an erg — which was the unit of energy that each human is able to produce in their life.

And the rational allocation of those ergs would be overseen by a very, very small but elite caste of engineers. That is an extremely far-fetched but very radical vision that actually, on the menu of existing political ideologies, probably comes the closest to what it seems Musk is describing.

But I think to get there, there are a few steps involved. To put it mildly.

[Both laugh.]

So there is the problem of the transition right now for Musk. I think it’s a central problem: How do we get from where we are now to this 10-year mile marker to the end of his life? Actually, he has no real plans for his succession.

One of the extraordinary things about SpaceX, from a corporate-law point of view, is that he has about 85 percent of the voting shares. If he dies tomorrow, you’d simply have to wait for those shares to be allotted.

I think he’s really banking on that transition having taken place in his own lifetime. How do you get there?

This is where the relationship to the “Hayek’s Bastards” discourse is actually relevant again. One of the arguments I make in that book is that under this atmosphere of uncertainty, in times of unrest, there is a tendency, as within the investor world, to do a flight to safety.

These far-right thinkers in association with neoliberal thinkers were offering different forms of refuge and safety where a certain value would be protected and more durable.

So those were things — your whiteness will save you, your access to a gold hoard will save you. Your citizenship value, which was one of Bannon’s talking points in the first Trump campaign, will save you.

The problem with Musk now is, if he’s saying that the people who will get through the filter are such a small minority, then how do you sell this vision to enough people to let you realize your goals in the medium term — except by defaulting to very shopworn scripts of ethnonationalism and social war?

So his decision to say: The way I’m going to win consent is simply to make you frightened of outsiders and make you believe you need my technology to save the lives of yourself and your family — is a way that politicians have used to justify a lack of material redistribution for over 100 years.

There’s actually nothing new about that, and it’s actually one of his, I would say, less innovative interventions, this belief that simply meming the fear of the invader will be enough to get you through that transitional moment until you get to, quite literally, liftoff or escape velocity.

I always thought the reason the Musk-Trump alliance was so potent, particularly in its early moments before Musk discredited himself as a direct actor through DOGE, was that they recognized in each other, and particularly Musk recognized in Trump — it’s why I think Musk endorsed him after the assassination attempt when Trump raised the fist — that Trump, in his third campaign for president, began to represent a return of masculine will to politics out of this era of woke, feminized, consensus-oriented politics.

For a lot of people, this was the idea of the builder, the industrialist, the Ayn Randian Superman.

The world spirit on horseback.

The world spirit on horseback. Yeah. Trump began to embody some of that. I don’t think he does now.

There’s this way in which, to Musk’s many, many, many fans — and pretty young to middle-aged male fans — that he represents this masculine conquering impulse.

It’s why some of the scary visions of the future he presents — at the level not of content but of texture — they don’t always scare people. Because to hear a person saying this is to hear a person saying: We will sally boldly forth and try to forge a glorious and unknown future for humanity. Even if it’s one that, on the face of it, barely seems like a future for humanity at all. But at least it is not afraid of the future.

Yeah, and I think it also sets up a Pascal’s wager or Roko’s Basilisk dynamic: The closer you are to him, the closer you adhere to his vision, the more likely it is that you’ll be one of the ones who gets through the filter.

So if he’s the one who is making the future, then to deny him would basically be to doom yourself to obsolescence — and that’s not an exchange that a lot of people are willing to make.

What’s amazing, really, is to see how resilient he’s been despite the depth of his provocations. But I think the important thing to see, just so as not to give him too much credit in this, is to see how he has actually managed to monopolize a lot of things that were the product of collective will, collective creation and collective intelligence.

That directs attention to the task of reform, which is: How can you dethrone Musk? Not in the sense of throwing away all of the technology that he produced by often piggybacking on other people’s innovations — but how can you create a different story whereby it’s the people, the nation, who decide this and not just one man and his posts?

Well that, I think, brings us back into the more material realm of immediate politics. Because one of the things that strikes me about what we are seeing now is that it is a fundamentally unstable equilibrium.

You have Elon Musk, whose companies, as you have described, are inextricably intertwined with politics. It would be hard to unwind the state from them.

But also by that very same token, if you’re a Democrat, the idea that you’re going to be dependent on Musk, and he can turn off Starlink if Ukraine or some other country or ally or even America is doing something he doesn’t like — that’s very, very dangerous. That intertwining with the state creates a vulnerability.

So it becomes very, very dangerous for him the more he becomes allied with a more extreme version of the right to have any left-wing or centrist candidates win really anywhere in the world in countries that are dependent on him.

At some point, either you end up in a situation where Musk needs to break the opposition to him or the state needs to contain the threat. It just seems fundamentally unstable.

It’s not like he gave $10 million. He just dropped $450 million like it’s nothing — because to him it is nothing.

The way that my co-author and I were talking about this — and we had, interestingly, more pragmatic conversations about Musk and Muskism in the book in Europe than we’ve had in the United States.

I think the proximity of Musk and the problems you’re describing to Americans makes it very hard to deal with it rationally. There’s such a desire to lash out at this level of cathexis — the suturing together of the future of the American economy with Musk and his products — that sometimes there’s a will to moralize and psychologize and just bat him away and denounce him without further reflection.

Whereas in Europe, we found that there was a three-R strategy that we were discussing. One is regulation, of course, and you could say that’s the route that the E.U. is taking.

The other model, though, is replacement. You look at what Musk has made, and you say: How much would it cost to make substitutes? And often the cost is very, very high.

But the third model is really to reimagine what the heck this tech is even for. And that’s where I think looking at the range of Musk’s history of business creation is actually helpful.

We alluded to this before, but the promise of electric autonomy, for example, that he was holding out when Tesla was mostly making E.V.s, but also when it was making solar panels and battery storage units, was actually much more socially useful and potentially inspiring than what he has done with his businesses ever since.

So I think one attitude there is to start from first principles and just say: What are we even investing in any of this for in the first place?

What do you want?

What do you want? And actually, one of the pedagogic virtues of Musk and his very monstrousness is he has made that question impossible not to ask. Because if the answer to that question is what we want is a continued level of being completely fused with this man, then I think a lot of people, on reflection, would not want that.

So he’s made us ask that question, and he knows that he’s put himself into a difficult situation with people asking that question — hence the impression of inevitability.

In The Economist interview, he said: It’s not like we can stop A.I. right now — that there’s no red button that says stop.

Actually, of course, there is.

Generative A.I., in its current model, requires an enormous amount of build-out of compute infrastructure that, right now, is being slowed down to the point of even stopping, through hundreds of moratoria across the country.

So it’s actually not true that there’s inevitability to the world that he’s imagining. It actually is true that we have options. Breaking out of that cosmic vector that he’s produced through the uses of the attention economy is the first step in that.

But it gets even more pragmatic than that. I’ll give you an example. In his newsletter this week, Paul Krugman — centrist liberal economist — has talked in two segments about nationalizing Starlink.

Paul’s only a centrist to you.

Right. Yeah. But this is actually not something that you would have expected him to talk about a few years ago. The use of the Defense Production Act, the comparisons to the nationalization of Montgomery Ward.

A pragmatic economist until recently would have said: If you nationalize a company that’s in the top 10 on the Nasdaq, you’re going to trigger a recession. There will be cascading effects of loss of confidence, repricing of risk ——

Loss of incentives for future companies to build this kind of capacity.

Absolutely. But the very fact that this controlled demolition of the very Muskist dependencies you’re describing is now being brought onto the agenda, I think, is very encouraging.

But this is what I actually mean by an unstable equilibrium. I saw those pieces from Paul. I thought they were very, very interesting. And as you say, it’s interesting that Paul — whom I call a progressive economist, but not a communist, not someone prone to nationalization — was talking about this.

I think it speaks to a world where you could really imagine a situation with a Democratic president, and Musk turns off Starlink at some critical moment, and the Defense Production Act is immediately engaged, and you do have this moment of genuine showdown.

I think from Musk’s perspective, this needs to be understood as a risk of what he’s doing and the way he’s acting. And from the other perspective, because you are getting to this position ——

And we should say, one reason I haven’t focused on the Ukraine Starlink question is, right now, the reporting is contested in a way I cannot untangle as to whether orders came from somewhere inside the Trump administration or were implied from somewhere in the Trump administration or the activity was from Musk.

I just don’t know the answer, and I can see it also being a miscommunication. These things happen, too. So I don’t want to say confidently what happened there.

But I think one can say relatively confidently that you could imagine a series of things happening where — and look, I don’t love Michael Bloomberg dropping $80 million at the end of the campaign, either — I don’t think this is the way campaigns should work — you begin having much more serious efforts at restricting the ability of megabillionaires to drop this much money.

Musk is a billionaire on a scale that somebody like Bloomberg cannot even touch. It’s like the Vatican of technology that he’s got over there.

But I think it’s also important to keep in mind one of the things that I started this conversation talking about, which is the financial dependency. SpaceX is in the top 10 in the stock market indexes.

More than 50 percent of Americans hold some kind of equities or stocks. Most of those are directly or indirectly, mostly indirectly, through passive funds, connected to the health of Musk’s businesses. So let’s say 20 to 30 percent of Americans have some kind of stake there indirectly.

One of the things that Musk has proposed recently is that he give one stock in SpaceX to every American child. The proposal is that would happen through the Trump accounts, which are automatically made accessible to children in the United States.

If he goes through with his plan, then that number of American citizens goes up to about 50 percent. There you’re approaching a way of blackmailing, you could say, the American population away from following someone like Krugman’s plans by saying: Do you really want to hurt yourself? Are you going to hurt your child like that by punishing me, Musk, the person who’s keeping the stock market afloat?

And if you get into the granular questions of who owns the stock, it gets even more interesting. Who are the top 10 investors? The Ontario Teachers’ Pension Plan — huge amount of SpaceX stock. The primary educational target of the Trump administration and often Musk himself, Harvard University, is a huge holder of SpaceX. University of North Carolina.

When you see that, although high-profile and superficially startling moments of turning on and off drone connectivity in the battlefield are the sharp edge of the wedge, the base of what anchors the dominance of the Silicon Valley digital sector in the United States is really in the stock market itself.

The fact that that’s how we do redistribution, that’s how we do the social contract, is saying: You pay into this market-determined pension fund, and you will be paid out of it. And whoever is keeping that thing propped up arguably has the real power of saying what the social contract looks like.

Then I think sitting in that bidirectional dependency, but that real dependency, it’s maybe worth ending in a strange place, but I actually do think it’s an important one.

You mentioned that the conversations in Europe are maybe a little less prone to psychologizing than the conversations in America. The problem, though, is that Musk’s psychology is very important.

There’s something that he says a lot. I was there in 2016 at the ReCode conference — I was, actually, I think, one of the first people to write about this — when he was talking about how he believed it was very likely we lived in a computer simulation.

This is a sort of idea that Nick Bostrom, that philosopher you mentioned, is very associated with. But he has kept saying it, actually in somewhat stronger terms. I want to play this clip. This is Elon Musk on Walter Isaacson’s show:

Archival clip of Musk: I once had a dinner at my house with a physicist and a computer scientist, and they’re both leaders in their field. I asked: What do you think the probabilities are that we’re in a simulation? The physicist said zero percent, and the computer scientist said 100 percent simultaneously. So I guess maybe I should go 50 percent. I don’t know. There are odd things that happen in this simulation, especially if you’re me. Think about it: If you’re me, should all these things really be happening? Should I really be making spaceships to go to the moon and Mars? That doesn’t seem real.

The simulation theory is that, if you just imagine an advanced society would create lots of simulations, the way we make lots of video games, you just shouldn’t assume that we are living in the base reality.

That’s sort of the argument in a nutshell. But he goes further, and I get, in a psychological way, why. I’ve heard him say this now repeatedly that what really seems unlikely is to be Elon Musk — to be building starships and influencing global politics and having 250 million X followers, or whatever it is, and being the first trillionaire.

The mixture of a life that is so unlikely and also not grounded in some other ontology — not some religion or some other way of imagining the world — plus an attraction to the simulation hypothesis and, of course, to be a lifelong gamer.

I just think that for a person with as much power as he has, to have that kind of psychology to suspect that this is a game where your role in it is making big moves — I don’t really know how to describe that situation. But it is a distinctive one that we all now have a lot of exposure to.

There are a couple of varieties of the simulation theory, too. One is we are all simply just artifacts of this future civilization’s computer video game.

But the other is that most people are shadow people and a small number of people are actually player characters. And it seems like that’s the one that orients him most.

In the libertarian community that reflected a lot on these things, one of the people is Robin Hanson, the George Mason University economist, who said: What kind of ways would we conduct ourselves if this were true?

And he says: If this were true, then it would make sense to first care nothing about other people — so things like foreign aid and the welfare state would be frivolous things to spend time on — to be as entertaining as possible, to accumulate as much wealth as possible and to try to simply organize the world around you in as successful a way as you can.

There’s this radical reneging on anything like social responsibility that is only possible if you embrace this incredibly nihilistic reaffirmation of your megalomania. And the question is how to remove the props and struts that hold him up in that delusion, because it is not true, and he needs to be somehow persuaded from the outside that it’s not true.

If he continues to be incentivized to indulge in those ways of thinking, then he will never be required to be divested of that.

I think that’s a place to end. Always our final question: What are three books you’d recommend to the audience?

Well, there’s so much amazing work happening right now in the world of tech criticism and political economy, so I wanted to draw attention to three exceptional recent books that move up the stack from under the ground to the clouds and the apps.

The first book would be Thea Riofrancos’s book “Extraction: The Frontiers of Green Capitalism,” which is about something that we’ve put out of our minds for the last 18 months or so, which is the challenges of climate combined with a world that still operates according to the price mechanism and where the state can fit in.

None of these machines, certainly none of the data centers, would work without the raw materials being extracted somewhere from the earth and put into their circuitry. Thea gives a wonderful description of the political settlements and tensions and struggles that emerge in the process of doing so.

The second book would be a book by Nick Srnicek called “Silicon Empires.” I think one of the handicaps we have right now in talking about tech ideology and tech political economy is a lot of things get jumbled together. When you talk about semiconductors, it’s somehow also a discussion of frontier labs, it’s also a discussion of neoclouds and data centers. But these individual market sectors actually operate under very different dynamics. And I haven’t read anything yet better than Nick Srnicek as a calm, guided tour up the stack of what A.I. actually is as a built entity.

The third book is one I think is really special. It’s called “Against Tech Oligarchy” by JS Tan and Clarissa Redwine, and both of them were organizers within tech companies in Silicon Valley.

Clarissa Redwine led the successful organization of Kickstarter, and both of them were at Twitter trying to organize employees at the time of the Musk takeover.

What they draw attention to is the fact that class struggle and class politics exist in Silicon Valley inside of tech companies, and they give the insider’s view of how you practice class power inside of the tech sector. It’s interestingly not always, as they see it, through the old-fashioned model of getting recognition for a union. That can sometimes slow down some of the dynamics that draw people into social activism.

So it’s a very cleareyed and wonderfully written insight into something that I think most people know very little about.
