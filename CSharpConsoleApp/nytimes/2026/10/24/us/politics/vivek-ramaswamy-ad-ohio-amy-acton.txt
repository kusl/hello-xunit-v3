ARTICLE

How the Most Ruthless Ad of the Midterms Divided Republicans
The fallout from Vivek Ramaswamy’s brutal spot targeting his Democratic rival in the Ohio governor’s race showed that even in the Trump era, a political attack can be seen as going too far.
By Reid J. Epstein and Campbell Robertson
Published 2026-10-24 09:03 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/24/us/politics/vivek-ramaswamy-ad-ohio-amy-acton.html

The most explosive advertisement of the midterm elections has split Republicans and again posed the question of what is fair game for campaign attacks in American politics.

In the days since the leak of the ad from Vivek Ramaswamy, the Republican nominee for governor of Ohio, many fellow G.O.P. politicians have denounced, disavowed or distanced themselves from the spot. Yet President Trump and Vice President JD Vance have defended the ad, a remarkably ruthless one even by the standards of modern political campaigns.

The ad features the estranged mother of Mr. Ramaswamy’s Democratic opponent, Amy Acton, calling her daughter a liar and urging Ohioans not to vote for her. Ms. Acton has said that she has not spoken to her mother since she was 12 years old, when she was removed from her mother’s custody after her stepfather was arrested and charged with raping her.

The outrage over the ad’s dark backstory has left Mr. Ramaswamy, a billionaire entrepreneur who exemplifies the anything-goes, attack-at-all-costs ethos of Trump-era politics, at the center of a self-created political firestorm.

The Republican disagreements over the ad show that major figures in the party can still become divided over the rules and red lines of political warfare in the Trump era. And they set the stage for high drama on Election Day in Ohio, a state that has trended solidly Republican in recent years but now has outsize importance in the midterms. The overarching political question is: Will a potential backlash to Mr. Ramaswamy hurt other Ohio Republicans in competitive races, especially Senator Jon Husted, who is trying to fend off a well-known Democratic rival in a vital race for Senate control?

There is now a degree of unpredictability in the state. Campaigns in both parties do not know how voters will react.

Mr. Ramaswamy, who along with his aides declined to be interviewed for this article, said at a campaign stop on Friday that he had no regrets. The ad, he suggested, was his attempt to change the rules of engagement for Republicans in Ohio — just as Mr. Trump has done in Washington.

“What we need is a governor at the top just like President Trump is for our country — a governor for this state who stands strong, who says we will not play on your terms,” he told a crowd in Strongsville, a Cleveland suburb. “We will stand for what we know is right and true, and we will not apologize for it.”

A Shocking Ad Emerges Online

There had been rumblings for weeks in Ohio political circles that Mr. Ramaswamy’s top aides were in touch with Dr. Acton’s estranged mother.

Dr. Acton’s troubled childhood was no secret, and had been the subject of reporting during her high-profile stretch as the pandemic-era health director for Gov. Mike DeWine, a Republican.

Mr. Ramaswamy had been trailing Dr. Acton in many public and private polls. Like other Republicans, he was confronting voter frustration with high gas prices, tariffs and Mr. Trump’s leadership.

Producing the ad was a politically hazardous move for Mr. Ramaswamy, a 41-year-old start-up founder who has shown a high tolerance for risk since he emerged on the scene during his 2024 campaign for president. During that race, he presented himself as a full Trump acolyte and made plenty of enemies with his brash style before placing fourth in the Iowa caucuses, dropping out and endorsing Mr. Trump.

The Ramaswamy campaign was still testing the ad to gauge its effectiveness when the spot burst into public view late Monday.

D.J. Byrnes, a left-leaning local journalist known as the Rooster, published the ad online, calling it “loathsome.”

The spot, which took little time to spread widely online, showed Dr. Acton’s 82-year-old mother, Donna Arthur, using harsh language to denounce her estranged daughter.

“Don’t let her become your governor,” Ms. Arthur said.

‘Just Plain Wrong’

The backlash was immediate and intense.

As the controversy swirled, Mr. Ramaswamy reached out privately to other Ohio Republicans to gauge their reaction, according to three people briefed on the exchanges. He felt the coverage by the news media was unfair and wanted a chance to explain his thinking, even if it did not lead to public support for running the ad.

Governor DeWine called the ad “deeply, deeply troubling” and “just plain wrong,” and he urged Mr. Ramaswamy — whom he has endorsed — to take it down. Mr. Husted, who faces his own tight election against former Senator Sherrod Brown on Nov. 3, said he would not have run the ad. Republicans in close races in other states, like Representative Ashley Hinson of Iowa, who is running for Senate, said it should come off the air.

Not only has Mr. Ramaswamy so far declined to back away from the ad, but his campaign also bought more than $4 million in Ohio airtime to show it to viewers.

Supporters of Mr. Ramaswamy echoed his contention that he has been treated unfairly by the news media and argued that there had been no public anger when Dr. Acton’s campaign had attacked him.

“Democrats and the media are trying to change the subject from ‘she’s a liar’ to ‘she was abused as a child,’” Beth Lear, an Ohio state representative from just north of Columbus, wrote in an opinion essay published Friday in The Columbus Dispatch. “It’s a masterful attempt to manipulate voters’ emotions by making Amy a victim.”

The ad served as an attempt to shift the focus of the campaign in its final days from frustrations about Mr. Trump’s economy to a referendum on Dr. Acton’s honesty. But in so doing, it placed Mr. Ramaswamy’s own morals and ethics in the spotlight, and reminded voters that Dr. Acton survived a seriously troubled childhood.

Mr. Ramaswamy has, in a sense, run a political psychology experiment on his home state. Will voters in Ohio be outraged that he has tried to secure a political advantage through a woman estranged from her daughter over allegations of rape, or will they better recall the accusation that Dr. Acton is lying?

“We ran an ad about lying,” Mr. Ramaswamy told the crowd in Strongsville. “Amy Acton is lying. She has been lying this entire campaign about me.”

But at a pumpkin festival on Friday night in Circleville, Ohio, Mr. Ramaswamy’s running mate, State Senator Rob McColley, said the campaign did not believe Dr. Acton was lying about her childhood abuse.

“Oh no, that is horrible,” Mr. McColley said. “We are accusing her of lying in her adult career.”

Anger From the Acton Campaign

Dr. Acton’s campaign has almost run out of words to express its disgust over the ad. A spokeswoman, Addie Bullock, has called it “despicable,” “disturbing” and “disqualifying.”

“Ramaswamy will do anything, including embrace child sex predators, to get what he wants,” Ms. Bullock said.

Court documents from Mahoning County, Ohio, back up the descriptions by Dr. Acton and her brother of the events of her childhood. In 1978, when Dr. Acton was 12, her stepfather, Cledith Arthur, was arrested and charged with raping her.

He pleaded not guilty and was indicted by a grand jury, after which Ms. Arthur lost custody of Dr. Acton and her brother. A month later, court records show, Mr. Arthur fled the area, and a warrant was issued for his arrest. The Arthurs appear to have lived the ensuing decades in Pennsylvania, and according to Dr. Acton’s brother, they stayed together until Mr. Arthur died in 2004.

In the campaign ad, Ms. Arthur accuses her daughter of lying about her past but does not address the rape allegations.

It is not clear where Ms. Arthur lives now, and she did not respond to messages this week.

She had been living in her car with some cats in the Calvary Church parking lot, just outside Boalsburg, Pa., according to the Rev. Stacy Sublett, the church’s pastor. Mr. Sublett said that he had tried to find Ms. Arthur permanent housing in the area but that she declined the assistance.

He said he was not aware of friends or family who came to see her.

Jessica Contrera contributed reporting from Akron, Ohio, David DeKok from Boalsburg, Pa., and Kevin Williams from Circleville, Ohio. Kitty Bennett contributed research.
