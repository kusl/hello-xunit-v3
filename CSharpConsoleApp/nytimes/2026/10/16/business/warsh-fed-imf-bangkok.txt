ARTICLE

Warsh Vows to Tame Inflation as A.I. Delivers ‘Shock’ to Economy
The Federal Reserve chairman stuck to his pledge to avoid providing guidance about the path forward for interest rates, but did nothing to convey an urgency to raise them later this month.
By Patricia Cohen and Colby Smith
Published 2026-10-16 05:02 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/16/business/warsh-fed-imf-bangkok.html

Kevin M. Warsh, chairman of the Federal Reserve, reiterated his commitment to “deliver price stability,” amid rising energy prices and soaring investments in artificial intelligence, which he called a “shock” to the economy. But he did not convey an urgency for the central bank to raise interest rates at its meeting at the end of the month.

Mr. Warsh spoke Friday in Bangkok before a standing-room-only crowd at the annual meetings of the International Monetary Fund and World Bank. He stuck to his approach to avoid giving specific guidance about what the Fed might do next in terms of policy adjustments.

“I don’t prejudge meetings. I don’t believe in saying now what I might do at the meetings we have in the balance of this year,” Mr. Warsh said. “It’s a new chapter at the Fed, and we’re not in that business anymore.”

The chairman conceded he would only “make some judgments at the October meeting and the December meeting,” the final two gatherings of the year.

His comments, made in conversation with the I.M.F.’s managing director, Kristalina Georgieva, came right after she had warned of inflation pressures and the need for central banks to keep a tight rein on monetary policy in an earlier session.

“Central banks should be laser-focused on price stability,” she said, adding that she welcomed the “firm language” coming from the Federal Reserve and other central banks.

“Rate hikes today are better than falling behind the curve” and having to impose bigger rate increases later, she said.

Mr. Warsh also noted most central bankers around the world were tightening monetary policy in sync in response to rising global inflation.

Mr. Warsh’s comments were the last scheduled remarks before the Fed enters its communications blackout on Saturday, ahead of its policy meeting on Oct. 27-28. That meeting comes just days before the U.S. midterm elections, which will determine whether Republicans retain control of Congress.

New inflation data this week that showed price pressures accelerating in September has complicated to some degree the Fed’s deliberations in October.

Overall consumer prices rose 0.6 percent, the largest monthly increase in five months. Compared with the same time last year, prices were 3.7 percent higher. Wholesale prices also rose, while retail sales figures showed consumers continued to spend.

Mr. Warsh on Friday expressed optimism about the overall state of the economy, highlighting specifically the enormous impact that A.I. is having.

A.I. promises huge economic benefits in the long run, he said, but warned the path there “is likely to be a very bumpy one.”

“The advent of this new technology is probably the most consequential change in the U.S. and the global economy in my lifetime,” said Mr. Warsh.

Booming A.I. spending is one of many factors that have pushed up inflation this year. Another driver is the war with Iran, which has led to soaring energy prices.

According to the I.M.F.’s projections, inflation in the United States is not expected to return to its 2 percent target until the middle of 2028.

Still, several top policymakers made the case in recent weeks that the Fed could raise rates gradually after voting unanimously in September to lift them by a quarter of a percentage point to a new range of 3.75 percent to 4 percent.

According to rate projections released alongside that rate decision, most Fed officials expected one more quarter-point increase this year after September’s move.

If the Fed stands pat in October, as is widely expected, Mr. Warsh will be under pressure to explain why the central bank is not moving more aggressively in the face of broadening price pressures.

Raising rates will keep the central bank in the cross hairs of President Trump, who has repeatedly attacked it for failing to acquiesce to his demands for substantially lower borrowing costs.

Mr. Trump has spared Mr. Warsh, whom he handpicked for the job, from the bulk of the criticism. Instead, he has directed his ire at the chairman’s colleagues on the Board of Governors.

Mr. Trump is engaged in a protracted legal fight with one of those seven members, Lisa D. Cook, whom the president has tried to fire for over a year over allegations that she committed mortgage fraud. The White House last week announced an investigation into Ms. Cook, culminating in a hearing that she was directed to attend.

Mr. Warsh made a point on Friday of defending the Fed, its previous leaders and its staff. “Power comes from our institutional credibility, bestowed on me by the great chairmen that came before me,” he said.

Since taking over as Fed chairman, Mr. Warsh has sought to recast how the central bank operates in a range of dimensions. In addition to jettisoning the provision of guidance over the future path of rates, he has also downplayed the importance of specific data releases and instead described data-point dependence as “a dangerous preoccupation.”

As part of Mr. Warsh’s goal of enacting “regime change” at the central bank, he has announced five task forces to examine issues ranging from how the Fed communicates to how it measures inflation. One of those task forces is focused on productivity gains tied to A.I. and the broader impact on the labor market. Mr. Warsh said on Friday that there would be a “hearing” on the subject on Nov. 16 in San Francisco.

Mr. Warsh noted that A.I. could transform how the Fed did its work because of its enormous analytical potential. This is “a big deal for central banks,” he said, adding that it will “fundamentally change the conduct of monetary policy.”
