What if the A.I. Economy Crashes?
Azeem Azhar and Paul Kedrosky debate the case for and against an A.I. bubble.
By Ezra Klein and Rollin Hu
Published 2026-10-16 09:04 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/16/opinion/ezra-klein-podcast-azeem-azhar-paul-kedrosky.html

This is an edited transcript of “The Ezra Klein Show.” You can listen to the episode wherever you get your podcasts.

Right now, A.I. is the driving force behind the gains in the U.S. stock market and U.S. economic growth.

It is sucking up so many construction resources, it now appears to be crowding out housing, manufacturing facilities and basically everything else you might need to build.

And the question, if only from a financial perspective, is: Is this all worth it?

Is all of this investment going to pay off? Or is A.I. a bubble, and one of these companies is going to miss an earnings target or get downgraded, and the whole thing is going to pop — taking all of our retirement portfolios with it?

So I wanted to have two different perspectives on this.

Paul Kedrosky is a partner at the venture capital firm SK Ventures. He’s a fellow at M.I.T.’s Initiative on the Digital Economy and has a popular newsletter at paulkedrosky.com. He thinks we are in an A.I. bubble and has been very vocal about it.

Azeem Azhar is a British technology entrepreneur, an author and an analyst. He is a creator of the Exponential View, which is both a great Substack I read often and a research platform where, among other things, they have been creating dashboards tracking whether there’s financial stress in the A.I. industry.

I think it’s fair to say that Azhar’s view is somewhat more optimistic than Kedrosky’s. So I wanted to put the two of them into conversation together to try to see why you might or might not think we are in an A.I. bubble.

So what’s unusual about this — let’s just talk about, really quickly, what a bubble is. A bubble occurs whenever asset prices detach from any semblance of economic reality.

We have one of the largest competitive capital expenditure spending waves in history. So in Western economic history, there have been eight or nine of these moments when we build out some giant general-purpose technology — a G.P.T. Not the ChatGPT kind — but a general-purpose technology.

And we’re building it out like we were the railroads, like we were electrification, like we were the canals, like we were the interstate highways. And every one of those moments has led to overbuild.

There isn’t a major economic capex episode in Western economic history that didn’t end in a rolling series of crises, vastly inflated asset prices and a vast oversupply of capacity of whatever the thing was — railroads, fiber-optic cable, take your pick.

And so that’s the bubble. The bubble is — once again, we’re vastly oversupplying something because of the availability of capital and the eagerness of the suppliers of the capital to continue building out in anticipation of future demand.

Now, none of this is to say that A.I. is frivolous or it doesn’t work, or that there aren’t incredibly useful cases for it. That’s not the point any more than that was the point for railroads. Railroads were incredibly useful, and it still ended up where something like 50 percent of the track miles ever laid in the U.S. turned out to be useless.

So the bubble is this vast oversupply of something, largely fueled now by credit, which in turn will lead to massive business failures and write-downs of assets. And all of that credit has spread through the economy. It’s inside of insurers, inside of private credit, it’s inside of sovereign wealth funds.

We’re on the order of trillions of dollars now. And it’s probably going to hit $10 trillion before it’s done.

I mean, so far we’re sitting at around trend data with respect to U.S. productivity growth. There’s no sign of A.I. improving productivity. If we saw participation rates sharply increasing, that would be something that would help to tell me: OK, A.I. is an inclusive technology in bringing people back into the economy. That would really help.

If we saw G.D.P. growth going back above trend — we’re still sitting around the levels of the post-World War II trend, at the low end of it — any of those things would tell me that something big was going on, like a true productivity shock in the economy that warranted the kind of expenditures.

So if any of those, and probably, the three of them at once happened, that would tell me I was wrong. If those productivity numbers come through, then fine, everything changes. But if it doesn’t, you’re looking at something that looks more like the 1930s than it does like the 2000s.

All right. I think that’s a good place to end.

So always our final question: What are three books you’d recommend to the audience? Paul, why don’t we begin with you?

Kedrosky: Sure. “Middlemarch” by George Eliot is my go-to on thinking about normal people being swept along by the winds of change as a major technology revolution is taking place. It’s set at a time when the Industrial Revolution was taking hold, and I think it’s a tremendous book about that.

So the other one that I like to recommend is “Liberty’s Dawn” by Emma Griffin, which is a collection of the average workers’ biographies and notes from during the Industrial Revolution. It’s just a wonderful, wonderful look at what it was like in the middle of that wrenching transition. And as people would talk about economic data and everything else, what it was like to live through it.

What did it feel like? What was the pain I felt? And what are my hopes for the future? It’s just a wonderful book about that.

And the last one that I always point to is about Samuel Insull, a largely forgotten figure. He was probably one of the most important entrepreneurs of the 1920s. His empire in electricity shows what happens — building an empire on top of a G.P.T. and even doing that successfully can still end up in collapse and calamity. But it’s a wonderful story. So “Insull: The Rise and Fall of a Billionaire Utility Tycoon,” by Forrest McDonald.

Azeem?

Azhar: So my three books — the first is “The Day the Universe Changed.” It was written by BBC science journalist James Burke back in the 1980s. I read it as a teenager, and I’ve just reread it, and it is all about how, as we develop new types of knowledge, we start to rethink our role in the world, what matters to us and how unsettling that is. And he walks us through 1,000 years-plus of that through Western and Asian history.

The second book is “Fathers and Sons” by Ivan Turgenev, which is a book written in the 19th century. It’s at that moment where the ideas of science and modernity are making their way into Russia, and you have the generational conflict between the more traditional generation and the younger generation, and what stays as a bond between them — what even the most recent breakthroughs in science can’t tell you about what it is to be human. I think it’s very relevant.

And then the final one, which I’m just rereading again, is “Candide” by Voltaire.

Azeem Azhar, Paul Kedrosky, thank you very much.

Kedrosky: Thanks, Ezra.

Azhar: Thank you.
