ARTICLE

Avalanche of G.O.P. Cash Keeps the Party Competitive Despite Its Problems
Republican Senate and House candidates are being out-raised by their Democratic rivals, but the party has received a huge boost from groups tied to Elon Musk and President Trump.
By Theodore Schleifer and Matt Zdun
Published 2026-10-16 09:02 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/16/us/avalanche-of-gop-cash-keeps-the-party-competitive-despite-its-problems.html

The political fund-raising picture in the final month of the midterm elections is something of a Rorschach test.

If you’re a Democrat, new election filings show many of the party’s Senate and House nominees raising more than their Republican rivals. This campaign cash is the most important money in politics, because candidates can control it directly and buy ads at cheaper rates.

If you’re a Republican, the same filings show that the G.O.P. has a significant advantage in money from super PACs and other outside groups tied to Elon Musk, President Trump and the party’s big donors. In many races, this edge has allowed the party to club Democrats with advertising even though Republican groups have to pay more for ads.

In fact, both of these perceptions are true. And for Republicans, the ability to hold their own — at least — in the midterm money fight is a rare bright spot as the party faces a gloomy political environment. This financial resilience has allowed Republicans to put up a robust defense of the Senate, in particular, as they try to keep control of Congress.

A bonanza of Federal Election Commission filings on Thursday offers a view of how much every federal candidate in the country raised and spent from July through September, as well as the finances of many of the outside groups supporting them. Even more filings for those groups will land next week.

Here are seven takeaways from the filings:

Democratic Senate contenders have raked in cash.

In eight of the Senate races expected to be most competitive in November, Democrats have raised more money this year through their main campaign committees than their Republican challengers, the filings show.

No Democrat raised money like James Talarico, the Democratic nominee for Senate in Texas. His campaign hauled in a staggering $114 million from January through September, about $100 million more than his Republican rival, Ken Paxton, raised.

The Democratic edge was also striking in Kansas, a surprising Senate battleground. Adam Hamilton, the Democratic nominee, raised $13.4 million in the first nine months of the year, roughly six times as much as his Republican opponent, Senator Roger Marshall. (In just the third quarter, Mr. Hamilton brought in nearly $10 million, compared with Mr. Marshall’s roughly $800,000.)

The money will help Democrats’ effort to take back the chamber, but many of the seats the party needs to flip are in solidly red states — and Republicans have more money from outside groups.

Democratic House candidates also have an edge.

A similar story played out in the House, with Democrats out-raising Republicans in 38 of the 42 races currently considered competitive.

One of Democrats’ largest fund-raising advantages was in North Carolina’s 11th Congressional District, a tossup race that was thrown into turmoil when the incumbent Republican, Representative Chuck Edwards, dropped his re-election bid amid sexual harassment allegations. The Democratic nominee, Jamie Ager, raised about $4.6 million in the first nine months of the year, versus about $350,000 for the Republican nominee, Jennifer Balkcom.

But Republicans are winning with outside money.

Despite Democratic candidates’ fund-raising strength, party leaders have expressed profound concerns that Republicans hold a financial advantage.

Why? Because Republican super PACs and other outside groups have raised and spent far more than their Democratic counterparts.

That helps explain why Republicans are currently slated to have an advantage of about $650 million in general-election advertising, according to a review of past and future ad reservations across federal and state races by AdImpact, a media-tracking firm. (Ad reservations are fluid and are likely to change before Nov. 3.)

The main super PAC for Republican Senate candidates, the Senate Leadership Fund, out-raised its Democratic rival by about $122 million this quarter. The Republican group also revealed on Thursday that it had been the sole funder of super PACs backing several of the party’s battleground candidates: Mr. Paxton in Texas and Senators Pete Ricketts of Nebraska, Susan Collins of Maine and Dan Sullivan of Alaska.

The parties’ main House super PACs appear to be in a more competitive financial fight, according to advertising data. The main House Republican group raised $94 million in the last quarter, but its Democratic counterpart will not disclose its finances until Tuesday.

Nationally, some of the gap between Republicans and Democrats can be traced to three Trump-affiliated super PACs, which have combined to spend over $210 million on advertising, according to AdImpact. These groups’ finances will not be revealed until Tuesday, either.

If Paxton wins in Texas, he owes Elon Musk big time.

For weeks, it wasn’t quite clear how national Republicans were able to spend close to $160 million to help Mr. Paxton, who has long struggled to raise money and faces the single strongest Democratic midterm fund-raiser, Mr. Talarico.

The answer was Elon Musk.

The filings on Thursday suggested that the world’s richest person indirectly funded Texas PAC, the biggest-spending super PAC backing Mr. Paxton. He did it by sending enormous sums to the main Republican Senate super PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund, which had long resisted spending on behalf of Mr. Paxton. The group feared that doing so in a pricey state like Texas would divert resources from other competitive Senate races.

But Mr. Musk, the new filings show, made two huge donations to the Senate Leadership Fund: $75 million on Sept. 9 and $83 million on Sept. 23. After each one, the Senate Leadership Fund sent an identical amount the next day to Texas PAC — which had no other donors through Sept. 30.

So even as Republicans worry that Mr. Paxton could do the unthinkable and lose to a Democrat in Texas, Mr. Musk appears to have given him a better chance of hanging on.

Republican megadonors are still giving generously.

Democrats have their own big donors. Two super PACs funded by the Soros family entered October with about $174 million on hand. And two of the party’s biggest donors, the Facebook co-founder Dustin Moskovitz and his wife, Cari Tuna, put $16 million into the midterms fight, the new filings show.

But most of the biggest midterm donors are supporting Republicans.

Dick Uihlein, a Midwest-based billionaire industrialist, put a staggering $67 million more into his super PAC last quarter, which in turn gave $15 million to the anti-tax Club for Growth.

Jeff Yass, a hedge fund investor, gave $10 million more to a super PAC backing Vivek Ramaswamy, the Republican nominee for governor of Ohio. Mr. Yass has now spent $30 million backing Mr. Ramaswamy.

Mr. Yass and Mr. Uihlein have each now donated well over $100 million this cycle to federal political committees.

And then there’s Mr. Musk, who backed a few Republican groups beyond S.L.F., including about $89 million he gave to his own super PAC. In sum, Mr. Musk disclosed on Thursday a total of about $289 million in third-quarter contributions to federal committees, making him the biggest known midterm donor.

Mysterious dark-money groups are everywhere.

Several political groups made disclosures on Thursday that did not actually disclose much of anything about the true origins of their cash.

That is a reflection of the rise of dark money, with both parties relying more heavily on nonprofit groups that are not legally required to report the identities of their donors.

A lot of Democratic money traces its way back to super PACs with names like Government That Works PAC, Your Community PAC, and United Futures. Those groups, in turn, got their cash from a battery of liberal dark-money nonprofit groups.

The main Democratic Senate super PAC raised a majority of its money in the third quarter from dark-money groups, according to an analysis of its donors.

On the Republican side, a super PAC called Defend Us PAC, which is spending against Mr. Talarico in Texas, said it raised $8.3 million last quarter. Of that, $6 million came from a group called the American Prosperity Caucus Inc., which does not appear in state records in Georgia, where it says it is based.

And most of the money raised by a super PAC backing Senator Jon Husted of Ohio, $5 million, came from a dark-money nonprofit in Delaware nonprofit that was created in May.

There are plenty of other examples. Confused? In modern campaigns, that’s the point.

There were other revelations, too.

Here are a few other things that caught our attention in the third quarter:

- Democrats are getting $38 million worth of digital help at the last minute from a new super PAC. That group, Lower Costs Now, received that much in late September.
- Trump administration officials continue to be major donors to political causes even while serving in government. The ambassador to Britain, Warren Stephens, and the White House chief design officer, Joe Gebbia — both of whom are billionaires — each donated over $1 million to Republican efforts.
- The Republican Governors Association seems particularly invested in electing Zach Lahn to lead Iowa. The group’s super PAC sent $12.5 million to his campaign, an investment it did not make for any other candidate.
- A super PAC for the sports-betting industry, Win for America, has now raised $82 million this cycle, making it one of the wealthiest industry interest groups in the midterms. About half of that sum has come from the company DraftKings.

About the data

Fund-raising figures in the graphics include total contributions to each candidate’s main campaign committee, not including refunded contributions and contributions that candidates made to themselves. The totals also include transfers to the main campaign committee from other committees.
