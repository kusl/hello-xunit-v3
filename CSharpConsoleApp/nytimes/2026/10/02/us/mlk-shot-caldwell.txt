ARTICLE

A Shot Rang Out. M.L.K. Went Down. And a Reporter’s Instincts Kicked In.
Earl Caldwell was staying at the Memphis hotel where the civil rights leader was killed.
By Marc Lacey
Published 2026-10-02 09:00 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/02/us/mlk-shot-caldwell.html

The first loud noise that Earl Caldwell heard on the night Martin Luther King Jr. was murdered was the sound of a soda bottle falling from the second-story balcony of the Lorraine Motel.

Thinking it was a gunshot, Caldwell rushed to the door of his room. It was a false alarm.

Then came a second sound, this one louder. He likened it to an explosion in its ferocity, and it turned out to be what he feared: a rifle shot directed at King, mortally wounding him.

Caldwell was the lone reporter at the motel in Memphis on that spring night in 1968. He was in Room 215. King was upstairs and several rooms over, in 306. Caldwell’s dispatch in the next day’s New York Times captured one of the civil rights movement’s seminal moments. There would be weeks of rage over the murder, which he covered. And decades of dispute over how it all went down, which he participated in as well.

“Reporters believe that if you are there, you can see what happens and then you can tell it, the real story, witnessed firsthand,” Caldwell wrote more than 50 years after the assassination. “That day — April 4, 1968 — was my lesson on what really happens: You are there, yet you don’t see everything. But in the tangle of events, there is a sliver of whatever it is that happened that comes past you, and that’s precious.”

The Times had sent Caldwell to Memphis to keep close to King, who was then under siege. There were death threats against him and challenges to his leadership. A week earlier, a rally he led in Memphis for striking sanitation workers had turned violent, and critics questioned whether he was in control of his movement anymore.

Caldwell, who joined a virtually all-white staff at The Times in 1967 after a decade of reporting on sports and news for smaller papers, had never met King before shaking his hand in Memphis on that trip. And he almost bungled his first big assignment.

He had landed an interview with King the day before the fatal shooting, in which King spoke of the movement’s future. But as a storm swept through Memphis that evening, Caldwell was unaware of King’s last-minute decision to go to Mason Temple, a local church, and deliver what would be his final speech, the one in which he seemed to anticipate his own death.

Caldwell never heard King say he’d been to the mountaintop. That he had “seen the Promised Land.” That he thought he “may not get there with you.”

When Caldwell learned of King’s speech the next morning on the news, he thought he might lose his job. The church had been full of competitors, who captured King’s prophetic words. Caldwell spent the day trying, and failing, to match the story he had missed.

That evening was warm, and Caldwell, back in his motel room, kicked off his shoes, stripped down to his underwear and left his door cracked open.

Then the bottle fell.

And, at just after 6 p.m., history unfolded one floor up.

Caldwell darted into the motel parking lot. He saw King’s driver with his hands on his head screaming, “Oh, no, oh, no, oh, no.”

“What’s the matter?” Caldwell shouted. “What’s the matter? What’s going on?”

Someone responded: “They shot him. They shot him.”

Caldwell knew it had to be King.

“I dashed out into the lot but a few steps outside the door I remembered my trousers,” he said, recounting these events later that month in an internal Times publication called Times Talk. He put on his pants. He slipped into some shoes. He grabbed a jacket, a stack of paper and a pen. Then he rushed out, forgetting his room key and locking himself out.

He had missed King’s prophetic final speech. He had heard but not seen the gunshot that killed him. As he later noted, on-the-scene reporting often comes down to luck. (“You are there, yet you don’t see everything.”) So with respect to the details that he was able to observe, Caldwell tried to be meticulous. Hustling up the motel stairway to the murder scene, he bent over and, as he told a friend later, inspected the wound in King’s neck.

“Earl’s reportorial instincts were as sharp as a tack,” said Jack White, a retired journalist who spoke to Caldwell many times about the events of that night. “Dr. King is lying there on the ground. He’s being cradled by Ralph Abernathy. Earl gets close to King and he told me about the gaping hole in his face and said he put his fist up — and he’d later describe it as fist size. He wasn’t just guessing. He actually put his fist up to Dr. King’s face.”

Joseph Louw, a South African photographer who was working on a documentary about King, was also at the Lorraine that night. He took the famous image of King sprawled on the balcony with his closest aides pointing in the direction from where the fatal shot had come. In another of Louw’s photos, Caldwell is visible next to King’s body.

At some point, Caldwell sprinted to a nearby pay phone and inserted two nickels. Out of breath, he told his editor in New York what had occurred, prompting a wholesale remake of the next day’s front page.

“Blood gushed from the right jaw and neck area. His necktie had been ripped off by the blast,” Caldwell dictated over the phone to a rewrite man in New York, who turned his account into a story that appeared under a banner headline: “Martin Luther King Is Slain in Memphis; A White Is Suspected; Johnson Urges Calm.”

The details Caldwell gathered transported readers to the scene. “Dr. King was shot while he leaned over a second-floor railing outside his room at the Lorraine Motel,” the article reported. “He was chatting with two friends just before starting for dinner.”

There was one detail in his article that would be debated for years. Caldwell quoted King’s driver, Solomon Jones Jr., saying that he had seen a man creep away from a thicket across from the motel. Although Caldwell did not mention it in his Times story, he said later that he, too, saw a man moving through the bushes.

This was at odds with the F.B.I. account. The bureau insisted that James Earl Ray, who pleaded guilty to the killing, had acted alone and had fired from a nearby rooming house. (Ray later recanted.)

Caldwell believed that the shot might have come from someone in the bushes and resented that no one from the government interviewed him until 1998, when President Bill Clinton reopened the investigation into the shooting at the behest of the King family. Ultimately, the Justice Department reaffirmed that Ray had acted alone, writing in its report that Caldwell “understandably misrecollects observing someone in the brush.”

Caldwell was disillusioned, and in his subsequent job as a columnist for The New York Daily News he regularly raised what he considered the unsolved mystery of King’s killing. His friend Jack White said that Caldwell fumed at the suggestion he had misremembered anything.

Today, the Lorraine houses the National Civil Rights Museum, and Caldwell’s former room is now a storage closet. But earlier this year, the museum added an exhibit on alternative theories of the killing. Caldwell’s deep baritone can be heard describing holes in the F.B.I.’s case.

The next chapter in Caldwell’s career proved equally momentous. As the Black Panther Party rose in prominence after King’s death, Caldwell was dispatched by The Times to Northern California to report on the group. He earned the Panthers’ trust, spending long hours inside the group’s hangouts, where he witnessed its stockpile of weapons. In one attention-getting piece, he quoted a Panther leader saying that the group backed the “overthrow of the federal government.”

Under the directorship of J. Edgar Hoover, the F.B.I. was intent on undermining the Panthers and frequently pressed reporters for information on their sources. Once, as Lee Levine recounts in a forthcoming book, “In Bad Faith: Earl Caldwell and the Fight for a Free Press,” an agent showed up at the Times newsroom in New York seeking information from Caldwell about a story, and Caldwell handed over his notes.

But now, aware that any cooperation with the government would end his access to the Panthers, he rebuffed the F.B.I. He then received a subpoena ordering him to testify before a federal grand jury. After an attorney for The Times suggested that he would likely have to turn over some information to federal authorities, Caldwell retained his own lawyer. Reporters, he insisted, should not be used as spies for the government. “It goes against everything you stand for in journalism,” he later told Time magazine. He refused to appear before the grand jury and was held in contempt of court.

He fought the subpoena all the way to the Supreme Court, which, in a case that included two other reporters, ruled that the First Amendment did not protect Caldwell and the other journalists from having to testify. (Earlier this year, the Trump administration invoked the ruling to argue that its subpoenas of five Times reporters was lawful. A federal judge disagreed, and prosecutors withdrew the subpoenas.)

Though he lost his case, the ruling against Caldwell prompted dozens of state legislatures to enact “shield laws” protecting journalists from being forced to turn over their confidential sources to the government. It also solidified his place at the center of much of the drama of the civil rights era.

“You have to show up to be in place where things are happening,” said Alex Caldwell Maynard, Caldwell’s godson, who has stayed close to him even as he has suffered ill health and, now 91, has moved to a nursing home. “He seemed to be everywhere.”
