ARTICLE

Tim Janus, One of the World’s Greatest Competitive Eaters, Has Died
He brought rigor and strategy to the sport, rising to No. 2 in the world and eating 141 pieces of nigiri in six minutes in the process.
By Clay Risen
Published 2026-10-14 20:13 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/14/obituaries/tim-janus-eater-x-dead.html

Tim Janus, one of the world’s highest-ranked competitive eaters who, at various competitions, consumed four pounds of tiramisu in six minutes, 10.5 pounds of ramen in eight minutes, 71 tamales in eight minutes, 7.7 pounds of boneless Buffalo wings in 12 minutes and 141 pieces of nigiri in six minutes, died in Boston on Sept. 16. He was 49.

He was diagnosed with an aggressive form of lung cancer in 2022, said his sister, Tess McNair.

For more than a decade, Mr. Janus reigned at the top of his sport, peaking at No. 2 and racking up records that remain standing two decades after he set them.

Some eaters specialize; Mr. Janus ate it all. Cannoli. Marshmallow Peeps. Shoofly pie. And, of course, hot dogs — he frequently placed in the top three at the Nathan’s Famous Hot Dog Eating Contest, where contestants eat frankfurters by the score every July 4.

His career, beginning in 2004 and ending, with his retirement, in 2016, tracked the evolution of competitive eating from a state-fair novelty to a serious undertaking, with televised events, sponsorships and a governing body, the International Federation of Competitive Eating.

The early days were often more spectacle than sport, with outsize characters with outsize names — among them Crazy Legs Conti, Badlands Booker and a 100-pound woman known as the Black Widow. Mr. Janus, who went by the nom-d’estomac Eater X and painted his face with a luchador-style mask, fit right in.

“He wanted to win these contests, and he practiced and positioned himself to win,” said George Shea, who with his brother, Richard, operate the governing federation. “But he also understood throughout that process that there was an absurdity to all of it, and he could hold those two things in his mind.”

Mr. Janus stood out because he actually looked like an athlete: lean and muscular, he was 5 foot 10 and just 170 pounds — ten pounds less than when he started to compete.

He was also more intellectual and analytical in how he approached competitions. Like any serious athlete, he trained constantly, developing a unique regimen of cottage cheese and water to expand and maintain his stomach capacity, not to mention his ability to force feed himself long past the point of satiety (he also ran four days a week).

He watched tapes of his competitors. He filled notebooks with data. And he developed eating strategies: Tiramisu, he decided, should be eaten cream first, since it is denser than the ladyfinger portion, and competitors are judged based on the weight remaining on their plate.

“He had a real science-meets-tomfoolery approach to competitive eating,” Mr. Conti, who lived with Mr. Janus for over a decade, said in an interview.

After leaving the world of competitive eating, Mr. Janus began a new career as a Foreign Service officer. In 2023 he had to cut short a deployment to Mexico City when he was diagnosed with cancer.

Like many of the earliest competitive eaters, Mr. Janus stumbled into the sport. He was working as a day trader in Manhattan, hating the stress of his job. One day he went looking for a way to relieve the pressure and found it in a St. Patrick’s Day eating contest — corned beef and cabbage.

He didn’t win, but he was hooked. He started going to more events on the East Coast, and within months was named rookie of the year by the International Federation of Competitive Eating.

“He brought this unique level of thoughtfulness and earnestness to competitive eating,” Jason Fagone, the author of “Horsemen of the Esophagus: Competitive Eating and the Big Fat American Dream” (2006), said in an interview. “He took this thing that a lot of people just throw off as a joke, and he took it seriously.”

As he evolved as a competitor, so did the sport. Safety standards were imposed. Vomiting — euphemistically called “the Roman method” — was deemed a disqualifying misfortune. A circuit emerged, based around state fairs and regional foods and anchored at the Nathan’s contest, which draws tens of thousands of spectators to Coney Island every summer.

With his good looks and everyman demeanor, Mr. Janus became a poster boy for the sport’s aspirations to seriousness. He appeared on MTV and “Good Morning, America.” In a video interview with The Wall Street Journal, he gamely squared off against two journalists in a hot dog eating race.

Before they started, he offered some tips.

“The first thing is, you have to open your mind to the possibility of eating more hot dogs than you think you can,” he said.

Timothy Peter Janus was born on Dec. 31, 1976, in Farmington, Conn., and raised in nearby Simsbury. His mother, Lucinda Hannon, was a licensed social worker and his father, Peter Janus, was a labor attorney.

Along with his sister, he is survived by his parents and his stepmother, Nancy Janus, a licensed clinical social worker.

He studied political science at Southern Methodist University. Following graduation in 1999, he moved back to Connecticut for two years, where he tended bar, before moving to New York to work in finance.

After he took up competitive eating, he began working nights at a pizza parlor, and eventually quit day trading to make pies full time. He never earned enough money in the sport to live off it exclusively — at the time, no one did — but he also insisted that money didn’t interest him.

“It’s never been about the cash; it’s always about the adventure,” he told The New Era, a newspaper in Lancaster, Penn., in 2006. “One of these contests recharges me more than a week at the beach ever would.”
