ARTICLE

Christa Pike, Awake and Confused, Is at the Center of a Legal Storm
Her awareness and condition deepen ethical and legal questions after Tennessee’s failed attempt to execute her for murdering a classmate decades ago.
By Emily Cochrane
Published 2026-10-07 21:50 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/07/us/christa-pike-tennessee-legal.html

Six days after being shot full of a drug meant to kill her, Christa Pike awoke in a Nashville hospital, shackled to a bed and in a room where she knew no one.

Ms. Pike’s lawyers had been told to expect life support to end because her brain was not functioning after the attempt on Sept. 30 to execute her for killing a classmate decades before. But now, she was awake and in a swirl of what one lawyer called a mix of anger and confusion.

Her condition, disclosed on Wednesday after a court hearing, remains severe. She has a blood clot in one arm and pneumonia, and had not eaten — she was unable to swallow when her lawyers saw her. She could not move her arms, and her hands had swollen to resemble inflated surgical gloves, said the lawyer, Randy Spivey.

“She woke up in a hospital surrounded by people she didn’t know with a tube down her throat,” Mr. Spivey said during a news conference. “We don’t know the toll all of this will take, but Christa is a survivor.”

Ms. Pike’s awareness and evolving condition heightened the stakes of a procedural hearing in Nashville on Wednesday. A judge said she would order Tennessee to preserve records and evidence tied to the execution attempt and to ensure Ms. Pike could communicate easily with her lawyers.

The hearing came as Tennessee grapples with the ethical and legal questions surrounding the case of Ms. Pike, 50, the first person to survive injection drugs that were supposed to be lethal. A lawyer with the office of the state’s attorney general told a judge on Wednesday that he could not say whether Tennessee would try again to end her life.

A spokeswoman for Gov. Bill Lee, a Republican, who paused executions while an independent investigation is underway, has not responded to questions about whether he would grant Ms. Pike clemency. Her lawyers have pleaded for him to allow her to spend the rest of her life in prison, arguing that she has suffered enough at the hands of the state.

“The dynamic is different because she’s now awake,” said David Raybin, a Nashville lawyer who helped write the state’s death penalty statute. The avenues for litigation, including for violations of her civil rights and constitutional protections against cruel punishment, have suddenly expanded, he said.

Ms. Pike has been on death row since she was a teenager, after she was found guilty in 1996 for her role in the torture and murder of a classmate, Colleen Slemmer, 19. Ms. Pike, along with two other people, lured Ms. Slemmer into the woods, where they slashed at her with a box cutter and smashed her head with pieces of asphalt.

Ms. Slemmer’s family, who raised money to drive to Nashville from Florida for the execution, had supported capital punishment for Ms. Pike. Ms. Pike was 18 at the time of the murder and the only one of the three students to receive the death penalty.

Ms. Slemmer’s mother, May Martinez, has since expressed frustration that the justice promised to her family has been denied.

Ms. Pike’s lawyers had repeatedly warned that Ms. Pike’s medical conditions, including her small veins, could lead to a complicated and unnecessarily painful execution. But on Sept. 30, they said, she was “at peace” after repeatedly expressing remorse for her crime.

The execution was first delayed by a flurry of last-minute legal maneuvering. It resumed after about nine hours, when word came just after 5:45 p.m. that the conservative majority on the Supreme Court said it could proceed.

But over the next two hours, witnesses described Ms. Pike as alert and lucid for stretches of time. Behind curtains, for nearly an hour, they could hear her breathing.

“She helped the execution team find what they thought was venous access,” Mr. Spivey said. “She was kind and polite as they worked for an hour, stabbing her arm with needle after needle.”

Just before 9 p.m., witnesses were told to leave. An ambulance arrived, and Ms. Pike was taken to a private Nashville hospital. Through the weekend, she had been unconscious, on a ventilator and intubated, as medical staff members worked to remove the drug, pentobarbital, from her veins. The drug shuts down electrical activity in the brain and could have caused long-term complications by lowering her oxygen levels or weakening circulation.

The details of the current lethal injection protocol in Tennessee, implemented in 2025, has been largely concealed. That was among the factors that prompted Ms. Pike’s lawyers on Wednesday to ask a judge to order the state to preserve all records and evidence related to the botched attempt.

“Today’s hearing was about transparency,” Luke Ihnen, one of Ms. Pike’s lawyers, said after the hearing. The state’s word, he added, “is shrouded in secrecy and legal jargon in attempts to obfuscate and obscure what really happened.”

John W. Ayers, an assistant attorney general, argued that the request was unnecessary, given that the agency was already required to preserve evidence. He said observation logs, medical waste and products were among the records already collected by the Department of Correction, in addition to pictures taken the night of Sept. 30.

But the judge, I’Ashea Myles of Davidson County Chancery Court, sided with Ms. Pike’s lawyers and outlined the contours of an order that she said she would sign. Among the records she listed were waste, needles, catheters, medical supplies, restraints, gurneys, syringes, drugs and IV lines, as well as communications that night.

“You will take special care not to lose, misplace, accidentally delete any information that may be pertinent,” she told Mr. Ayers.

The judge also instructed the state to allow easy communication between Ms. Pike and her lawyers, ensuring they have the same access as when she was held in prison.

Ms. Pike’s lawyers also raised concerns that one hand, as well as her feet, remained shackled to her hospital bed, despite requests from medical staff that they be removed. Chancellor Myles said that she was likely to consider the request if it directly came from the medical staff. That is expected to be kept private in order to shield the privacy of both Ms. Pike and the staff members tasked with her medical care.

Mr. Spivey said that Ms. Pike still had “a lot of cognitive confusion.”

But, he said, “she is very aware that the state tried to execute her.”
