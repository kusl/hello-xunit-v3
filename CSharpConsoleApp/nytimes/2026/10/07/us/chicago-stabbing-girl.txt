ARTICLE

Fatal Stabbing of a 7-Year-Old Girl Rattles Chicago: ‘That Was a Baby’
The attack has raised questions about whether the suspect was overlooked by authorities despite a history of mental health problems and violent offenses.
By Bill Ruthhart and Robert Chiarito
Published 2026-10-07 20:27 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/07/us/chicago-stabbing-girl.html

Tiera Whalum sat in her parked car in Chicago on Saturday morning, waiting for a hair appointment. Outside her window, she saw a woman waving her arm while approaching a pair of young sisters who were holding hands while walking down the sidewalk. Then one girl screamed.

“I thought she was swatting away a bee or a bug from the girl,” Ms. Whalum, 30, said of the woman. “But she was actually stabbing her, and she walked away like nothing happened.”

Eddimar Hernandez Chacon, 7, lay on the ground with multiple wounds to her back. As the girl’s 17-year-old sister sobbed and bystanders subdued the attacker, Ms. Whalum and a police officer took turns administering CPR to the girl. But they couldn’t save her.

Days later, the brazen and unprovoked killing of a child in broad daylight continues to rattle Chatham, a neighborhood on Chicago’s South Side. The authorities have said there is no evidence that the girl was targeted. But the attack has raised questions about whether the woman charged with her murder — Shavon Gayden, 48 — had been overlooked by the criminal justice and health care systems despite a history of violent offenses and mental health problems.

“Obviously, this woman had mental health issues to do something so heinous to a child just walking down the street with her sister,” said Roderick Sawyer, a longtime Chatham resident who represented the area in the City Council for 12 years. “I’ve not seen this kind of random violence in Chatham, and I’ve been here all my life.”

The stabbing has also renewed attention to violent crime in Chicago, at a time when Mayor Brandon Johnson, a Democrat, is proclaiming amid his re-election campaigning that the streets are getting safer.

Chicago is on pace this year to record fewer homicides than last year’s 60-year low. Even so, President Trump, who for years has cast the city as a den of violence, on Tuesday again raised the idea of federal intervention, citing “a particularly violent crime wave that is getting worse by the day.” He did not mention the girl’s death.

Mayor Johnson told reporters on Sunday that while the city was working to eliminate all violence, “anytime a tragedy happens to a young person, it’s that much more egregious.”

Ms. Gayden, who has been charged with first-degree murder, remained hospitalized on Wednesday with a head injury she sustained when bystanders chased her down. She has not yet appeared in court.

Prosecutors said at a court hearing on Monday that Ms. Gayden first threatened a store clerk on Cottage Grove Avenue, a busy commercial thoroughfare. Soon after exiting the shop, she approached the girls and pulled a knife out of a pumpkin-themed bag, they said. The older sister ducked, and Ms. Gayden pulled Eddimar to the ground and stabbed her multiple times in the back, the police and prosecutors said.

Over the past decade, Ms. Gayden has twice faced charges of domestic violence, but the cases were dropped, court records show. In 2024, she pleaded guilty to felony aggravated battery for threatening to stab a bus driver and was sentenced to two years’ probation, according to prosecutors and court records.

In 2025, she pleaded guilty to misdemeanor battery for striking a police officer and an employee of a homeless shelter where she was staying, prosecutors and court records say.

A warrant was issued for Ms. Gayden’s arrest in February 2025 after she violated the terms of her probation in the case involving the bus driver. She went on to appear in court four times for the 2024 homeless shelter case but was never arrested under the warrant, court records show.

It is unclear whether Ms. Gayden would have been held in jail if she had been arrested — and if so, for how long. A judge ordered her release less than two months after her arrest at the homeless shelter, according to the Cook County Sheriff’s office.

“She had a lot of mental health challenges over the years,” Ms. Gayden’s mother, Christine Gayden, said in a brief phone interview on Tuesday. “I went through hot water trying to get her help.”

After the stabbing on Saturday, bystanders knocked Ms. Gayden unconscious, the police said.

Ms. Whalum said Ms. Gayden tried to fight off the bystanders with a knife but was overwhelmed, including by a man who swung a set of jumper cables.

Ms. Whalum, who is a nursing student, said she jumped out of her car and applied pressure to Eddimar’s wounds with a jacket and a blanket before assisting with CPR.

“It’s really, really heartbreaking,” Ms. Whalum said, adding: “That was a baby. She didn’t even get to live her life.”

On Tuesday, a makeshift memorial of candles, stuffed animals, flowers and balloons lay on the patch of sidewalk where Eddimar was attacked in Chatham, a neighborhood long considered a bedrock of the city’s Black middle class.

Two men who stopped said they had previously seen Ms. Gayden behaving erratically.

Jimmy King, 72, said a woman he squeezed by in a crowded supermarket aisle about a month ago hit his head twice, knocking him to the ground. He declined to report the incident to the police, he said. When he saw Ms. Gayden on TV after her arrest, he recognized her as the woman who had struck him.

“I said, ‘Oh man, I didn’t know she was on probation,’” Mr. King said. “If I would have said something, maybe they would have locked her up and this wouldn’t have happened.”

Traevon Wiliams, 34, said he came across Ms. Gayden two months ago at the University of Chicago Hospital. Ms. Gayden screamed vulgarities at him and his daughter’s mother, who later recognized her on social media after her arrest.

Most of the focus at the memorial, however, was on Eddimar. Tiger Thompson said he had visited the site two or three times a day since her death. The girl’s mother works for him as a construction laborer on drywall and painting projects, he said.

“She’s an excellent mom. She works and takes care of her kids, and that’s why I keep coming here and crying,” said Mr. Thompson, 55, who lives in suburban Dolton, Ill. Mr. Thompson said he had recently driven Eddimar and her siblings to school.

“They left a bunch of candy under my seat, and I was fussing at them about it. She said, ‘I’ll get it, I’ll get it,’” Mr. Thompson recalled.

He said he believed that Eddimar’s tragic death showed the need for more mental health resources.

“If they give people the mental health they need,” he said, “a lot of this can be prevented.”

Robert Chiarito reported from Chicago.
