ARTICLE

Far, Far Away
Making a home on the world’s most remote inhabited island.
By Nick Schönfeld and Julia Gunther
Published 2026-10-25 04:01 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/25/world/africa/tristan-da-cunha-island.html

To reach Tristan, roughly halfway between Africa and South America, Kelly Green crossed 1,738 miles of the South Atlantic from Cape Town aboard the M.V. Edinburgh, a 205-foot lobster boat. She had never been to sea before, and she worried about seasickness, but it never came.

After a week at sea, the ship dropped anchor off Calshot Harbour, in front of Tristan da Cunha’s only settlement — Edinburgh of the Seven Seas. Its two small piers, jutting into the water, looked to Kelly like arms reaching out to welcome her.

Kelly could see most of the island’s residents (then 264 people) waving, including her mother and father, Marina and Sean Burns. Her father had been sent to the island, a British Overseas Territory, to serve as its administrator. He persuaded his reluctant 22-year-old daughter to visit, though Kelly, living in Britain, was more interested in clubs than remote islands. Kelly remembers him saying: “You have to come out, because you’ll never get the chance again.”

Among the men who helped carry her bags off the boat was Shane Green, 21. They met again that night at the island’s only pub. They danced.

Kelly went home after six weeks. The first night back in Cape Town, South Africa, where most ships leaving Tristan travel to, her hotel phone rang. It was Shane. They talked every night for months.

That was in 2012. Two years and several visits later, Kelly made the decision that would change her life: She moved to Tristan and became the first woman in over a century to settle there for good. The last had been three Irish sisters, in 1908.

Tristan was first sighted in 1506 by Tristão da Cunha, a Portuguese naval commander. Britain annexed the island in 1816 and set up a garrison for a year. A Scottish corporal stayed behind with his wife, their two children and two English stonemasons. That was the beginning of the community that lives there today.

Only 220 people now call it home, down from a peak of 300 in 1993. In the past three years, there have been 21 deaths and only six births. A few islanders have emigrated over the years.

Before settling on Tristan, Kelly’s life had been defined by travel. As a diplomat’s daughter, she had moved every few years — Antigua, Bangladesh, Senegal, Kenya — then became a flight attendant. Shane had never lived anywhere but Tristan. Where Kelly could describe the color of the sand on a beach in Mombasa, Shane knew every cove and gulch of his island and could explain, in great detail, the safest way from Green Hill to the beach called Down-Where-The-Minister-Landed-His-Things.

Part of what made it easier was that she and Shane, despite their very different lives, grew up watching the same British TV shows, which Kelly’s father had taped and brought to the island.

Living on an isolated island requires residents to be rather social — to pitch in for the common good, wear multiple hats, work long days and make their own fun.

Before Kelly moved in with Shane, he took her to dig potatoes, clean tripe and gut fish. He was impressed by how quickly she adapted. She said, “If I didn’t want to get my fingernails dirty, we wouldn’t have worked out.”

When Kelly steps outside to hang laundry, she can see the vast expanse of the ocean stretch out before her. To her right lie the remnants of the 1961 volcanic eruption, a vast lava wall nearly 300 feet high that hems in Edinburgh, which residents call “the Settlement,” on its eastern side. Behind her, a giant cliff face closes off the sky above.

There is little of the ambient hum that fills most inhabited places — no traffic or electrical thrum. Even the diesel generators that power much of the Settlement are only audible up close. At times it is quiet enough to hear the rush of wind under an albatross’s wings, or the echo of a tumbling rock kicked loose by grazing sheep.

When winter storms hit, the only sounds are the roar of the wind and the window-rattling boom of waves exploding against the cliffs. Salt spray fills the air and eventually streaks everything with rust.

Most mornings, Kelly bakes bread and packs lunches before leaving by half past eight for her job as the island’s Head of Tourism.

At work, she might deal with a private yacht mooring offshore, sending her on a run of chores and errands — arranging tours, ferrying grateful guests to and from showers and washing their clothes. The next day might be spent fielding media questions, writing posts for the island’s social media accounts, or, on Wednesdays, serving lunch for a small group of expatriates.

On fishing days, Shane is at his boat before dawn with Steve, tying frozen bait to traps by the light of their headlamps. They’ll stay out all day and return around six in the evening, to find their daughters waiting with a cooler of homemade mutton pies and cans of soda.

Tristanian children have unusually free rein on the island. In summer, Savanna spends a lot of time at the community pool across the road from the pub, running around with school friends, or dancing in the same hall where Kelly and Shane first danced, and their parents before them.

The farthest Kelly and Shane travel regularly is to the Patches, a cluster of small stone-walled plots two and a half miles west of the Settlement, where Tristanians have grown potatoes, pumpkins and cabbages for more than a hundred years.

The family’s 24 “patches” sit inside a mosaic of low volcanic stone walls and wire fencing, built to keep the island’s cows out. Antarctic terns nest in the cracks of the walls and divebomb anyone who strays too close.

Leaving Tristan is difficult, and getting back is even harder. The two ships that call regularly carry just twelve berths each, with priority given to government officials and islanders needing medical treatment. Only the SA Agulhas II, a South African research vessel, which calls at Tristan once a year, has more spaces, but those fill up quickly too.

A vacation abroad is nearly impossible: Kelly’s family alone would take four of those spaces. The only other chance is a passing cruise ship, and those cabins cost more than most islanders can afford.

Kelly last left the island in 2023, for her father’s funeral. Sean had suffered a series of strokes while serving an unexpected third term as administrator on the island. No ship was close enough to reach Tristan quickly, so the RV Meteor, a German research vessel working in the region, was dispatched. It took several days to arrive.

Sean was carried aboard, and the ship immediately turned for Cape Town. Although his wife, Marina, was able to accompany him, there was not enough room for Kelly.

Sean died during the crossing, somewhere in the South Atlantic. Kelly has the exact coordinates tattooed on her left arm.

“Raising a family here is amazing,” Kelly said, but she always reminds Savanna and Seren that there is also a world beyond Tristan. “There is life to experience before you decide what you want to do.”

Shane wants his children to study off the island if they choose to, but he is just as intent on teaching them to splice rope, plant potatoes, or catch fish from the beach — skills they can fall back on if they decide to stay.
