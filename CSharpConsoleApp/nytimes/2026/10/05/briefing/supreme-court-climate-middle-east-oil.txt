The Supreme Court spent the opening hours of its new term hearing a complex dispute over whether local governments can hold oil companies responsible for the damages of climate change. It was not clear from the arguments if either side had the upper hand, in part because Justice Samuel Alito — who owns oil company stock — recused himself.

The legal fight began eight years ago, when officials in Boulder, Colo., sued two oil companies, Exxon Mobil and Suncor Energy, claiming that they should be liable for a share of the costs for disasters like droughts, fires and floods. A win for Boulder would likely clear the way for dozens of similar lawsuits, together demanding billions of dollars in damages. A win for the companies could doom those efforts.

During the arguments today, justices across the ideological spectrum posed skeptical questions to the oil companies. The liberal justices seemed likely to side with Boulder; Justice Elena Kagan compared this case to earlier tobacco and opioid lawsuits. The conservatives were more receptive to the companies’ arguments, though Boulder needs only one of their votes to prevail because of Alito’s recusal.

Attacks in the Persian Gulf renew energy supply fears

At least 11 tankers have been attacked in and around the Strait of Hormuz over the last week, including four in the last two days. The strikes are a reminder that the recent rebound in oil exports from the Middle East is a result of fragile workarounds and successful U.S. military protection, not a cessation of violence.

In fact, the regional conflict appeared to deepen over the weekend, when the Saudi-backed government of Yemen announced a major military campaign against the Iran-backed Houthi militia.

At a speech today in London, the chief executive of Saudi Arabia’s national oil company said that the world’s oil stockpiles had fallen from 10 billion barrels when the Iran war started to six billion barrels — a number he called “scarily thin.”

Nobel Prize week has begun

Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine today for developing a tool to switch on specific brain cells with pulses of light. Their pioneering work in optogenetics has led to functional maps of the brain, and may one day lead to a treatment for blindness. Here’s what to know about it.

The other five Nobel Prizes will be announced later this week, for work in physics, chemistry, economic sciences, literature and peace. This year, there were 287 candidates for the peace prize.

When gurning, you can’t hold back

In the sport of gurning, competitors stick their heads through a horse collar and distort their faces as much as possible. The more grotesque your grimace, the better. Inspired, my colleague Rob Tanner, a soccer reporter at The Athletic, took part in this year’s World Gurning Championships.

The competition, in a remote area of England, is tightly contested. One participant was able to pull his bottom lip to touch his nose after practicing three to four hours a day. But Rob was not dissuaded: He went up there, gave it his best shot, and nearly passed out. Here’s his story.
