ARTICLE

Who’s Winning the Race for Congress?
It’s Oct. 5 — 29 days away from the midterms. Here’s the state of play.
By Lisa Lerer and Shane Goldmacher
Published 2026-10-05 09:03 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/05/us/politics/whos-winning-the-race-for-congress.html

“It’s a five-alarm fire.” A “slow moving car crash.” Republicans are “hemorrhaging” and “should brace for losing everything.”

Republicans have been full of doom in recent days, as they confront the reality that their political prospects do not seem to be improving. And their candidates are rapidly running out of time for a turnaround.

With less than 30 days before the election, Democrats are reaching deeper into traditionally conservative areas while Republicans find themselves fighting to save their incumbents.

Decisions last week by Republicans to pull money out of the North Carolina Senate race and divert resources to Kansas demonstrated the depth of their party’s challenge, as new New York Times/Siena University polls found strength for Democrats in five reliably Republican states that could determine control of the chamber.

The first events in what President Trump has promised will be a series of midterm appearances — all in deep red states — underscored the anxiety among Republicans that even some of their most loyal voters could stay home from the polls in November.

And a series of economic indicators released last week, showing job growth slowing, mortgage rates rising and unemployment climbing, only reinforced the difficulties of the national environment for Republicans.

To be sure, a wave of red cash could save Republican seats. But it’s also clear that Democrats are playing hard on their turf.

The Battle for the Senate

Republicans have made some significant moves on the Senate map.

The Senate Leadership Fund, the main Republican Senate super PAC, pulled money from North Carolina, a sign that some top party strategists doubt that Michael Whatley can defeat former Governor Roy Cooper in the perennial battleground state. Even Trump, who tapped Whatley, the former Republican National Committee chairman, to run for the seat, said he was “worried about him” on Friday before leaving for his campaign swing.

The group made its first investment in Kansas, where Senator Roger Marshall, a Republican seeking a second term, appears increasingly embattled. The more than $10 million in ad reservations comes as both sides increasingly view Kansas as a late-breaking battleground. Democrats are considering spending there to support Adam Hamilton, a Methodist pastor and Democratic challenger.

In Texas, Republican spending smashed records as the party frantically tried to boost Ken Paxton, the scandal-tarred attorney general running for Senate. This week, Trump will host a rally in San Antonio — his second trip to Texas in two weeks, and an indication of the party’s worry about a seat it has held since 1961.

The Race for the House

The House map keeps growing and growing for Democrats.

The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee added eight districts to its list of seats to flip on Friday, pushing the total number of competitive districts for the party to 66. That’s nearly double the number the party ended the 2024 cycle with, the committee said.

The committee expanded its electoral frontier deeper into Republican areas, adding races that included seats in rural Virginia, Oklahoma and a Houston-area district that was redrawn to favor Republicans. All eight seats were considered safely Republican when the midterm campaign began in earnest at the start of the year.

The expansion comes a week after The Cook Political Report shifted 15 races toward Democrats, creating three new “tossup” districts, in conservative swaths of North Carolina, Texas and Florida.

Inside the Air Wars

The campaign arm of House Democrats is planning to spend $13 million in the coming week in 35 House districts, according to an ad-buy plan shared with The New York Times.

The spending includes some new districts, most notably Pennsylvania’s First Congressional District, a seat held by Representative Brian Fitzpatrick, one of the only Republicans seeking re-election in a seat that President Trump lost in 2024. The other new districts are California’s 48th, Michigan’s Fourth and Seventh, and New York’s 19th.

Of the races the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee is spending in, most are on offense. The party is spending on 21 seats currently held by Republicans and 14 held by Democrats.

One Key Metric

The average 30-year, fixed-rate mortgage, the most popular home loan in the United States, rose to 7.28 percent, according to the mortgage financing giant Freddie Mac. That’s the highest since November 2023.

Top Talker

Strategists on both sides of the aisle were buzzing after a report on Thursday that Paxton told a group of conservative donors and lobbyists that Trump’s midterm convention in Dallas had hurt him politically.

“Do you feel like he’s a little bit of a drag?” Paxton is heard being asked in an audio recording obtained by The New York Times.

Paxton responded: “So to be honest with you, the numbers — when we did that convention — it dropped our numbers. Everybody’s numbers dropped. Right now, yeah, not good.”

What captivated Republicans about the report was simple: Paxton, a longtime Trump loyalist, said aloud what they were all whispering but didn’t dare say for fear of provoking the president.

Paxton just had the misfortune of being caught on tape.

One Poll Number

84 percent.

That’s the percentage of independent voters who disapprove of Trump’s handling of gas prices, according to the NYT/Siena poll released on Saturday — a figure that has alarmed Republicans.

On the Ground

Hispanic voters along the border are voicing frustration with the president’s approach to immigration and the economy, raising questions about whether Republicans could lose districts in South Texas that the party redrew to tilt in their favor.

Ariel Salinas, the owner of a construction company in McAllen, Texas, blamed the president’s immigration crackdown for his inability to reliably find laborers for his work crews.

“Years ago, you put a fence up because people were stealing stuff. Now you put the fence up because ICE is stealing the workers. There was even a fence shortage in the summer. It caught all of us off guard,” he said.

Quotes of the Week

“It is really obvious to me that, at this very moment, Ted Cruz is running for president. At this moment, I’m not. I’m trying to help elect Republicans. That’s what I’m going to keep on doing.” — Vice President JD Vance

“In my experience, people engage in political bomb-throwing when they’re feeling insecure. So he’s welcome to make whatever comments he likes. I’m going to keep focusing on the midterms and helping us win on Election Day in the next 30 days.” — Senator Ted Cruz

Vance and Cruz, two potential presidential candidates, engaged in a bit of early 2028 sniping last week, trading barbs about their midterm campaign schedules. Vance suggested that Cruz was more focused on laying the groundwork for a 2028 primary bid than helping Republicans win the coming midterm elections. Two days later, Cruz shot back, brushing off Vance’s jab.
