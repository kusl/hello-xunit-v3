New Leaders Emerge for 2028

Live from Brooklyn, Michelle Cottle, Jamelle Bouie and David French weigh in on the “blue tsunami.”

Nov. 5, 2026

By Jamelle BouieMichelle Cottle and David French

Produced by Derek Arthur

A rout. A tsunami. A landslide. Whatever you call it, Democrats won big in the midterm elections. What happens next?

Our politics round table regulars — the national politics writer Michelle Cottle and the columnists Jamelle Bouie and David French — were at the Brooklyn Public Library on Thursday for a live taping of “The Opinions.” They unpacked the results and looked ahead to what they mean for the next two years, the future of both parties and the 2028 presidential race.

This discussion was recorded live. Panelists’ remarks were not fact-checked by The Times.

Below is a transcript of an episode of “The Opinions.” We recommend listening to it in its original form for the full effect. You can do so using the player above or on the NYTimes app, Apple, Spotify, Amazon Music, YouTube, iHeartRadio or wherever you get your podcasts.

The transcript has been lightly edited for length and clarity.

They’d just kind of given up on it.

Bouie: Right.

Cottle: Oh, yeah.

French: Ever got.

Cottle: I think, though, the top of the list for them is going to be a stock trading ban.

Cottle: Well, see, these are all things that are part of their first 100 days agenda, which they’re saying they’re going to roll out.

French: Yeah. And you might get Republican buy-in on stripping the tariff power. That’s one where you’re going to put right to Republicans, perhaps, the most difficult, an incredibly difficult vote, right at the moment where, for the first time in 10 years, the greater threat to their career is the general election voter than it is Donald Trump. And so now they’ve seen what’s happened, and a lot of these senators are in less “safe” seats than the senators from South Carolina and Texas and Mississippi were, and strip that tariff power. Tariffs are deeply, deeply unpopular. They have made things more expensive for people. People are suffering right now, and that would be a fast way to move to deal with where people live.

Cottle: Well, that is one of the things you’re seeing with Jeffries talking about his first 100 days rollout, that they’re looking for things that unify the caucus. And this is a big caucus. It’s diverse; it’s sprawling. So they have intentionally looked for things on the affordability issue, on the Iran war, on the tariffs, that they think will not cause divisions. And they might get a Republican or two.

French: Here’s one: Rescue Ukrainian civilians from Russian missiles.

Bouie: Put Stephen Miller on the rack. Just put him on there.

Cottle: Which does bring up the accountability thing, and we all assume the top of the to-do list for any kind of investigation is Pete Hegseth. Yes, we think that that’s it?

Bouie: Well, I mean, Hegseth is the lowest-hanging fruit. Like, he’s bad at his job.

Cottle: In so many ways.

Bouie: Very bad at his job.

Cottle: Family show. Family show.

Bouie: Personally unpleasant, and like, has not done himself any favors. And I do think Thomas Massie has an impeachment resolution. I think there’s a chance for a Democratic-led impeachment of Hegseth in the next Congress.

Cottle: Yeah, in the next Congress.

Bouie: And I do think you would get 11 Republicans to vote for removal.

French: I think of that with Hegseth and Blanche. And the reason why I single out the Pentagon and the D.O.J., these are the two executive agencies that we most depend on to exercise independent judgment. Of course, a president gets to set policy priorities for the D.O.J., and of course for the Pentagon, of course for the United States military. But it is imperative to our republic that the law enforcement agency is not a politicized, sycophantic entity that prosecutes political opponents. And it is imperative for our national security that the military is not a political military. I was talking to some of the flag officers, the generals and admirals who were fired by Hegseth, and I want this to be very clear.

People don’t recognize this. They were fired for following orders. They did not create the D.E.I. programs that they were fired for. That was a political priority of the Biden administration. They were fired for implementing the orders of their civilian bosses. If you create that as a pattern in the military, you gut the military. You gut it. Because what’s next? Is the next Democratic president going to come in and fire everyone who followed Pete Hegseth’s orders? No, bad idea. Bad idea.

Now, if they followed a plainly illegal order, do that. Like, that’s the law. But if you’re going to fire officers for following the orders of the civilian commander in chief, you are destroying our military. And so these two men, I think they should be impeached, convicted and barred forever from serving in federal office, and I think that is one of the only ways that we can start sending a message now that these agencies have obligations to the Constitution and the American people, not just to Donald Trump.

Cottle: All right, wait.

Bouie: No. I’m just going to say, real quick, just this — very short. I think this is also worth doing because it adds to this showing the public that you’re doing things, right? Like, it’s a very easy thing to say, “Hey, we fired this guy,” and it’s an immediate thing to the public. We have accomplished something.

Cottle: OK. Yeah. So as much as I would like to spend hours and hours talking about the downfall of Pete Hegseth and Todd Blanche, perhaps I figure we, at this point now, have to look to the future of the Republican Party. The terrible, horrible, no good, very bad day they’ve had, and what that means and what the results are and the response. And I want to just, for giggles, start with the vice president’s response to this monumental development, which is: It’s just the war. That’s it. That’s all. And he didn’t like the war, so you can’t blame him for it, right? So what do you make of this, and where do you think that’s going to land him? Where is this going? David, I feel we should start with you.

French: I think the second biggest loser of the night, besides Donald Trump, was JD Vance. I think that when you’re looking at a man — and this is absolute cope. One of the most hilarious things that I read was this — across MAGA Twitter — “We lost the midterms because of neocons.” And I’m like, you spent 10 years purging every last neocon you could find.

Cottle: Yeah, like all five neocons cost them, yeah.

Bouie: Notably, one of the most famous neocons alive, Bill Kristol, is like a resist-lib now.

French: Right, exactly.

Cottle: Liz Cheney.

French: So this is massive cope, when the reality was, if you look at the approval ratings, they were plummeting before the Iran war. They were absolutely — it was the ICE crackdowns. It was tariffs. It was all of the chaos of just the Donald Trump persona. Everything was going down, down, down. And I would say a defeat of this magnitude is about the only thing at this point, after 10 years of Trump. And it wasn’t just 10 years of Trump; it was 10 years of building a cult around the man. A level of zeal and dedication that I never saw around Ronald Reagan. I certainly never saw it around H.W. Bush. He only had one term. Never saw it around W. Bush. Never saw that cult of personality. So the effort to rebuild the Republican Party was always going to be extraordinarily difficult because of that cultish nature around Trump and MAGA.

But now I’m already beginning to see a lot of voices who were quieter — a lot of people who had been, frankly, cowed and intimidated — are now beginning to say to those who are going to that old, deeply insulting playbook, that old personal attack, vicious cruelty: Stop it. Stop it. You’re like Biff from “Back to the Future.” We’re sick of it, you know? And so, I think that the sheer extent of the defeat — at this point, 10 years in — is the only thing that actually gives me hope for green shoots of renewal. Because the bottom line, guys, I know there’s probably only about 49 percent of you who are Republican in here. But ——

Cottle: You can raise your hand if you want.

French: But we really do need two good, functioning parties. In a two-party democracy, you have to have two good, functioning parties, because what ends up happening is the country then becomes inherently dangerous and unstable in the absence of that. I’m reminded of what Susan Eisenhower, Ike’s granddaughter, told me about his decision to become a Republican — and this is a really interesting and, I think, a story that demonstrates the wisdom of the need of two healthy parties. He was probably more dispositionally Republican than not, but there was a real competition for him. And NATO was already being condemned by the isolationist Republicans. There was already the isolationism that arose after World War I was coming up after World War II.

And Eisenhower had the wisdom and the foresight to realize this system of international security will only work if both parties buy in. And he knew the Republicans wanted him, and he was pretty sure he would win the presidency, and what did that do? That set in — not granite, obviously, because NATO is under question now — in a softer form of stone for decades that was indispensable to the health and welfare and well-being and the peace and prosperity of this country.

And so I think that a wipeout of this kind — now, I totally agree, Reagan Republicanism is not coming back. That was something for a different time in American history. But you can have a conservatism that focuses on civil liberties, that focuses on individual liberty, that focuses on economic freedom, that focuses on character and integrity, that does have and can adapt to the current moment. And I’m going to be writing about this in the future — what could it possibly look like?

But for right now, I would say only a wipeout of this level could lead to a potential renaissance. Otherwise it’s just, like, trying to throw mattresses into the side of the Titanic. You had to have a destruction before there was a renewal, and I hate to say that. I hate that it came to this. I hate it. But I think at this point, one of the reasons why I felt a lightness is because, for the first time in a decade, I felt hope. I actually felt hope that we could have the Republican Party being a party of decency and character and thoughtfulness, and one, in my preference, that really centers around the preservation of the American Constitution and the principles of our founding.

Cottle: Jamelle, did you ——

Bouie: Yeah. I’ll say, Eisenhower came after four F.D.R. terms and the Truman term, so if it takes that long to get a responsible Republican Party back — fine by me, you know?

Cottle: You’re good.

Bouie: I’ll just note, I do think that the next two years — this process, when it begins, it will begin after 2028, right? Because I think the next two years ——

Cottle: You think he has to be gone?

Bouie: I think Trump actually has to be gone, has to be out of power.

French: I agree with that.

Bouie: I think that Vance, who desperately wants to be president, is still going to be trying to find some way to do a synthesis. I do think that, although there are people who immediately kind of threw their hats into the ring on Wednesday. Ron DeSantis gave his press conference. You know, Chris Christie went after Vance on television. You’re seeing people begin to kind of consider their position here. I do think that ’28 is probably going to be a Republican wipeout.

Cottle: OK. I need to talk about this for a minute. So nobody has spent more time slapping Donald Trump than we have over the past decade, but he is a generational talent in terms of appealing to kind of tuned-out political people. Like, if you’re not paying attention to politics, he’s your guy. And so he built this unorthodox coalition that has now been blown apart. So young men are gone, Latinos and the Black constituency that he had pulled together are gone, and the Republican Party has just for years now been whatever he says it is.

So the coalition’s gone. He’s going to be gone. Who’s going to do that? Who’s going to be able to build their own coalition? And I’ll tell you who’s not going to be able to do it: Ron DeSantis.

Bouie: No. DeSantis ——

Cottle: JD Vance. People who can’t order a doughnut without pissing off people in the room.

Bouie: I don’t think this coalition gets rebuilt in two years. I just don’t think that. I don’t think there’s a clear way out, because the depth of the losses this week also means that a bench has been wiped out. Brian Kemp is probably, along with Nikki Haley, probably the two best general election candidates the Republicans have. They can never get through a primary, and importantly, Kemp — he’s just — Georgia is deep blue, right? And so, he doesn’t have that to sell to a Republican ——

Cottle: I was talking to the Georgia operatives and strategists. They were super hoping this new governor candidate was going to help them figure out what to do beyond Trump. Because Kemp was their guy for years — he’s the boy who lived. You challenge Trump and you survive. What do you do now?

French: I’m glad you said Brian Kemp, because conservatives like me, if we’re looking at the landscape, Brian Kemp is the No. 1 name that we come up with for a couple of reasons. One, in a lot of ways, he governed Georgia in a way that was not — he was not given to ideological extremes. He was very interested in economic development and he was focusing like a laser, not on culture war, but on the prosperity of the state of Georgia.

And so when you have that, I think that’s your formula going forward, where you’re not going to be Mr. Culture War striding onto the scene. But you can walk onto the scene and you can say: Here are how conservative ideas can make you better able to buy a house. Here is how conservative ideas will bring more jobs. Because people are, at this point in American history — and you guys feel it, you guys know it — there is just a sense of deep-seated despair and pessimism.

I teach at a Christian college in Nashville, and there are so many wonderful young Christian kids there. And I hear, again and again, “I don’t think I’ll be able to buy a house.” And that just grieves me. You know, you think about my own children and our neighborhood in Nashville. My own children could not, after they graduated — even several years after they graduated from college — could not possibly buy a house. And it’s just Nashville, for crying out loud. Although it’s kind of trendy now. You’ve got to have a tattoo sleeve to move to Nashville, though, Michelle.

Cottle: I got you. That’s not a problem.

French: But I do think that, say, a Brian Kemp figure, or people like that, have an opportunity in the future. I don’t know how they break through in 2028.

Bouie: Yeah.

Cottle: All right. So we are sadly running short of time on this, so I want you to do this not as a lightning round, but I need you to give me your top-line thought on Trump himself for the next two years. What are you expecting?

Bouie: OK. A lightning round? OK. Really quickly. Obviously, I thought he was going to respond to losing with, like: It’s rigged. It’s fraud. No. He was like: You know what? Sometimes you lose.

Cottle: Sometimes that happens.

Bouie: Sometimes it happens.

Cottle: Sometimes it just be that way.

Bouie: He was so demoralized. And I think, actually, that is the dynamic. That he’s just not going to want to be there anymore.

Cottle: Aw. David?

French: If it’s possible, we’re going to hear more about the ballroom.

Bouie: Yeah, yeah.

Cottle: Oh my God. I live in Washington. Every time I cross the bridge to go to the airport, I’m blinded by the newly gilded statues. They are a traffic hazard. I just need him to stop.

Bouie: Oh, you mean the ones by the Lincoln Memorial? Oh, they’re awful.

French: I don’t think there’s much speculation about what we are going to get for the next two years. More Donald Trump. And we know exactly what that is, and that’s one of the reasons why — look, he still has to deal with what’s going on in the Persian Gulf. He is the commander in chief. OK. Sorry. I’m stopping.

Cottle: That’s enough.

French: All right. Sorry.

Cottle: All right. It is time for my favorite part of the show: recommendations. Who wants to go first?

Bouie: I’ll go first. I was joking with Michelle earlier, she asked me if I was going to have a highbrow or lowbrow ——

Cottle: I knew, I knew.

Bouie: Recommendation.

Cottle: I didn’t really have to ask.

Bouie: And I said highbrow.

Cottle: OK. Yeah.

Bouie: After thinking about it for a moment. And then I realized that this is, like, I’m doing a bit, to an extent, but ——

Cottle: Lean in.

Bouie: I’m going to lean into it. A buddy of mine teaches a really interesting class at U. Va., and he had me come in to talk about a movie that I love, and I’ve done this a couple of times. We end up watching this movie every year. It is the movie “Danton,” about the French revolutionary.

Cottle: Oh, sweet Jesus.

Bouie: I can never pronounce the name of the director, so I’m not going to attempt, but it’s a Polish film, a Polish-French production that was released in ’84, anticipating the anniversary of the French Revolution. There’s a funny story about this. The Mitterrand government in France provided money for the production because they were thinking it was going to be a hagiographic film. It turns out it is a somewhat dour and jaded view of human nature kind of film, and the French hated it. They were like, “This is the worst thing that’s ever been made.” But it’s a wonderful movie. It stars noted scumbag Gérard Depardieu ——

Cottle: You’re really selling it hard, bud.

Bouie: As the titular character. But it’s really a movie about political ambition. It’s a movie about, like, revolutionary passions. It’s a movie about the way those things interfere with, like, basic human connection. I find it an immensely compelling film, and I would highly recommend it.

Cottle: David, please do your lowbrow for me.

French: I got you.

Cottle: OK. Hook me up.

French: But I bet this is going to be controversial in this room, because I’m guessing there might be a few “Lord of the Rings” nerds in here, just maybe one or two.

Bouie: Yeah, you have my ax. Go ahead.

French: OK. The next season of “Rings of Power” is coming on Amazon, and I am a giant fan of it. Now, don’t boo me, Tolkien nerds, because, look, we all know this, it covers the Second Age of Middle-earth. We all know that’s 3,441 years.

Bouie: We all know.

French: So if you’re going to be covering 3,441 years of history in, like, a five-season arc, you’re going to have to compress. It’s OK. But it is a beautiful show. I’m just a mega nerd. I love the portrayal of the younger Galadriel. She’s a few thousand years younger than she was in the movies, where she’s more impulsive, just as Tolkien said.

But anyway, it’s really fascinating, and what’s phenomenal about it is that in this will-to-power age, this Machiavellian will-to-power Trumpist age, it is very in keeping with the Tolkien spirit that you do not defeat evil with might. You defeat evil with virtue. And that is shot through the show. It’s really well done. I’m a huge fan. Don’t hate me, but I’m going to be bingeing the heck out of it as soon as it starts.

Cottle: OK, fair enough. So it falls to me to tell you what to do this weekend, which is, as it happens, tomorrow night, “Wild Horse Nine” is opening, Martin McDonagh’s new film. I don’t know if you’ve seen the trailer. I don’t know if you’ve seen the reports from the film festivals.

It is basically John Malkovich, in what may be the role of his lifetime, and Sam Rockwell as two C.I.A. agents in 1973, right around the time of the Chilean coup, who get mysteriously dispatched from Santiago to Easter Island for a weekend. It looks like it’s going to be the most uproarious comedy you’ve ever seen, but then it suddenly turns into — because Malkovich is a genius — this really kind of dark, sad, and also hilarious meditation on death, male friendship, with a lot of big heads, as the ads keep pointing out. So I think it opens tomorrow.

I highly recommend it. Got to see an advanced screening, and I’m going to go again this weekend because I just can’t get enough of it. So there you have it. We have provided you with your weekend entertainment and a lot of bingeing opportunities. And with that, we will basically cede the field now.

Thank you very much, guys. It’s always a pleasure.
