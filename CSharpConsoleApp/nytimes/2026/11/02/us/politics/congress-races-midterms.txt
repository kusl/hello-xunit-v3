ARTICLE

Who’s Winning the Race for Congress?
We’re one day away from the midterms. Here’s the state of play.
By Patricia Mazzei and Shane Goldmacher
Published 2026-11-02 10:03 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/02/us/politics/congress-races-midterms.html

Time for some deep breaths: The midterm elections are one day away.

Democrats appear well positioned to take control of the House. The battle for the Senate remains tight, with polls slightly favoring Democrats in a set of much closer contests that could yield surprises and go well into election night or beyond (looking at you, Alaska).

Much will depend on Election Day turnout, especially for Republicans, who have had a financial advantage throughout the campaign. They have blanketed the airwaves and called and texted their loyal voters to prod them to the polls. Money often wins in American politics.

Some fundamentals have not changed. The Senate battlegrounds are mostly in states that President Trump won in 2024. Many voters dislike or distrust the Democratic Party. The country remains deeply polarized.

Gas prices are high. The Iran war is still going. Midterms tend to hurt the president’s political party, and Trump is more unpopular than he has ever been.

Now, voters get their say.

The Battle for the Senate

Democrats lead Republicans in six of the seven battlegrounds that will determine Senate control, according to the final set of New York Times/Siena University polls released on Sunday.

Democratic candidates are ahead in Kansas, Maine, Michigan, Ohio and Texas, and have a narrow edge in Alaska, the polls found. In Iowa, the seventh battleground, the candidates are effectively tied.

Republicans currently hold all of those states except Michigan. Democrats need to keep all of their seats and flip at least four in Republican hands to win a Senate majority.

The Democrats have a chance in these typical Republican strongholds because Trump and his party have lost the public’s trust on the year’s most salient issue: the economy. Across all seven states in the polls, Democrats had a nine-percentage-point advantage on the cost of living.

Some voters remain undecided. One of the few positives in the polls for Republicans is that voters who decided in the last month favored Republicans.

Still, only in one state, Maine, was the Republican candidate (Senator Susan Collins) viewed more favorably than the Democrat (Troy Jackson).

One perennial question about polls is what if they’re wrong. But our own Nate Cohn crunched the numbers and reports, “Democrats lead by enough that they could win the Senate even if the polls underestimate Republicans by as much as they did in 2016 or 2020.”

The Race for the House

Democrats could win as many as 240 seats in the House, according to the final race ratings by the nonpartisan Cook Political Report. Such a large majority seemed like a long shot at the start of the election year. It would be Democrats’ largest majority since 2009.

Getting there would require Democrats flipping 25 seats, including districts that Trump won comfortably just two years ago. Republicans have spent heavily to defend the seats they hold and compete in battleground districts.

Among the races that the Cook report shifted in Democrats’ favor, citing Republican concerns about low turnout, was the North Carolina district held by Representative Richard Hudson, the chairman of House Republicans’ campaign arm.

The Money

The last three months of the campaign saw an influx of hundreds of millions of dollars, with megadonors playing an outsize role, according to a Times analysis using data from the media tracking firm AdImpact.

The top 20 megadonors have put about $2 billion into federal races throughout the campaign. That amounts to roughly 15 percent of all money spent on political ads this year. Most megadonors lean Republican.

It has been the most expensive midterm election since at least 2018, as measured by political advertising.

Inside the Not-on-the-Air Wars

The video spread quickly on X after it was posted by a local radio host. There was Senator Roger Marshall, the embattled Kansas Republican, defending his pursuit of medical debts as an obstetrician in a 60-second video that looked just like an ad.

“I never had anyone arrested,” Marshall said looking straight into the camera.

One Key Metric

$11.6 billion.

That is what total political advertising is expected to be by Election Day, according to AdImpact. About $5.6 billion of that is flowing to broadcast television, a figure even higher than the $5.3 billion spent on broadcast advertising in the 2024 presidential race.

Top Talker

A dizzying back-and-forth between Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic Senate nominee in Michigan, and Representative Haley Stevens, whom he defeated in the August primary, has consumed the final days of a race that could determine Senate control. An attempt to broker a peace between the two failed spectacularly, and the final split was months in the making.

First, Stevens said she would not campaign with El-Sayed before Tuesday’s election, despite having pledged to vote for him. Her fiancé publicly endorsed El-Sayed’s Republican opponent, former Representative Mike Rogers, and campaigned with him.

Then Stevens reversed course and said she would appear with El-Sayed at a church on Sunday. But she expected him to apologize for comments he had made about Israel and to disavow what she described as an antisemitic ad from an outside group.

El-Sayed, a forceful critic of Israel, responded on Friday that Stevens seemed “intent on relitigating” the primary, which he won by less than a percentage point.

Said Stevens, “I don’t think this is a melodrama.”

On Saturday, El-Sayed said he did not want Stevens to join him after all. On Sunday, Stevens did go to church — but with Rogers. She then endorsed him. Her chief of staff and other aides resigned.

One Poll Number

62 percent.

That’s the share of Texas likely voters who said in the latest Times/Siena poll that they had already voted. James Talarico, the Democratic Senate nominee, has a 10-percentage-point lead over Ken Paxton, the Republican, with the large share of Texans who have already cast their ballot: 53 percent to 43 percent.

Quote of the Week

“When I get to heaven one day, I am just going to choke Lindsey.”

That was Senator Darline Graham, Republican of South Carolina, speaking to conservative radio hosts last Wednesday about her neck-and-neck race against Dr. Annie Andrews, the Democratic Senate nominee. The hosts asked Graham if she was angry at her brother, Lindsey Graham, who died in July, for “putting you in this situation.” (She laughed.)
