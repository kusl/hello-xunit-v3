ARTICLE

Republicans Point Fingers, but Trump Does Not Appear to Be Listening
After last week’s midterm losses, the Republican Party entered its most uncertain moment in years. Some Republicans called for moderation and cleaning house, but that will be difficult with President Trump still in the White House.
By Patricia Mazzei
Published 2026-11-08 10:01 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/08/us/politics/republican-blame-trump-midterms.html

The backlash began with some of the Republican Party’s most ambitious figures.

As soon as the party’s huge losses in last week’s midterm elections became starkly clear, prominent Republicans rushed to provide explanations for the embarrassing rout. Some criticized White House policies — if not President Trump directly — harder than they had in years.

“The reason we lost is the war,” Vice President JD Vance, who had long been considered the leading 2028 Republican presidential contender, wrote in a long statement on X the morning after Election Day. He stumped in nearly a dozen states during the midterm campaign.

“Understandably, people were pissed off, and elected Republicans didn’t do enough to address their very real pain,” Senator Ted Cruz of Texas said on Wednesday on his podcast, where he had spent the previous two years championing Republicans.

That same day, Gov. Ron DeSantis of Florida denounced the party’s slate of candidates.

“They didn’t have a great positive case for themselves — they were flat-footed,” he told reporters, while also bragging that his own state remained a red bastion amid the Democratic surge, though some Republicans won by surprisingly narrow margins.

The rush to point fingers comes as the Republican Party is plunging into perhaps its most uncertain moment in years. For a decade, Mr. Trump has controlled the G.O.P. with an iron grip, remaking Republicanism in his MAGA image with little tolerance for dissenting views. Now, as they worry about what could amount to generational losses for their party, some Republicans are embarking on the earliest of efforts to transform the midterm wipeout into a new path forward.

These fledgling conversations about the future are taking shape against the backdrop of a quietly bubbling 2028 presidential campaign. Mr. Vance, Mr. Cruz and Mr. DeSantis injected themselves into the debate with their early and extensive assessments in no small part because they are considered potential presidential contenders.

They may not be totally in charge of charting their own destinies — or their party’s. Mr. Trump, not even halfway through his four-year term, remains in the White House. He has shown little inclination to change course.

As if to emphasize that, Mr. Trump named Stephen Miller his new chief of staff on Friday, elevating a hard-line adviser who was the architect of his mass deportation effort. Polls showed this was among the issues that had soured voters on the president.

Mr. Miller replaces Susie Wiles. The move signaled that Mr. Trump did not plan to moderate his policies in his remaining two years in office, while some Republicans had made clear since Tuesday that they believed his doing so was the only way forward.

“What the American people told us is that they want a centrist government,” Representative Nicole Malliotakis of New York told CNN.

Mitt Romney, the Republican presidential nominee in 2012, also called for a “course correction, for the country and for my party.” In a post on X on Thursday, he argued that “bad policy, bad character make bad politics.”

Mr. Trump has seemed uninterested in charting a different road. He spent the day after the midterm drubbing showing off his White House renovation projects. On Friday, in addition to promoting one of his most polarizing aides, he endorsed Joe Gruters, the chairman of the Republican National Committee, to remain at the helm.

It was not what some in the party were hoping for.

“If in business all of a sudden you lose that much of your work to your competitor, you’re going to fire people,” Representative Marlin Stutzman of Indiana told Newsmax last week. “I think there should be some heads rolling from the White House right now.”

He added: “We need to start with our own house right now, making sure we have new leadership, new voices.”

Some anti-Trump Republicans hoped for at least one silver lining out of the dismal election results: a return to the G.O.P. that predated the president.

But that party no longer exists, said Mike Madrid, a Republican strategist who co-founded the Lincoln Project, an anti-Trump group.

“Once the party and its infrastructure decided to kind of bend the knee to Trump, that means it kind of gave up its philosophical underpinnings,” he said. “That glue no longer exists. That makes a typical right-wing versus center debate impossible.”

Republican operatives say they are cleareyed about the staggering scope of the party’s losses. They have acknowledged that Democrats nominated quality candidates in traditionally Republican states, including Kansas, South Carolina and Mississippi. In other battlegrounds, Republican voters turned out but did not vote for Republican candidates, a worrying indicator that the party has lost some of its coalition.

In Louisiana, a state that Mr. Trump won by 22 points two years ago, Democrats came within one percentage point of flipping a Senate seat.

In Florida, Representative Mario Diaz-Balart, a 12-term Republican incumbent, claimed victory while leading by less than a point in a race that was never expected to be competitive and remains too close to call, according The Associated Press.

Republicans lost six congressional seats in North Carolina alone — including one held by Representative Richard Hudson, the chairman of House Republicans’ campaign arm.

“Whatever the future holds, I will keep fighting for those principles and for the people who believe in common-sense, conservative policies that will save our country and make the lives of the American people better,” Mr. Hudson wrote in a statement acknowledging his defeat. “In the words of Ronald Reagan: ‘We lost a skirmish, but the battle goes on.’”

Before the presidential race gets underway in earnest, Republicans must elect leaders in the House and Senate, a process that may reveal what direction lawmakers will want to take — as the minority party.

In the immediate days after the midterms, Republicans have shown little appetite for a formal autopsy, a written report detailing the party’s failures. In the past, similar reports in both parties have either gone unheeded or tended to cause infighting that has done little to settle internal debates over the future.

It can be hard to predict what voters will want, often rendering such efforts fruitless. Republicans conducted an extensive autopsy in 2012 after President Barack Obama won re-election. Among its conclusions was that the party should embrace voters of color and women.

Four years later, they nominated Mr. Trump, who followed none of the autopsy’s prescriptions.
