ARTICLE

A Stake Through the Heart of Trump’s Red Map
Realignment is so 2024.
By Ezra Klein
Published 2026-11-08 11:00 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/08/opinion/midterms-democrats-schumer.html

When Donald Trump called the 2024 election “a historic realignment,” he, or whoever wrote those words for him, was referring to something specific. The idea of a realignment comes from a paper by the political scientist V.O. Key Jr. Writing in the 1950s, Key sought to describe a kind of “critical election” that recurred across American politics, but only occasionally. These critical elections had three common features: high voter turnout, a “sharp alteration” of the normal voting patterns in the electorate and, most important, “new and durable” groupings of voters that persist for years to come.

Trump’s 2024 election seemed, to many, to fit the model. Voter turnout was high. Trump’s support among Hispanic voters, Asian voters, Black voters, young voters and working-class voters spiked. This was, for Republicans, a new kind of coalition, and it built on Trump’s 2020 coalition, in which he’d made unexpected gains with some of those same groups, albeit not, then, large enough gains to win.

Tuesday’s repudiation of the Republican Party did more than drive a stake through the heart of Trump’s realignment. It raised the possibility of a dizzying new era in American politics — not one in which the electoral groupings are clear and durable, but rather one in which they are fluid and malleable.

It wasn’t a realignment election; it was a dealignment election: an opportunity for “a generational remaking of the Senate map,” Chuck Schumer, the leader of the Senate Democrats, told me.

The House vote, so far, shows a 15-point swing toward the Democrats from 2024. But the swings are much larger among the groups that supposedly realigned rightward two years ago: a 33-point swing toward Democrats among voters age 18 to 29; a 29-point swing among Hispanic voters; a 19-point swing among voters who didn’t attend college; and an 18-point swing among Asian voters.

These swings, and others like them, were large enough to dissolve the colors on the political map. Democrats bested Republicans in Texas and Georgia, Ohio and Iowa. They won a Senate seat in South Carolina and look likely to pick up another in Mississippi. They have already won more than 250 House seats, which is the largest majority either party has held since 2009. A top House Democratic aide told me he learned the names of some of their winning candidates only on election night.

I don’t know any Democrats who are counting on these voting patterns to automatically persist. What the 2026 midterms offer, rather than durability, is possibility. The election demolished old certainties. Democrats can compete in places they thought they could not. If it is possible for them to win in Kansas and Iowa this year, well, why not in 2028, when those states will again see Senate elections — alongside Florida, Alaska, South Carolina, and many more?

“We need to show the people in these states that Democratic senators can actually help them,” Schumer said. “And that’s a two-way street. Those senators need to instruct us on what issues matter most in their states. So now we have an opportunity in these states to learn what might work — and see if what might work in Kansas might work in Nebraska, if what might work in Mississippi might work in Alabama. We have to pay very close attention to that.”

To Schumer, this moment is reminiscent of when the Democrats began to realize that the Southwest, once a Republican stronghold, was winnable: “When we started out, the big four states of Arizona, Colorado, Nevada and New Mexico had six or seven Republicans, and now they have eight Democrats. We learned how to do that.”

A Democratic Party that won or came close in states where it hadn’t been competitive in decades will pursue them again and others like them — Democrats lost the U.S. Senate contest in Louisiana by less than two percentage points — with renewed force. Candidates who might not have considered running when victory seemed impossible may take another look.

It is hard to achieve what you don’t consider possible. “So many party leaders and donors told Gina it couldn’t happen,” Rebecca Katz, one of the founders of Fight Agency, whose clients included Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa and Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed, told me. “We almost didn’t win Texas because a lot of people didn’t believe we could.”

But now they know they can. This is the promise of dealignment: a country in which the parties take fewer voters for granted and compete more seriously and creatively in more places. Democrats won this year running seminarians and pastors and pediatricians. They won running D.S.A. members and prosecutors, even a paralympian and a crooner. “To win in the future, we need to be curious,” Katz said.

Midterms often produce big swings, both because disillusioned voters switch sides and because disaffected or uninterested voters stay home. Presidential elections can bring out a differently shaped, or differently inclined, electorate. Republicans won wave midterms in 1994 and 2010 only to bobble the presidential contest two years later. Democrats would err if they interpreted the election as a fulsome endorsement of their party.

“Midterm voters viewed the Democratic Party more unfavorably than favorably, including even a significant share of people who voted for Democrats,” Frances Lee, a political scientist at Princeton, told me. “This looks like an election where the out party was the vehicle for repudiating the party in power. The exit polls point toward an election that was a verdict on the administration: on prices, tariffs, corruption and the war with Iran. Rejecting the party in power is not the same as endorsing the new majority’s program.”

But the Democratic Party emerging out of the 2026 midterms is fundamentally different from the Democratic Party that emerged out of the 2018 midterms. In 2018, there was still a sense that Trump was an aberration or a fluke; that he’d gotten lucky in 2016 and Democrats could double down on who they were already and end MAGA without changing much themselves.

Democrats won power in 2018 and 2020 and wielded it so unconvincingly and alienatingly that Trump and his movement roared back, stronger and more dangerous, than ever. The Democratic Party that emerged from that bruising is chastened in a way its predecessor wasn’t. Democrats know they need to appeal to voters who mistrust them. They are not as liable to mistake Trump’s unpopularity for their popularity. All they have won is a chance to prove themselves. And so they may be more willing to learn lessons that this election had to teach.

“One of the major findings of my research is that geographic polarization is primarily driven by cultural battles,” David Hopkins, a political scientist at Boston College, told me. “Voters in red and blue America differ more on cultural views than on economics. And the 2026 election was overwhelmingly fought on economic grounds. In part, this was because of the affordability crisis putting financial concerns at the top of voters’ minds, but it was also because every faction of the Democratic Party and the larger liberal media/political sphere started talking less about controversial cultural topics and more about economic fairness after 2024.”

I asked Schumer about the tensions Democrats are going to face balancing bills that might lower costs with the manifold other priorities that Democrats hold. “If affordability doesn’t prevail, we’re in trouble,” Schumer told me. “We cannot impose on people what we think is important. We have to listen to what’s important to them and then deliver. The fact that we’ve had such success helps us sell this message. But these senators like Turek and Hamilton and Peltola will help us balance our caucus.”

And Democrats will now be thinking about what it might mean to win another Senate seat in Kansas or rebuild strength in Indiana, where Beau Bayh, a Democrat, just won the election for secretary of state, or break the Republican hammerlock on Florida. That is the gift of a dealignment election, one in which the seemingly durable electoral groupings reveal that they, too, are products of a political moment, and they will be broken in time, and that time may be now, if you are wise enough and creative enough to both lead and listen.

“Texas being competitive is overdue given its demographic composition,” Hopkins said. “The Texas Democratic Party has been basically moribund since the 1990s, and now it has not only a U.S. senator but also a whole cadre of state-level officials to build the party’s governing and organizational strength. Even if Texas Democrats can’t sustain the kind of success they had this week, it’s hard to believe that the state will easily snap back to unchallenged Republican rule in the years ahead.”

The electoral map may be somewhat fixed from year to year, but it is not fixed from generation to generation. Texas was once a Democratic stronghold. California was the political home of both Ronald Reagan and Richard Nixon. Sometimes stasis is less a function of the world around us than of our outdated perceptions.

And this may not be the bottom of Trump’s popularity nor the boundaries of the chaos he might cause. Trump is in his 80s, and his speech and behavior seem ever more discursive and erratic. The true state of his health is opaque. His response to an election in which Republicans hemorrhaged support among Hispanic voters was to … appoint Stephen Miller as his new chief of staff. The extent of his family and his administration’s Brobdingnagian corruption will come into clearer view over the next two years. The war with Iran could worsen, as could Russia’s assault on Ukraine, as could the consequences of A.I. to the economy or national security or human flourishing. None of us knew, days after the 2018 election, that much of the 2020 election would happen amid lockdowns and face masks.

Democrats will have more chances to prove who they are, or who they are becoming. Schumer, for one, is counting on it. “We have an opportunity in these red areas to change what they think a Democrat is,” he told me. “Talarico is a Texas Democrat, not a national Democrat. Turek is an Iowa Democrat, not a national Democrat. Most of what people see as a Democrat is a Fox News caricature. Now we have an opportunity to actually show people in these states that a Democrat is different than what they thought.”
