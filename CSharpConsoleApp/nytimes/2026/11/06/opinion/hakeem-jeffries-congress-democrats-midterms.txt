ARTICLE

He’s the Most Powerful Democrat in Washington. Is He Up to the Job?
He’s the country’s top Democrat now. He needs to act like it.
By Michelle Cottle
Published 2026-11-06 10:01 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/06/opinion/hakeem-jeffries-congress-democrats-midterms.html

There is a cheeky piece of parent-to-teenager advice circulating that goes something like this: Try to stay out of jail — and if you do wind up in jail, establish dominance quickly.

In real life, this is dubious guidance for adolescence or incarceration. When it comes to serving as the speaker of the House, there may be something to it. Especially for Representative Hakeem Jeffries, the New York Democrat poised to claim the gavel in the next Congress. Mr. Jeffries, who has spent nearly the past four years in training as the chamber’s minority leader, is known for his light touch and consensus-oriented style. He is now about to become his party’s most senior official, the first Black speaker in the history of the Republic. To succeed, and to deliver for the American people, he will need to dial up the boss energy as he works to set the legislative agenda, find his footing as President Trump’s chief foil and ride herd on a diverse, restive Democratic majority. Or else the job will ride him into the ground.

His testing has already begun.

“Today begins the great American comeback!” Mr. Jeffries crowed on election night, as it became clear his team was on track to romp. His body language was looser and his smile wider than usual as he ran through Democrats’ opening agenda, including reversing Medicaid cuts and holding the president and his people accountable for “unprecedented corruption.”

It was a compelling to-do list, and one intentionally crafted to unify his caucus in all its ideological and temperamental messiness. To make any progress on his party’s mandate, the speaker has to herd the cats, as they say. It is an ulcer-inducing challenge in ordinary times, much less in the pressure cooker of the late Trump era with an impatient left wing itching for a fight. Mr. Jeffries has already acknowledged that there will — and should — be “a robust family conversation about any given issue.”

So, how will that go? Mr. Jeffries wasn’t built to thrill his party’s rowdy progressives, and their skepticism will be his most immediate challenge. Growing up in the Crown Heights neighborhood of Brooklyn, he was a careful, thoughtful, focused high achiever. And that he remains, an institutionalist rather than a revolutionary. The district he represents, a working-class, predominantly Black part of Brooklyn, is one of the nation’s bluest — but in an old-school, more traditional Democratic vein rather than an anti-establishment, democratic socialist kind of way. A pragmatist, he is not identified with many policy crusades and is uneasy about the rising generation of lefty rabble-rousers. In last year’s New York mayoral primary, his delayed endorsement of Zohran Mamdani royally ticked off progressives.

Several incoming members affiliated with or adjacent to the Democratic Socialists of America have been plotting to squeeze concessions from Mr. Jeffries — on imposing wealth taxes, abolishing ICE and other pet policies — in exchange for supporting him for speaker. Some have been demanding spots on key committees in exchange for their votes, à la the tactics that the House Freedom Caucus used to strong-arm Republican leadership.

Mr. Jeffries’s fat new majority should give him a break in this department, providing him wiggle room to push back against undue demands from any faction. The various cliques, recognizing the new landscape, are said to be preparing to try to play better together, though a bump-free transition is perhaps too much to ask for. The party’s fresh faces and progressive crusaders deserve a seat at the table and to have their priorities taken seriously. But the presumptive speaker should draw the line at stocking powerful positions with his fringiest members, daring insurgents to prove how much support they really have on the floor.

The rest of his caucus will be watching. Mr. Jeffries gets high marks for making people feel heard and understood. He fosters personal relationships with members. He knows what motivates them, what keeps them up at night, how far they can bend, or not, on this or that issue. His leadership style is defined by outreach and patient consensus building. That all lends itself to a common concern — from voters, his fellow members and even some major donors — is that he is too risk-averse, too deliberate, not aggressive enough for this political moment.

In September, two Democratic moderates, Jared Golden and Marie Gluesenkamp Perez, surprised their caucus by voting to advance a package of Republican-backed bills. This was the second time in three weeks they had defected, despite Mr. Jeffries and his leadership team issuing a warning after the first time. Mr. Jeffries has yet to dole out punishments.

Worse, the Democratic leader was apparently caught off guard by the defections, which fueled grumbling that his vote-counting operation was weak. This can be mildly embarrassing when you are in the minority. In the majority, it can prove disastrous. Once you start losing floor votes, as Speaker Mike Johnson can attest, you can quickly lose your grip on the chamber. Nancy Pelosi’s policy was to never hold a vote unless she knew she had the votes to win.

Mr. Jeffries should show early that he’s willing to use sticks as well as carrots. He can cut individual members slack for deviating from the party line on issues crucial to their districts. But he should tolerate no surprises, making clear that anyone who fails to communicate with him will pay a price: losing committee assignments, having legislative priorities shelved, getting frozen out of decisions, missing out on the piles of campaign cash controlled by the caucus leadership. And if people pull stunts on the level of the departing representative Haley Stevens, who issued an abrupt, 11th-hour endorsement of the Republican candidate in Michigan’s hotly contested Senate race, then heads should roll — metaphorically, of course. Kicking her out of the Democratic caucus seems perfectly reasonable.

Even with a divided government, Mr. Jeffries will be expected to produce concrete results. Checks on Mr. Trump and his minions. Passing vital legislation, such as for funding the government. Even with bills that cannot get past the Senate, much less Mr. Trump, House passage will make clear Democrats’ priorities while forcing Republican members to take a stand on tough issues. If the new speaker spends too much time chasing consensus, weighing options, deferring to committee chairs, special interest caucuses and other actors, the whole process will get hopelessly bogged down.

Democrats are nursing two years of pent-up frustration and energy now primed to erupt in a frenzy of competing demands and priorities. Central to channeling this energy, Mr. Jeffries must balance the two sides of the party’s dual mandate: advancing popular policies and holding Mr. Trump and his people to account.

Confronting a president as incompetent and ethically challenged as this one, the new majority will be under pressure to put everyone from cabinet officials to Trump family members under the microscope. With Democrats also taking control of the Senate, expect impeachment fever to rise. Mr. Jeffries will need to establish priorities. Some of the most emotionally charged investigations, such as digging into self-enrichment by the Trump clan, may not resonate as broadly as focusing on administration officials accused of, for instance, unauthorized military action.

Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth is assumed to be near, if not at, the top of the Democrats’ impeachment list — an eminently sensible choice. A Republican House member, Thomas Massie, has already introduced a resolution to this end, though he lost his primary and is on his way out of the chamber. By contrast, pursuing a third impeachment of the president, while cathartic, would feel like pure political theater. There is no chance of conviction in the narrowly divided Senate, the blowback from MAGA world would be next level, and the entire circus would allow Mr. Trump to play the victim. Again. Mr. Jeffries should direct his caucus toward better uses of its time and energy.

Promoting a Democratic legislative agenda must remain central as well. Mr. Jeffries is charting a sensible course so far. The party’s first 100 days agenda will focus on restoring Affordable Care Act premium subsidies and Medicaid funding cut in Mr. Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill, ending the Iran war and curtailing the president’s tariffs. These are all core concerns for a majority of Americans — unlike, say, restoring transgender rights and protections, as some Democrats are raring to do.

Things could get bumpy as moderates from swing districts urge Mr. Jeffries to find areas of agreement with the president, while others in the caucus maintain that any gesture of cooperation would be a sellout. Some Democrats were upset with Mr. Jeffries in August after he met privately in New York with Jared Kushner, Mr. Trump’s son-in-law, to discuss possible points of cooperation. There’s room for finesse. Mr. Jeffries can keep the door open to deals on Americans’ top priority — affordability — while continuing to hammer the administration on other issues. His relations with Mr. Trump are frosty. To have any prayer of progress, Mr. Jeffries will need to make skillful use of communication back channels — he and Secretary of State Marco Rubio are on warmer terms — as well as public pressure.

Which brings us to another piece of the job Mr. Jeffries needs to work on: his public salesmanship. He has the raw talent. His first high-level leadership post was as a co-chair of the Democratic Policy and Communications Committee. He can give a good speech and deliver a zippy turn of phrase. He knows the importance of keeping his messaging clear and simple. But off the cuff, he often sounds unnatural and overly programmed. He desperately needs to loosen up, stray from his talking points, venture onto riskier podcasts, get younger staff and colleagues to help up his social media game, embrace the blinding spotlight he is about to step into. In an attention economy, up against the Trump megaphone, he must adapt or fail.

Following Ms. Pelosi, the most ruthlessly effective speaker of modern times, will make Mr. Jeffries’s new job even harder. The Pelosi comparisons are already inescapable — and largely unfair. For all her gifts, Ms. Pelosi did not start out as an untouchable badass. She made plenty of missteps. And she weathered plenty of attacks from her own troops — for being too compromised, too easily caricatured, too autocratic, too old — including a coup attempt or two. She was also seen as a meh messenger for much of her time in office, her delivery and smile too stiff to wow the masses. But tough fights often brought out the best in her, and her ability to get the best of Mr. Trump in their interactions made her a legend.

Like Ms. Pelosi, Mr. Jeffries may well rise to meet the moment. He will have to find his own way forward — with policy, with his people, with the president. This will require leavening his inherently cautious nature, edging even further outside his comfort zones. He will make mistakes, maybe big ones. But the American public is itching to see its elected leaders make bold moves. Democrats especially are in no mood for timidity. In this political moment, playing it safe may be the biggest risk of all.
