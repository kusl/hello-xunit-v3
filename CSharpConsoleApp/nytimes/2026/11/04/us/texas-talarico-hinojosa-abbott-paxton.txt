ARTICLE

‘An Earthquake’: A Democratic Rout Stuns Texas
In a seismic shift that was once unthinkable, victories by James Talarico and Gina Hinojosa upended decades of statewide dominance in what had been the nation’s most populous conservative state.
By J. David Goodman and Lauren McGaughy
Published 2026-11-04 05:27 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/04/us/texas-talarico-hinojosa-abbott-paxton.html

Texas may never be the same.

Democrats shattered the 32-year-long Republican grip on statewide offices in what had been, until Tuesday, the nation’s most populous conservative state.

James Talarico’s victory over Ken Paxton, a scandal-scarred Republican candidate, in the U.S. Senate race was only the beginning of a series of seismic victories for Democrats on Tuesday in Texas.

Gina Hinojosa made history with her defeat of Gov. Greg Abbott, the third-term governor and political powerhouse who, just a few months ago, appeared to have a lock on winning a record fourth term. She becomes Texas's first Hispanic governor.

In one night, the state was transformed.

“Talarico is a symbol. Gina is an earthquake,” said Garry Mauro, a former Texas land commissioner who was among the last Democrats to win a statewide race, in 1994. “It’s a tsunami. The world has changed. The political maps will never be the same again.”

The decades of total Republican Party domination are over. Its hegemony had resulted in a succession of increasingly conservative leaders, from George W. Bush to Rick Perry to Greg Abbott, and increasingly conservative lawmaking, including abortion bans, Christian teaching material in public schools and expanded gun rights.

Democrats in Texas rose as national blowback against the Trump administration mounted among voters troubled by the war in Iran, an intense immigration crackdown and cost-of-living concerns. But Mr. Paxton owed much of his success to his closeness to Mr. Trump and Mr. Abbott tied himself closely to Mr. Trump’s policies.

Republican leaders in the state also embraced a data center boom that was unpopular in many rural and suburban areas and pushed through a school voucher program that many saw as undercutting public schools. In 2026, some party members made the state’s swelling South Asian community a boogeyman, warning of sharia law. A candidate for a top regulatory job attacked his opponents with slurs for gay and disabled people.

“I’ve watched Republicans fumble this ball from bottom to top,” said Austin Vaughn, 31, of Fort Bend County, who said he “literally checked every blue box for the first time in my life.”

He said he used to feel at home in the Republican Party in Texas, and even worked for a state legislator. But in recent years, the party “got really out of control socially.”

He now has a daughter and did not like the state’s stringent abortion ban. And, he said, while the party used to have a good message boosting and attracting businesses, they long ago lost that plot.

“No one can afford anything anymore,” he said. “Our water and air is the dirtiest and our education is horrible.”

Also swept away Tuesday was the assumption that Texas’s political future would resemble its past. In its place, something far less certain, more contested and decidedly Democratic emerged on Tuesday.

It was, for Democrats, so unexpected and so sudden, that many were still processing the implications.

“I’m jubilant,” Rafael Anchía, a Democratic state representative from the Dallas area, said as he watched the returns at Mr. Talarico’s election night party in Austin. “This is exceeding my wildest expectations.”

Mr. Anchía said that Democrats had racked up big gains all over the state, even in formerly conservative suburbs, even in rural areas where they had not competed for years: “It’s a nuke.”

The magnitude of the Democratic wave was amplified by the remarkable spectacle of Elon Musk, the world’s richest man, spending hundreds of millions of dollars in a doomed effort to preserve the state’s Republican leadership. After moving parts of his business empire to Texas, Mr. Musk poured at least $212 million into supporting Mr. Paxton, which was part of at least $224 million devoted to races in the state.

Republicans were left rattled and trying to explain not only Mr. Paxton’s spectacular loss, but how Mr. Abbott, seen as such a formidable opponent, could have been washed away as well in the national Democratic wave.

“Our party is going to have a reckoning unlike any in the history of our state,” said Matt Mackowiak, a Republican strategist who worked on Senator John Cornyn’s doomed primary campaign earlier this year against Mr. Paxton, who had been Texas’s attorney general.

As the results crystallized, Democrats believed their victory would draw more campaign money into Texas for future elections, along with greater national support. The party could seize more offices and perhaps have a shot at flipping Texas blue in a presidential election.

“You never give your enemy a beachhead; once they get it, they advance,” said Chad Wilbanks, a Republican political consultant and the former executive director of the Republican Party of Texas. “The dam is broken.”

Democratic officials said the election could also reshape state policy, ending Texas’s role as a laboratory for conservative laws nationwide. Mr. Talarico, as a U.S. senator, will not directly affect day-to-day governance in Texas. But Ms. Hinojosa will have the power to veto legislation and to appoint agency leaders.

“Texas is ready for change,” said Kendall Scudder, the chairman of the Texas Democratic Party. He said Democrats were “excited and ready to deliver on the policies that will make life more affordable” and to “root out the corruption that has plagued our politics for too long.”

President Trump crisscrossed the state, hoping he could save Mr. Paxton’s campaign in its final weeks. But his efforts at bringing Texas Republicans together against a shared Democratic enemy masked deep underlying divisions within the party.

The state’s Bush-era conservatives long opposed Mr. Paxton and his Trump-aligned wing of the party, and instead supported Mr. Cornyn in the primary. They argued that the Republican Party had drifted too far from its principles.

The two main factions of Texas Republicans have been at odds for years. Mr. Paxton often found himself at the fault line of those conflicts. His 2023 impeachment on allegations of corruption by a majority in the Texas House became a referendum on the direction of the party. His defense team argued that a faction of old-school conservatives — aligned with the Bush family and longtime Republican strategist Karl Rove — had been behind the impeachment. Republicans in the Texas Senate, overseen by Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick, an ally of Mr. Paxton, acquitted him.

Even before the vote on Tuesday, Republicans in Mr. Paxton’s camp were seething at those they saw as having supported him with insufficient vigor.

Steve Toth, a Republican candidate for the U.S. House and a Paxton ally, said those close to Mr. Cornyn and the Bush family were to blame for “dirtying” Mr. Paxton with the failed impeachment and during the Republican primary campaign.

“They never got on board,” Mr. Toth said on Monday at one of Mr. Paxton’s last campaign events, in Conroe, a city outside of Houston.

Those in Mr. Cornyn’s camp remained frustrated that Mr. Paxton had challenged him at all. Mr. Cornyn had shown little vulnerability among general election voters, even as Republican activists accused him of being insufficiently supportive of President Trump. Mr. Paxton, the hard right’s choice, easily defeated him in a primary runoff despite being vastly outspent.

Before Tuesday, the closest that Republicans in Texas had come to a statewide loss in recent years was in 2018, when Beto O’Rourke, a U.S. representative from El Paso, came within three percentage points of defeating Senator Ted Cruz. In response, Republicans in the state appeared to moderate their agenda in the next legislative session, focusing less on cultural issues and more on property tax and school financing reforms.

Now, any change in direction will involve the decisions not only of Republicans. It will involve Democrats as well.

“It’s a whole new day for Texas,” said Mr. Mauro, the former land commissioner. “After I lost in ’98, I was thinking we were one cycle away from winning to the next election. It’s been a long wait.”
