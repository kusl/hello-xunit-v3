ARTICLE

How a Blue Wave Became a Red Wipeout
Republicans lost control of the House and Senate as voters offered a stinging rebuke to President Trump’s agenda.
By Lisa Lerer
Published 2026-11-04 08:48 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/04/us/politics/democratic-midterms-wipeout.html

In the end, it wasn’t just a wave. It was a wipeout.

A Democrat won a Senate seat in Kansas for the first time since the Herbert Hoover administration. The party picked up Senate seats in Texas, North Carolina and South Carolina. Republicans even fell short in House districts in the South where they had tried to give themselves an edge by drawing new, gerrymandered maps.

The sweeping Democratic victories on Tuesday night offered a decisive rebuke to President Trump and his aggressive, often chaotic second-term agenda.

The party seized control of both houses of Congress. Voters rejected Republicans in conservative states that supported Mr. Trump by double-digit margins just two years ago, in places that had not seen competitive elections for major seats in decades.

Even some Democrats were stunned by the extent of their wins, which included defeating Greg Abbott, the three-term Republican governor of Texas; and Susan Collins, the five-term Republican senator from Maine.

“It’s a blood bath,” said Dan Sena, who was the executive director of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee during the last Democratic wave, in 2018. “Democrats are going to be in power in places they haven’t been in power in decades.”

And so the era of one-party rule in Washington ended with a crash, as voters pushed back on high gas prices, an unpopular war in Iran and stubbornly high inflation. As Wednesday dawned, there were signs that the country was entering a new and uncertain political time, in which both parties would be forced to consider how to navigate a post-Trump political era.

While Democrats have not solved all the problems that cost them the 2024 election, their wins were a powerful sign of renewal as a new generation of rising stars captured the country’s attention.

For years, Democrats seemed to compete on a smaller and smaller map, concentrating on fewer states, blue cities and diversifying suburbs and trying to cut their losses in rural America. But Tuesday’s results showed that the last two years had turned off voters even in deep red terrain. For Republicans, the losses were a generational defeat, giving Democrats a foothold from which to build a robust political operation in places that had been long out of reach.

In Seward County, Kan., a majority-Hispanic area, the Democratic Senate candidate, Adam Hamilton, won by double digits. Just two years ago, Mr. Trump won there by 40 percentage points.

In Iowa, a largely agricultural state, Democrats won the governor’s mansion and a U.S. Senate seat and flipped three of the state’s four congressional seats.

And in South Texas, Democrats won three heavily Latino border districts that Republicans had redrawn to be more favorable to their party.

In many states, Democrats ran candidates who offered a decidedly different brand of politics from the “resistance” ethos that characterized their response to Mr. Trump’s first term.

Instead, they focused their message on the deep economic anxiety that has roiled the country, arguing that the president and his Republican allies were enriching themselves while ordinary Americans struggled. Survey after survey showed a majority of voters, and a notable share of Republicans, offering low marks to his signature policies: tariffs, immigration enforcement and the Iran war.

“When Trump first came into office, there was this churn that was constantly going on,” said Gov. Mikie Sherrill of New Jersey, whose victory last year was an early indicator of Democratic wins to come. “These candidates have not gotten sucked into the churn.”

Many cast their campaigns as above partisan politics, trying to distance themselves from the party’s beleaguered national brand to woo voters in red states.

“This win is not about the Democratic Party or the Republican Party,” James Talarico, the Democratic Senate candidate in Texas, told cheering supporters at his victory party. “It is much bigger than that. There is something broken in America.” He added, “People across the political spectrum are hungry for a different kind of politics.”

The result nationally was a big tent that encompassed Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, a liberal firebrand in Michigan, and Mr. Hamilton, a Kansas pastor and former Republican who fueled his rise with appeals to conservative voters.

The breadth of the victory indicated that the party’s ideological future remained unsettled, setting the stage for the 2028 primary campaign. That race is expected to be crowded with politicians eager to ride what they see as a groundswell of support into the White House.

A big tent can leave more room for disputes underneath it. In many respects, the midterms showed that the party was still divided: between progressives and moderates, over Israel, over the best path forward.

But the results leave open a crucial question: Did the Democrats really win? Or did Republicans just lose? The answer will become clear in the coming months, as Democrat try to retain their gains and work to win the presidency.

“This is just the beginning of what is going to be a decades-long rebuilding process. The worst thing Democrats could do is think all our problems are solved,” said Lis Smith, a strategist who started a Democratic political group to promote insurgent candidates. “The problem with a good election night is it can conceal a lot of weaknesses.”

Yet it was clear Democrats had repaired some of the weaknesses that cost their party the White House just two years ago. In the final national New York Times/Siena poll before the election, Democrats held an eight-point advantage on the economy and a 15-point edge on the cost of living. Those ratings mark a significant reversal from 2024, when Mr. Trump was favored on the economy.

Republicans framed the election not as a referendum on Mr. Trump but as a choice between their “common sense” policies and a Democratic Party controlled by a socialists, communists and an un-American left flank. They attempted to cast Democrats as out of touch with the values of red states, casting them as sexual predators, supporters of Shariah law and even vegans.

Again and again, Republicans tried to shift the structural dynamics of the election in their favor. They spent an extraordinary $12 billion, enlisting the help of Elon Musk, the world’s richest person, to help fund their attacks.

In an unprecedented mid-decade redistricting, they redrew congressional lines across conservative states, tilting districts in their favor.

Those efforts fell short against the realities of American pocketbooks and a clear belief that Mr. Trump’s policies were responsible for the higher prices of housing, groceries and gas.

Concern about the cost of living cut across nearly all sectors of American life. One sign — more omnipresent than any campaign billboard — provided an inescapable reminder: the price of fuel advertised at gas pumps across the nation.

“We don’t feel enough is being done to make everyday necessities affordable,” said Sateria Peterson, 42, a business owner in Columbia, S.C. “We’re stretching every dollar just to cover the basics, with less room for emergencies or savings.”

Jalyn Washington, 21, of Dayton, Ohio, said she took out her economic frustrations on the Republicans. “Whoever Trump wanted, I voted the opposite,” she said.

In addition to traditional kitchen-table concerns about gas prices and cost of living, voters expressed a growing anxiety about issues that were entirely new: data centers and artificial intelligence.

Across the country, vulnerable Republican candidates distanced themselves from the president’s full-throated support for data centers. It wasn’t the only issue where the party’s candidates, even in traditionally red states, sought to separate themselves from Mr. Trump.

On the Iran war, tariffs and immigration enforcement, they offered disagreement from Mr. Trump’s approach.

For many, the dissent came too late.

Already some Republicans are looking toward a future in which Mr. Trump will play a less prominent role.

“I do want to fight for the soul of our party — I feel like we’re going down a bad path,” said Representative Don Bacon, a retiring lawmaker from Nebraska who plans to deliver a speech this month laying out a direction for his party.

His seat in Congress, however, will be filled by a Democrat.
