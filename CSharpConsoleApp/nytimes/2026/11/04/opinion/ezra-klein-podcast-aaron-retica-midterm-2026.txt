Ezra Klein and Aaron Retica react to the Democratic Party’s major midterm victory.

The big tent is real, and the political map is fake.

That is the lesson of the Democrats’ historic midterm, the blue tsunami that we saw on Tuesday.

Democrats won in places nobody thought they even had any chance to compete a year ago: Kansas, Texas, Iowa, South Carolina.

As I record this, it’s unclear which party is going to win Mississippi. Let me say that again: It is unclear which party is going to win a Senate seat in Mississippi. That was unimaginable three months ago.

Democrats won governorships in Ohio, Texas and Iowa. This was a win that destroyed the reality of the red-blue map people had come to believe in so firmly. And so we’re entering a new era, an era when much is unsettled.

The realignment may not be what we thought it was in 2024. The realignment may be a map that is entirely up for grabs from here on out.

Democrats didn’t do this on their own, of course. To a very large extent, President Donald Trump did it for them. And what Trump and the Democrats both did wrong was the same thing: not listening.

Trump did not listen — not to the voters, not even, at times, to himself. He was elected promising to lower costs, to end wars, to bring order to the border. Instead, he raised costs through tariffs and through new wars, and he created disorder after closing the border and sending in masked ICE agents to harass and upend settled communities.

Trump at no point pivoted. He dismissed almost everything that people were angry about, and his party paid the price.

At the same time, I think the Democrats’ central political failing for years has been an unwillingness to listen. They listened to themselves — not to the voters they needed to win over.

This time around, they course corrected. In state after state, district after district, they found people who fit: Josh Turek in Iowa. Adam Hamilton in Kansas. James Talarico in Texas. They found seminarians. They found Paralympians. They found a whole new class of candidates who could put into play a whole new set of districts.

Retica: You know what it makes me think of?

Klein: What?

Retica: It makes me think of the first line of the Percy Bysshe Shelley poem, “England in 1819.” Let’s see if I can do it right. It’s “An old, mad, blind, despised and dying king.” That’s the beginning.

“Princes, the dregs of their dull race.” I could go on. But that poem actually speaks to what we’re talking about.

It goes through how horrible the Senate is, but then, at the end of it, he asks: What’s the glorious phantom that’s going to come from this? Which is what we’re talking about.

And also just Trump’s personality. I don’t know what to call it because I’m not a doctor, and even if I were, I wouldn’t say it. But there’s some kind of disintegration going on, or a man not in supergreat contact with reality to begin with.

Klein: You don’t feel like the reflecting pool speech was the closing argument Republicans needed?

Retica: If you look at the world in a historical and literary frame, as I tend to, the anxiety is — and you just outlined it well — that this is 1912. This is 1913. This is 1914.

Klein: This is ’06.

Retica: And these things are all converging on us, and then the president is — let’s just say he is not adaptable.

As you pointed out earlier, he doesn’t react to events. He doesn’t even seem to know they happened in the way that other people experience reality. And so that is unlikely to go superwell.

Klein: Yeah, and his staffing is not going to get better in the lame-duck period of his second term.

Some of these people might get shuffled out as some of them have, like the former Department of Homeland Security secretary Kristi Noem.

But the people who come in are not — Trump is not going to suddenly surround himself with a team of rivals. He’s not going to suddenly begin listening to people’s advice. So what you tend to have, particularly in a second term, particularly in the back half of it, is an exhausted staff less able to challenge the boss.

As you say, the boss is more erratic, more mad. He is not obsessed with Republicans winning in 2028. He does not want to lose control. And so the more Republicans try to win in 2028 ——

Retica: Now, in your theory, he would like them to lose.

Klein: I think he would prefer them to lose while embracing him than to win while running away from him. I don’t think there’s any doubt about that.

Retica: To get to your point about investigation and corruption, there will be people who say: Let it all go. Don’t impeach him again. Don’t do this. Don’t do that. We have to govern.

But here’s the problem with that. First of all, there’s the level of the corruption and incompetence and just grotesque, grotesque behavior — just staggering, amazing stuff that they did.

They destroyed huge agencies, the U.S.A.I.D. and Department of Education and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — all of that. There’s the immigration. There are all these things.

So what do you think they should do on the investigation and corruption front?

Klein: I don’t think anybody in the party is saying: Don’t investigate.

Retica: Don’t impeach — they are saying that.

Klein: Let’s hold impeachment aside, because they’re not going to impeach right now. That’s not their first-day plan.

What they’ve been planning for is investigation. And the challenge they’re going to face on investigation is actually how target-rich the environment is.

The resources of Congress are significant, but the amount of corruption, malfeasance, lawbreaking, etc., in the Trump administration is so vast that in order to make anything stick, you need to focus on a couple of things people can begin to hold in their minds.

It’s one reason Jon Ossoff has been better at making the corruption argument than a lot of others. He tends to focus on one small thing at a time. It allows you to conceptualize the whole and the part.

The way I hope they will do it, as an organizing thread, at least at the beginning — not because it will be the most emotionally satisfying or even important, but because I think it actually reflects what the American people are interested in — is I want them to follow the money.

I want to know who bought what from the Trump administration, who gave what to the Trump administration. Things like the Qatari jet. How much could we not know?

Retica: Which foreign countries?

Klein: How much did the Trump family get? What contracts went into the Trump family’s hands during all this?

What’s going on with Jared Kushner’s investment firm? I would love to know real details on that Jared Kushner firm that has seemed to be so popular among Gulf state funders.

Retica: [Laughs.] It’s surprisingly popular, yes.

Klein: And on the flip, not just who bought what, but what did they get?

And then where has the money been going for the Trump family and for Trump associates? As you’ve been getting hosed at the pump and in the grocery store and when you’re trying to buy a home — how many billions of dollars has he made exactly? And not just him — Don Jr., Eric, all their friends and family, Steve Witkoff’s son.

What’s going on with all the crypto? There is so much money that has been moving around. To me, that is the place to start.

But as you were suggesting, too, there are all these other questions about what laws were broken in different agencies. And I’m not saying they can’t do that, too, but they’re going to have to choose what to focus on first ——

Retica: Well, and which things to try to put back. Do you just try to reopen the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau? What do you do there?

Klein: It’s maybe worth spending a moment on what happened to the DOGE leaders, Elon Musk and Vivek Ramaswamy, who I think together — Vivek lost the Ohio governorship to Amy Acton, running one of the most grim and disreputable campaigns in history ——

Retica: Oh, I thought you were going to say the ad, because that was the worst thing ever.

Klein: For people who didn’t follow this, Ramaswamy had an ad on from her estranged mother, who said that Amy Acton is a liar. And what she was suggesting Amy Acton lied about was being sexually abused by her stepfather, of which there was quite good evidence.

Grotesque, just reprehensible behavior. Ramaswamy was already on track to lose that election, but he’s lost it and really ——

Retica: Yeah, he should be drummed out of public life, no question.

Klein: And then Elon Musk — largest single donor in history in this cycle.

Retica: It’s only $500 million.

Klein: I mean, for him, it’s like nothing.

Retica: What are you talking about? [Laughs.]

Klein: But it’s not just that money didn’t do anything, or it doesn’t appear to have done very much. A huge amount of it was spent in Texas, and there is nowhere in the country that Republicans collapsed like they did in Texas.

Retica: And if you do this for a living, the dream of a blue Texas has — or really what appeared to be, at first, the fantasy of a blue Texas — has been alive and well for decades. And now it’s here.

Klein: Alive and unwell for decades. [Laughs.]

Retica: The fantasy was alive, but ——

Klein: Yeah, the fantasy was alive.

Retica: But here it is.

Klein: But hopes kept getting dashed.

But Musk spent a tremendous amount of money — a large amount of that money, certainly a plurality of it, went into Texas, specifically.

It bought him nothing. I think it ——

Retica: No, it hurt.

Klein: It hurt, right? People don’t like their elections being bought.

Retica: There were two forms of one-man rule on the ballot: Trump’s and his, and they both got crushed.

Klein: And Musk has moved himself and a lot of his corporations to Texas. So the question of Musk is going to be a very interesting one going forward.

Retica: Yeah, I mean, there’s a lot to say about Musk.

I hope this means that when you verge, or maybe more than verge, on white supremacy or certainly white dominance as your central idea, that you’ll fail. I hope that’s true.

Klein: And replacement of human beings with robots might also not be hugely popular.

Retica: And replacement of human beings with robots. [Laughs.] Also not great, right? A.I. centers are a huge electoral issue that elites are maybe not focused on as much as they should be.

Klein: I mean, I think they were pretty focused on it in this election, though.

Retica: They were, right.

Klein: But they — I mean, the amount that Democrats ran on that. Sherrod Brown, running against Jon Husted, in Ohio, ran heavily on data centers. Gina Hinojosa, in Texas, ran aggressively on data centers. Abdul El-Sayed ran on data centers.

So there were dozens of data center moratoriums on the ballot, and I think, so far, every single one of them except for one has passed.

Retica: The exception that proves the rule.

So a little inside baseball: You came to The New York Times the week of Jan. 6, 2021. You and I had already met, obviously.

Klein: Yeah. My first week at The Times.

Retica: Right, your first week at The Times was the week of Jan. 6.

That was effectively the down payment on the second Trump term. It also made this even more the Trump era than it might have been.

Is this finally the beginning of the end of Trumpism? It’s definitely the beginning of the end of Trump — he’s out in two years no matter what. But is it ——

Klein: There’s no Trumpism. There was only ever Trump.

The work that various right-wing intellectuals and pseudo-intellectuals tried to do, to create this coherence around Trump’s impulses and extract from that what he was going to try to do with the tariffs — what a highly structured tariff regime, to bring manufacturing back to America, would look like. That was a failure. Donald Trump’s tariffs were insane.

Trump is an end of the neocon Middle East meddling era in foreign policy — a failure. Trump did not agree with you. That was not Trumpism.

Over and over and over and over again, there have been these efforts to say what Trumpism is, but Trump himself would not hold to any of them.

And so one reason all of this collapsed, and one reason I think the infighting of it on the right is ultimately going to be so vicious, is because you’re still going to have the people who say: Real Trumpism has never been tried. You already saw this happening ——

Retica: It was happening last night. The MAGA people were all like ——

Klein: The neocons did this to us ——

Retica: The neocons brought Iran ——

Klein: Who are the neocons? It was Trump.

Retica: Yeah.

Klein: Marco Rubio didn’t get you into Iran — it was Donald Trump himself, with Benjamin Netanyahu, who got us into Iran. That wasn’t Bill Kristol. Are you kidding me? It’s ridiculous.

JD Vance is going to presumably run for president as a human sacrifice to say: This is what Trumpism could have been.

Retica: Trumpism, right — without the war ——

Klein: There was this whole view that JD Vance could have been the intellectual nerve center of the Trump administration. He wasn’t in the end, but maybe he’ll run as that. The problem is nobody actually thinks Vanceism, which is what that really is, is appealing.

JD Vance was not an electoral juggernaut in Ohio when he ran. He underperformed terribly, and he’s not popular now.

And so the movement is going to be to try to figure out what can come after. And that jockeying, particularly outside of the House and the Senate, is already going to begin.

And the key thing is: Trump cares about his control. He doesn’t care about his ideology. Trump wants fealty. He doesn’t want consistency or coherence. He’s not an ideas guy. He wants to keep his family out of jail ——

Retica: Amazing statement, yes.

Klein: Keep their profits, right? But everybody knows it to be true. The Republican Party all knows it to be true.

Retica: You don’t think he’s home reading Patrick Deneen?

Klein: [Laughs.] I don’t think he’s home reading Patrick Deneen.

Retica: Yeah. OK. Before we go, I want to ask you something.

So I was on my way here, and I was texting with a friend of mine who’s — well, she’s a historian. And I was telling her what I was doing, and she was like: OK, but don’t — as my mother always used to say: Don’t gloat.

And I don’t want to gloat. But I do want to focus on something: people whom we are very glad to see the end of — who lost last night. So I’m going to go first, and that is Susan Collins.

She has the mask of the moderate, right? When things were close, she was the most likely senator to vote against Republicans. But that’s still 15, 16, 17 percent. Most of the time, she voted with them.

And she was the chief enabler of the destruction of women’s right to choose because of the change in the court. And I’m very, very happy to see her go. [Laughs.]

Who are you happy to see go?

Klein: I think that the place where I am happiest to see politicians and political tactics fail is when they front-load unabashed cruelty. And I think that describes Ken Paxton in Texas.

Retica: Definitely.

Klein: And immorality. I think there’s a world where he ran a much more honorable campaign than this, but it’s not the world he ended up living in.

Vivek Ramaswamy, in Ohio. I think Roger Marshall, in Kansas, in the way that he — the incredibly cruel interest he imposed upon his own patients, the 18 percent interest rates he had.

Retica: [Laughs.] That’s just amazing. He’s an OB-GYN.

Klein: And the flip of that — because I think more about who I’m happy who won than who I’m happy lost.

The flip of that is that I am really, really interested and optimistic about this rise of a religious left, this revival of a religious left and a moralistic left. A left that is interested and able to speak in a language of virtue: James Talarico, Adam Hamilton, Abdul El-Sayed and Jon Ossoff. Ossoff is really good at speaking in a biblical cadence thick with the language of virtue.

I think one of the giant opportunities in politics right now is to rebuild a politics that is explicitly moral within the moral wreckage of Donald Trump.

Retica: Yeah, it’s a thermostatic response to their depravity and immorality.

Klein: And so I am quite interested in the candidates who are figuring out how to do that. I particularly think the Talarico, Hamilton, El-Sayed, Ossoff group there ——

Retica: Yeah, you always say ——

Klein: Has that moral language in a way that relatively few of the others do.

Retica: Let’s give it a name. You always like to give things a name, right? We’ll call it the Love Thy Neighbor Coalition. And we’ll call it a day.

Retica: Thanks a lot, Ezra.
