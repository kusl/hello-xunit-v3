ARTICLE

Xbox’s Millennial C.E.O. Isn’t Playing Around
“I think the industry will require disruption,” said Asha Sharma, who has overseen layoffs and divested studios since inheriting Microsoft’s flailing video game division.
By Zachary Small
Published 2026-09-30 09:00 UTC
https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/30/arts/xbox-microsoft-asha-sharma.html

Asha Sharma, the 38-year-old chief executive of Microsoft’s Xbox division, approaches questions with the camera-ready positivity of Zohran Mamdani and the corporate pragmatism of Sam Altman. Like those other millennial leaders, she answers swiftly, with proverbs on demand.

“Great things happen over time, and I think most people quit too early,” said Sharma, who became the face of Xbox in February despite having no prior experience in video games. Her appointment came amid a crisis in the industry, where production costs are soaring.

The challenges are particularly acute at Microsoft, which has sold far fewer consoles this generation than Nintendo and Sony. Flagship franchises like Halo and Gears of War have waning cultural relevance. An acquisitions-heavy strategy that brought in Minecraft and Call of Duty has produced meager profits.

“Our business today is not healthy,” Sharma declared in a July letter to Xbox employees.

Sharma, formerly the president of product within Microsoft’s CoreAI division and a chief operating officer at Instacart, made her first 100 days at Xbox a high-stakes campaign to win over hearts and minds.

The most expensive tier of Game Pass, a subscription service with hundreds of new and old games? Discounted. Console mascots on competing platforms? Throttled. The clumsy integration of artificial intelligence? Nixed.

Sharma gave her personal cellphone number to journalists and influencers. Her new chief strategy officer tutored reporters on the company’s economic challenges. She skillfully dodged questions about her predecessor’s strategic blunders.

The gambit appeared to work.

Misogynistic and racist vitriol that had greeted Sharma, who was raised by Indian parents who immigrated from New Zealand to Wisconsin, dissipated a few weeks into her tenure. Influencers posted about her “impossible Xbox problem,” describing the $23 billion division like an underdog.

The consensus among many gamers even held through a difficult summer when 20 percent of employees lost their jobs with Xbox and the company divested five of its development studios. Three days after the layoffs, it was announced that she had been appointed to the U.S. Federal Reserve’s Productivity and Jobs task force.

By September, Sharma had returned to the company’s headquarters in Redmond, Wash., following a nationwide tour of Xbox studios. In Maryland, union members had planted hundreds of red flags, one for every lost job. After everything she had seen, how much longer could the charm offensive last?

“In life there is only winning and learning,” Sharma said in her office, manipulating a NeeDoh stress ball and producing one of her Confucian managerial maxims. “I’m learning every single day.”

‘Xbox Is Not for Sale’

Microsoft, Sony and Nintendo are facing a cascade of economic problems. Video game consoles like the Xbox Series X|S are becoming more expensive to produce as A.I. companies, including Microsoft, drive up demand for memory and processing chips. Audiences are choosing free-to-play games on their phones, and even best-sellers struggle to meet ballooning development costs.

Industry leaders are calling it the worst crash since the 1980s, with one survey finding that a third of employees had lost their jobs since 2024.

Xbox has also faded in relevance at Microsoft over the past decade, accounting for about 6 percent of total profits at a company focused on data infrastructure and artificial intelligence.

“Xbox has always been an aspirational part of Microsoft’s portfolio,” said Jesper Juul, a professor at the Royal Danish Academy researching video games. “Now they are facing an existential question.”

Does Microsoft still need Xbox? Could Xbox survive without Microsoft?

“Despite the reset that we are going through, we still have 500 million players every single month,” Sharma said in New York this summer. She arrived for lunch at the Modern in a suede jacket despite the sweltering heat and wildfire smoke outside. Alongside her was Craig Cincotta, a corporate attaché in a fedora.

Cincotta now serves as her chief of staff, but the roles were reversed when a 22-year-old Sharma joined Microsoft’s marketing department. She worked in 2013 for Cincotta, then a communications director; he recalled an “incredibly curious” Sharma who approached tasks with “tremendous positivity.”

Now an early riser with three children and intense hobbies like boxing and racecar driving, Sharma has a singular focus on success. “All I can do is look at it from a first principles perspective,” she said. “And I think the industry will require disruption.”

When discussing her experience at Instacart, Sharma talked about walking through dozens of grocery stores to understand how they worked. She demurred when asked to pick a favorite. “I love all of them,” she said, as if she might be headed back into the frozen foods aisle. (“Publix is up there,” she later said.)

But she did not stall when asked if her endgame at Xbox was divestiture.

“Xbox is not for sale,” Sharma said. “We will do whatever it takes to set the company up for success, and we will look at the right partnerships, the right operating model and everything needed to achieve that.”

In boxing, they call that a bob and weave.

The chief executive of Microsoft, Satya Nadella, declined an interview request but said on a recent podcast that he expected Sharma to get Xbox back to growth in the next fiscal year. He has also complained that YouTube streamers earned more off Xbox’s games than Microsoft did.

Many analysts are predicting an eventual spinoff. An independent Xbox could form a strategic partnership with Microsoft to maintain its technological edge without dragging down the company’s balance sheet.

Microsoft executives noted that there is always speculation about a possible Xbox sale, and that it had never happened. “We’ve got a long way to go with Microsoft,” Sharma said, “and we’re going to take the long-term view.”

An Uphill Battle

In 2000, Xbox’s founders faced Bill Gates, Microsoft’s chairman, and Steve Ballmer, its chief executive, in a pivotal meeting known internally as “The Valentine’s Day Massacre.” The founders presented a PowerPoint showing that the venture would lose $1.6 billion. Gates and Ballmer yelled for hours before finally approving the concept.

Two former executives with knowledge of the meeting said a dual promise won them over: Xbox would extend Microsoft’s technological dominance from the office to the living room, while creating a wedge against Japanese competitors like Sony, which was making nearly a quarter of its revenue from gaming.

“Xbox was Microsoft’s spearhead into the home,” Juul said, explaining why subsequent console iterations pushed entertainment features like live television and web browsing despite lukewarm consumer interest.

Phil Spencer, Xbox’s chief executive before Sharma, modeled his strategy on Microsoft’s successful Office 365 and OneDrive subscription rollouts, aiming to grow Game Pass to 100 million users. “Subscriptions were all the rage back then,” Rhys Elliott, a market analyst, said.

Nadella, Microsoft’s chief executive, bankrolled a large acquisition spree to supply the service. The push began in 2014 with a $2.5 billion deal for the Minecraft studio Mojang, culminating nearly a decade later in the $75.4 billion purchase of Activision Blizzard, publisher of Call of Duty, World of Warcraft and Candy Crush.

The Activision deal might have cost Xbox its technological edge.

Fearing that Microsoft would monopolize cloud gaming, British regulators scrutinized the acquisition, saying it risked undermining innovation. To appease regulators and balance debt from the purchase, Xbox shelved designs for controllers devoted to cloud gaming, three former employees said. The experience that remains has been glitchy and disappointed players. (An Xbox spokeswoman denied that the controllers were shelved because of the regulatory process.)

Game Pass has also fallen short of expectations, reaching 34 million subscribers before membership began declining. Out of concern it was cannibalizing software sales, Microsoft is no longer immediately adding Call of Duty games to the service.

These are some of the challenges Sharma must confront from her Redmond office, where nearly a dozen Xbox controllers rest on pedestals like tiny sculptures. During an interview, her television lights up as gamers send friend requests to her Xbox Live account.

“Clarity is kindness,” Sharma said, using one of her favorite catchphrases. “I have to create clarity so we can confront the challenges and go solve them.”

Return of the Console Wars

Sharma wants Xbox properties to entertain a billion people, which means opening untapped markets in Africa, Latin America and South Asia by using cloud computing technology to run games on computers and smartphones.

Spencer marketed the same idea under the unpopular slogan “This is an Xbox.” Sharma flipped the perspective by saying she would provide “the ability to play anywhere.” Another pillar of her strategy is reinvesting in Minecraft as a competitor to Roblox, which has captivated a younger generation of gamers.

“We have always said play is a basic human need, but I think creation is a basic human need,” said Jason Ronald, who is leading Xbox’s efforts to shape its next generation. “I think the lines between players and developers are going to get blurrier and blurrier.”

Leaders at the company describe the future of its consoles — code-named Project Helix — as embodying that idea. Executives declined to discuss specifics, but their descriptions make it sound like a toy box, and developers have suggested that Microsoft will leverage its research into A.I. to accomplish such a task.

In recent months, Xbox has benefited from decisions by competitors. When Sony said it would stop making physical game discs for the PlayStation in 2028, Microsoft announced a disc-to-digital entitlement program that would let players carry purchases into the future. When Sony canceled its partnership with Hideo Kojima, Xbox quickly struck a deal with one of the industry’s auteurs.

After hours of diplomatic conversation about her competitors, Sharma had a rare moment of speechlessness when discussing the deal for Kojima’s upcoming stealth game Physint. She laughed at the idea of apologizing to Sony before shrugging and saying she was happy Kojima chose to work with Xbox.

The agreement was substantially below what Xbox would typically spend to develop a premium game, according to people close to the agreement, and included film and TV rights. Sharma also clarified that work with Sony would continue: “Competition and collaboration can coexist.”

Sharma spent a weekend in Tokyo with Kojima, posing in aviator sunglasses and an Xbox/Kojima Productions sweatshirt, and is traveling almost every week through the end of the year. On her birthday this summer, she was attending an Xbox Game Camp in Minneapolis with the incoming chief executive of Best Buy.

While she is away, a large counter in the beige Xbox building on Microsoft’s campus tallies Sharma’s time as chief executive. Over the atrium where employees take their coffee breaks, a banner exclaims “RETURN OF XBOX.”

Achieving that goal will not be easy. In late September, Xbox announced that nearly 270 employees would be laid off, a move that gutted the team behind Halo, putting the future of the science-fiction series under Activision.

“I don’t think you sign up for roles like this for work-life balance,” Sharma had said weeks earlier with a little swagger. “I think you signed up for them because you believe in the mission and you do what it takes.”
